## Maths: what a letter stands for

### Opener

Let's start with an equation, 3x − 4 = 11, and ask what it actually says before we try to do anything with it. The x is a number. We haven't been told which one, but the equation tells us something definite about it: if we multiply it by 3 and take away 4, we get 11.

Almost everything in this course comes back to reading lines like this as statements about a particular number, so it is worth getting that reading right before we learn to solve anything.

### Teaching

Why write it with a letter? We could write the same thing in words, and mathematicians did for centuries; the equals sign itself only appeared in 1557, invented by Robert Recorde to spare himself from writing "is equal to" over and over. The letter does for the unknown number what Recorde's sign did for those words. It is short, it can be carried from one line of working to the next, and if a problem has two unknown numbers we can call them x and y and never mix them up.

We call a letter that stands for a number in this way a variable, since in another problem the same letter can stand for a different number, and we call a line with an equals sign between two calculations an equation. The equals sign is what gives an equation its content, because it makes a claim, namely that the left side and the right side come to the same number, and that claim is true or false depending on which number x is.

### Testing a number

So we can test any value we like by substituting it and seeing whether the claim survives. Try x = 5 in 3x − 4 = 11: the left side becomes 3 × 5 − 4, which is 15 − 4, or 11, exactly the right side, so the claim is true and we call 5 a solution of the equation. Try x = 4 and the left side comes to 8, which is not 11, so the claim is false and 4 is not a solution.

Notice that the test has no notion of being close. The equation says the two sides are equal, so a value that leaves them one apart has failed in exactly the same way as a value that leaves them a hundred apart.

### Reply to a wrong answer

*Question: Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.*

Let's check it. With x = 3, the left side is 5 × 3 − 2, which is 15 − 2, or 13, while the right side is 12. The two sides are one apart, so the claim is false for x = 3, and 3 is not a solution.

### Recap

An equation is a claim about an unknown number: that its two sides are equal. We test a value by substituting it for the letter everywhere it appears and working out both sides; if they agree exactly the value is a solution, and if they differ by any amount it is not.

## Astronomy: parallax

### Opener

How would you measure the distance to a star? You can't travel there, and you can't bounce a signal off it and wait for the echo, since even the nearest star is more than four light-years away. What we can do is look at it from two different places and see how its direction changes, and Earth's orbit carries us between two such places every year.

### Teaching

Try it with your finger first: hold it at arm's length and close each eye in turn, and the finger jumps against the wall behind it, because each eye sees it from a slightly different spot. Bring the finger closer and the jump grows. We do the same thing with a star, using Earth's orbit as the gap between our two eyes. We photograph the star against the far more distant stars behind it, wait six months until Earth is on the other side of the Sun, photograph it again, and measure how far it seems to have moved.

We call half of that apparent shift the star's **parallax angle**, and it is astonishingly small; even for Proxima Centauri, the nearest star, it is about 0.77 arcseconds [note: An arcsecond is 1/3600 of a degree. The full Moon is about 1,800 arcseconds across.], roughly the width of a coin seen from five kilometres away. Astronomers tried for well over a century to detect it before Friedrich Bessel succeeded in 1838, with the star 61 Cygni. Because a smaller angle means a farther star, we define the **parsec** to make the arithmetic easy: a star with a parallax of one arcsecond is one parsec away, and in general the distance in parsecs is just 1 divided by the parallax in arcseconds.

### Reply to a wrong answer

*Question: A star has a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds. How far away is it, in parsecs? The learner answered 0.5.*

Let's run the rule. Distance in parsecs is 1 divided by the parallax, so a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds gives 1 ÷ 0.5, which is 2 parsecs rather than 0.5. It helps to remember which way the relationship runs: *half* the angle means *twice* the distance.

### Recap

We find a star's distance by watching it shift as Earth moves around the Sun. Half that shift is the parallax angle, and the distance in parsecs is 1 divided by that angle in arcseconds, so the smaller the shift, the farther away the star.

## Philosophy: validity and soundness

### Opener

Here is an argument: all fish can fly; salmon are fish; therefore salmon can fly. Is it a good argument? Most people's first answer is no, since the conclusion is plainly false, but a logician would tell you the argument is valid, and working out why they say that is one of the most useful things you can learn about reasoning.

### Teaching

The question a logician asks first is not whether the premises are true but whether the conclusion *follows* from them. Suppose, just for a moment, that every fish could fly and that salmon were fish. Could salmon then fail to fly? They couldn't, and that is what we mean when we call an argument **valid**: it is impossible for all its premises [note: The statements an argument offers in support of its conclusion. Met in “Reconstructing arguments”.] to be true while its conclusion is false. Validity is about the shape of the reasoning, which is why we can judge it without knowing anything about fish.

Of course, we also care whether the premises are actually true, and for that we need a second word. We call an argument **sound** when it is valid and every one of its premises is true, and a sound argument is the real prize, because its conclusion is then guaranteed. So there are two separate ways an argument can go wrong. It can be invalid, where the conclusion doesn't follow even if you grant everything, or it can be valid but unsound, where the reasoning is fine but it starts from something false; the flying-fish argument is the second kind.

### Reply to a wrong answer

*Question: Is the flying-fish argument valid? The learner answered: no, because its first premise is false.*

You've found the real fault, but it has a different name. A false premise makes the argument *unsound*; it would only be invalid if the conclusion failed to follow even with the premises granted. Grant that all fish fly and salmon are fish, and salmon must fly, so the argument is valid, though unsound.

### Recap

We call an argument valid when its conclusion has to be true if its premises are, and sound when it is valid and its premises really are true. When you assess an argument, ask the two questions separately: does the conclusion follow, and are the premises true?

## Psychology: correlation and cause

### Opener

Suppose a survey finds that people who spend more hours on social media report lower wellbeing. It's tempting to conclude that social media makes people unhappy, and plenty of headlines do. Before agreeing, ask what *else* could produce exactly the same numbers, because the answer to that question is the difference between reading psychological research well and being misled by it.

### Teaching

When two measurements tend to go up and down together across a group of people, we say they are **correlated**. The trouble is that a correlation fits several different stories. Perhaps social media lowers wellbeing, or perhaps it's the other way round and people who are already unhappy spend more time scrolling. Or something else entirely, loneliness say, or poor sleep, might push social media use up and wellbeing down at the same time, so that the two move together even though neither affects the other. We call a hidden factor like that a **confound**.

How do we tell these stories apart? We stop observing and start intervening. In an **experiment**, researchers change one thing themselves, for example by asking a randomly chosen group to cut their social media use for a month, and compare them with a group who carry on as usual. Because the groups were formed by random assignment [note: Placing participants in groups by chance, so that their differences even out. Met in “Experiments and assignment”.], loneliness, sleep and every other confound should be spread evenly between them, so if wellbeing differs afterwards we have good reason to credit the change in use. That is why psychologists choose their words carefully: a correlational study shows an *association*, and only an experiment earns the word *cause*.

### Reply to a wrong answer

*Question: A study finds that students who sleep more get higher grades. Does it show that more sleep raises grades? The learner answered yes.*

Think about what else could produce that pattern. Better grades might lower stress and so help students sleep, or a steady routine at home might improve both sleep and grades at once. The study shows that the two go together, but to show that sleep raises grades we'd need an experiment that changes sleep and watches what happens.

### Recap

A correlation tells us that two things vary together, but not why: the cause might run either way, or a confound might drive both. To establish that one thing causes another, we need an experiment that changes it deliberately, with people assigned to groups at random.
