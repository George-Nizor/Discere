## Maths: what a letter stands for

### Opener

Here is an equation: 3x − 4 = 11. The letter x in it stands for a number whose value has not been given, and the equation states a fact about that number: multiply it by 3, take away 4, and the result is 11.

Before learning any method for finding it, it is worth being clear about what a line like this says, because most of what follows in this course, from rearranging equations to drawing straight lines, depends on reading it as a statement about one particular number that you have not yet been told.

### Teaching

The same statement can be written in words, and for centuries mathematicians did write it that way, spelling out "a number, multiplied by 3, less 4, makes 11" in full. Even the equals sign only appeared in 1557, when Robert Recorde decided he would rather draw two parallel lines than keep writing "is equal to". A letter is shorter than the words, it can be carried from one line of working to the next, and when a problem involves two unknown numbers each can have its own letter, x and y, so that the two are never confused.

A letter used in this way is called a variable, because the same letter can stand for different numbers in different problems. A statement that joins two calculations with an equals sign is called an equation, and its content lies in that sign, which claims that the calculation on the left and the calculation on the right produce the same number. Like any claim, it is true for some values of x and false for others.

### Testing a number

That gives us a way to check any proposed value. To test whether 5 satisfies 3x − 4 = 11, replace x with 5 and work out the left-hand side: 3 × 5 − 4 is 15 − 4, which is 11, the same as the right-hand side, so the claim holds and 5 is called a solution of the equation. Trying 4 instead gives a left-hand side of 8, which is not 11, so 4 is not a solution.

There is no partial credit in this test. The equation claims that its two sides are equal, not that they are close, so a value that misses by one has failed just as surely as a value that misses by a hundred.

### Reply to a wrong answer

*Question: Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.*

With x = 3, the left-hand side is 5 × 3 − 2, which is 15 − 2, or 13, while the right-hand side is 12. The two sides differ by one, so the claim is false for this value and 3 is not a solution.

### Recap

An equation is a claim about a number you have not been told: that its two sides are equal. To test a value, substitute it for the letter wherever the letter appears and work out both sides, and the value is a solution only if they come out exactly equal.

## Astronomy: parallax

### Opener

The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 40 trillion kilometres away, and no instrument could ever stretch a tape across that distance. Astronomers measure it with geometry instead, using the fact that Earth's position changes over the course of a year, so that a nearby star is seen from slightly different directions in January and in July. That small change of direction, called parallax, is the first and most direct way of finding the distance to a star.

### Teaching

You can see the effect without a telescope. Hold a finger at arm's length and look at it with one eye and then the other, and the finger appears to jump against the background, because each eye views it from a slightly different position; the closer the finger, the larger the jump. Astronomers use the same geometry on a far larger scale, with Earth's orbit in place of the gap between your eyes. They photograph a star against much more distant background stars, photograph it again six months later, when Earth is on the other side of the Sun, and measure how far the star appears to have moved.

The shift is tiny. Half of it, the angle through which the star appears to move as Earth travels one astronomical unit [note: The average distance from Earth to the Sun, about 150 million kilometres.] from the Sun, is called the star's **parallax angle**, and even for Proxima Centauri it is only about 0.77 arcseconds [note: An arcsecond is 1/3600 of a degree. The full Moon is about 1,800 arcseconds across.], roughly the width of a coin seen from five kilometres away. Because a *smaller* angle means a *greater* distance, astronomers define a unit that keeps the arithmetic simple: a star with a parallax of exactly one arcsecond is one **parsec** away, and in general the distance in parsecs is 1 divided by the parallax in arcseconds. That puts Proxima Centauri at about 1.3 parsecs, or 4.2 light-years.

### Reply to a wrong answer

*Question: A star has a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds. How far away is it, in parsecs? The learner answered 0.5.*

Distance in parsecs is 1 divided by the parallax in arcseconds, so a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds gives 1 ÷ 0.5, which is 2 parsecs. The relationship runs inversely, so any parallax smaller than one arcsecond means a distance greater than one parsec.

### Recap

As Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star appears to shift against more distant ones, and half of that shift is its parallax angle. The distance in parsecs is 1 divided by the parallax in arcseconds, so the smaller the angle, the more distant the star.

## Philosophy: validity and soundness

### Opener

Consider this argument: all fish can fly; salmon are fish; therefore salmon can fly. Its conclusion is false and so is its first premise, yet logicians describe the argument as valid, and seeing why is the key to the rest of this module. Validity is a property of an argument's structure rather than of the truth of its parts, and keeping those two things apart is what lets you assess any argument fairly, including arguments whose conclusions you dislike.

### Teaching

An argument is **valid** when its conclusion follows from its premises [note: The statements an argument offers in support of its conclusion. Met in “Reconstructing arguments”.], meaning that it is impossible for all the premises to be true while the conclusion is false. The fish argument passes this test, because if every fish could fly and salmon were fish, salmon would *have* to be able to fly; there is no way to accept both premises and reject the conclusion. Its fault lies elsewhere, in the first premise, which is simply untrue.

That fault is captured by a second, stronger term. An argument is **sound** when it is valid *and* all of its premises are true, and a sound argument guarantees that its conclusion is true as well. The two questions are therefore asked separately: asking whether an argument is valid means asking whether its conclusion would follow if the premises were granted, while asking whether it is sound means also checking each premise against the world. An argument can fail in either way, and the fish argument fails only in the second.

### Reply to a wrong answer

*Question: Is the flying-fish argument valid? The learner answered: no, because its first premise is false.*

A false premise makes an argument unsound, but it does not make it invalid. Validity asks only whether the conclusion would have to be true if the premises were, and here it would, so the argument is valid but unsound.

### Recap

An argument is valid when its conclusion cannot be false while all its premises are true, and sound when it is valid and its premises really are true. Validity concerns structure and soundness concerns truth, so a valid argument can still reach a false conclusion if one of its premises is false.

## Psychology: correlation and cause

### Opener

Surveys regularly find that people who spend many hours a day on social media report lower wellbeing than people who use it little, and headlines often present this as evidence that social media harms mental health. The finding may well be accurate, but on its own it cannot show that, because a link between two measurements is a different kind of evidence from a demonstration that one causes the other. Telling the two apart is the most important skill in reading psychological research.

### Teaching

When two measurements tend to rise and fall together across people, they are said to be **correlated**. A correlation tells you that knowing one measurement helps you predict the other, but it leaves three explanations open. Heavy social media use might lower wellbeing; low wellbeing might lead people to spend more time on social media, which is the same link running in the opposite *direction*; or a third factor, such as loneliness or poor sleep, might raise the one and lower the other without either affecting the other directly. A factor of this last kind is called a **confounding variable**, or confound.

Researchers separate these explanations with an **experiment**, in which they change one variable themselves and measure the effect on the other. If people are assigned at random to cut their social media use for a few weeks, random assignment [note: Placing participants in groups by chance, so that their differences even out. Met in “Experiments and assignment”.] spreads loneliness, sleep habits and every other possible confound evenly across the groups, so a difference in wellbeing that appears afterwards can be attributed to the change in use. Correlational studies remain valuable, because many questions cannot ethically or practically be tested by experiment, but their conclusions have to be stated as associations rather than causes.

### Reply to a wrong answer

*Question: A study finds that students who sleep more get higher grades. Does it show that more sleep raises grades? The learner answered yes.*

It shows that sleep and grades are associated, but not which causes which. Better grades might reduce stress and so improve sleep, or a third factor, such as a steady routine at home, might improve both, and only an experiment that changes sleep directly could separate these explanations.

### Recap

A correlation shows that two measurements vary together, but it cannot by itself show that one causes the other, since the effect might run the other way or both might depend on a confound. An experiment with random assignment is the standard way to establish cause.
