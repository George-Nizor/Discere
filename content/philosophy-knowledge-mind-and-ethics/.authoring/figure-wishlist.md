# Figures wanted

Figures the first three lessons could use that the course's `philosophy_explorer` does not have. Each screen below teaches without its figure, using the existing argument map.

## reconstructing-arguments, step `one-step-leads-to-the-next`

- **Shows:** a chained argument map in one diagram. Two premises feed an intermediate conclusion, which then feeds the next premise alongside a third, ending in the final conclusion. The intermediate conclusion is drawn as both an output and an input.
- **Learner can change:** select any claim to see its role (premise, intermediate conclusion or final conclusion), and cut a claim to see which later claims lose their support.
- **Caption:** "Maya's two steps as one chain. Select a claim to see what it supports, then remove one and watch what falls with it."
- **Why:** the screen now shows the two steps as two separate maps and asks the learner to hold the join in mind.

## reconstructing-arguments, step `standard-form`

- **Shows:** a spoken passage with each clause highlighted, and an empty standard-form frame beside it (P1, P2, a line, C).
- **Learner can change:** drag each clause into a slot, or let the figure sort the clauses and compare the learner's order with the standard one.
- **Caption:** "Drag each clause of the hall remark into place. The conclusion goes last, whatever its place in the passage."
- **Why:** the current map shows the finished layout but cannot let the learner build it.

## validity-and-soundness, steps `no-way-to-fail` and `the-badge`

- **Shows:** a small "possible situation" builder for an informal argument. Each premise and the conclusion has a tick box for "true in this situation", and a banner appears when all premises are ticked and the conclusion is not.
- **Learner can change:** tick or untick each claim, and read a one-line story for the situation they have built.
- **Caption:** "Build a situation. If every premise can be true while the conclusion is false, you have a counterexample."
- **Why:** the informal maps show structure only. The row tester works for lettered arguments but not for the milk or badge examples, where the learner has to imagine the situation unaided.

## validity-and-soundness, step `valid-is-not-true`

- **Shows:** a two-by-two grid. One axis is valid or invalid, the other is premises all true or at least one false. Each cell shows what the conclusion is guaranteed to be.
- **Learner can change:** drop example arguments (whales, cats, the wet pavement) into cells, then see which cells guarantee a true conclusion.
- **Caption:** "Place each argument. Only the top-left cell guarantees a true conclusion."
- **Why:** the screen is prose over two maps. A grid makes the four combinations, and the fact that three of them guarantee nothing, visible at once.

## fallacies-and-charity, steps `looks-right-fails` and `what-was-answered`

- **Shows:** two columns, "What was said" and "What was answered", with arrows from the reply to its target. An ad hominem arrow ends at the speaker. A straw man arrow ends at a different claim from the one made. A fair reply ends at the original claim.
- **Learner can change:** switch between the neighbour's remark, the meat-free-day reply and a fair reply, and drag the arrow's target to see what each choice makes the reply about.
- **Caption:** "Follow the arrow from the reply. A fair reply lands on the claim that was made."
- **Why:** these two slips are about where a reply is aimed, which the argument map cannot show.

## fallacies-and-charity, step `the-strongest-version`

- **Shows:** a slider from the weakest to the strongest reading of Jo's argument. The added premise changes as the slider moves, with a marker for how much it commits the speaker to.
- **Learner can change:** the slider position, and a toggle to show which readings the speaker would accept.
- **Caption:** "Slide from the weakest reading to the strongest. The best one to answer is the strongest the speaker would accept."
- **Why:** the screen now offers two readings. A slider shows that there is a range, and that charity picks a point on it.

## justified-true-belief, step `safe-beliefs`

- **Shows:** a row of "nearby situations" for one belief. For Tess's guess, one situation has the coin landing heads and the next has it landing tails, and in the second her belief is false. For Kofi, every nearby situation has the coin landing as he sees it. Each situation is marked "belief still true" or "belief false".
- **Learner can change:** pick a case (Tess, Kofi, the stopped clock, the fake-barn county) and step the world through nearby variations to see whether the belief survives.
- **Caption:** "Step through the nearby situations. A belief is safe when it stays true in every one where the person forms it the same way."
- **Why:** the knowledge-case figure shows one situation and whether its evidence is connected. It cannot show what would have happened in a slightly different one.

## scepticism-and-closure, step `the-deceiver`

- **Shows:** three layers of doubt stacked on one belief about the world. A dream leaves arithmetic standing, the demon removes it, and "I exist" is the one claim that survives all three.
- **Learner can change:** switch each layer on or off and watch which beliefs stay and which go.
- **Caption:** "Switch on the dream, then the demon. Each layer removes more of what you can be sure of, and one claim is left."
- **Why:** the screen is prose only, because the argument maps cannot show a belief surviving or failing under a hypothesis.

## mind-and-body, step `elisabeths-question`

- **Shows:** a mind (a point with no size) and a body (a block that takes up space) with an arrow between them. The arrow is drawn where the mind is supposed to push the body, and a billiard-ball collision sits beside it for comparison.
- **Learner can change:** toggle between a contact push (two balls) and a push from the unextended mind, and see where the contact is missing.
- **Caption:** "A ball moves another by touching it. The mind has no place to touch the body."
- **Why:** the interaction problem is about the missing contact. The lesson explains it in words and tests it with a truth table, with nothing that shows it.

## personal-identity, steps `a-brain-split-in-two` and `the-teleporter-machine`

- **Shows:** one person's life as a line, with the "same person" label following the learner's chosen criterion. Under the bodily criterion the label follows the body, under the memory criterion it follows the memories, and at a split or a copy it shows what each criterion says about each branch.
- **Learner can change:** switch between the bodily and psychological criterion, and between fission, the teleporter and the branch line, to see which branch keeps the label and where both or neither does.
- **Caption:** "Pick a criterion and a case. The label stays with whoever the criterion follows, and sometimes it lands on two people or on nobody."
- **Why:** the current persistence figure draws the stages and the links but cannot show a verdict, so the screens describe in words what each criterion concludes.

## consequentialism, step `when-the-total-outvotes-a-person`

- **Shows:** a bar for each person affected by a choice, with losses below a baseline and gains above it, and a running total.
- **Learner can change:** the number of viewers in the transmitter room, to find the point where the total flips from favouring Jones to favouring the broadcast.
- **Caption:** "Add viewers one at a time. Each loses a little, and at some number the total outweighs Jones's hour of suffering."
- **Why:** the expected-utility figure gives two totals but cannot show a sum building up person by person, which is what aggregation means.

## kant-and-the-footbridge, step `two-plans`

- **Shows:** each trolley plan as a short chain of steps, with a "remove the one person" toggle. In the switch the chain still reaches the five being saved, and in the footbridge it breaks.
- **Learner can change:** the toggle for removing the one person, and the case (switch, footbridge, loop).
- **Caption:** "Take the one person away. If the plan still works, his death was a side effect. If it fails, he was the means."
- **Why:** the trolley figure shows the layout of the tracks but not the plan, and the test for a means is whether the plan survives without the person.

## virtue-and-the-mean, step `habituation` (`how-virtue-grows`)

- **Shows:** the range for courage on its scale at three points in a person's training, with the range shifting and widening as the person practises.
- **Learner can change:** a slider for months of practice, which moves and widens the range.
- **Caption:** "Slide the practice forward. The range of courage moves up the scale as training changes what you can rightly face."
- **Why:** the mean figure shows two fixed people, a passer-by and a firefighter, and cannot show one person changing over time.
