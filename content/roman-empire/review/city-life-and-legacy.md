## Prose

**Orientation.** Look closely at the Colosseum photograph, then use the timeline scrubber to compare the dated events. Notice how public life, citizenship, and Roman institutions extended beyond the western empire.

Roman city life revolved around public buildings: bath houses, aqueducts and places of entertainment. Emperors and wealthy citizens paid for these works, and donors expected public recognition in return. The Colosseum, the Flavian amphitheatre in Rome, was inaugurated in 80 CE and held tens of thousands of spectators at shows the state provided free. In the photograph, its scale helps show how architecture could organise both daily life and public spectacle.

Citizenship also expanded across the empire. It began as the status of the people of one city, then was granted piecemeal to allies and veterans. In 212 CE, the Antonine Constitution extended Roman citizenship to almost every free inhabitant of the empire, placing a very large population under the same body of law.

Constantine dedicated Constantinople as a new capital in 330 CE. In 476 CE, Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, and no new emperor was appointed in the west. This is the date normally given for the end of the western empire. Rule continued in the east from Constantinople for nearly another thousand years. Roman law, Latin and languages descended from it, and road lines still visible in modern Europe outlasted the western state.

**Takeaway.** Roman imperial rule ended in the west in 476 CE, but Roman citizenship, law, language and infrastructure continued to shape Europe.
