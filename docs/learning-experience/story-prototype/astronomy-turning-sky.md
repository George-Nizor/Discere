# Astronomy lesson 1, "The turning sky": story prototype

The text of the prototype the owner approved (writing style and structure). Screens marked *teaching* are explanation only, with a figure where one is named. Screens marked *practice* or *skill check* ask the learner something. The figures were custom for the prototype and are not all available in the real app; this file lists what each one showed.

The interactive page is `astronomy-turning-sky.html` beside this file. Read the `SCREENS` array in it for the exact text.

## Screen 1: The turning sky (teaching)

Go outside on a clear night, away from street lights, and give your eyes ten minutes to adjust. At first the sky looks still, a scatter of points pinned in place. Now pick one star above a rooftop or a tree and watch it for an hour. It has crept along a smooth arc, and so has every other star in the sky.

Face north and the pattern becomes clear. The arcs all curve around one spot, the way the grooves of a record curve around the spindle. Stars close to that spot sweep out small circles, stars further away sweep out big ones, and the circles never cross. The northern sky behaves like a wheel turning slowly about a hub.

*Figure (sky): A flat sketch of the sky facing north from Amsterdam. The ground is at the bottom, with west on your left and east on your right, and stars below the horizon are hidden. Press Play to watch a day go by.*

That wheel is what this lesson is about. It starts with why the sky turns and what sits at the hub. It ends with a trick sailors used for centuries, reading how far north a ship has sailed from the height of that hub. By the end you’ll be able to predict that height from where you live, and say which stars never set from there. It takes about 12 minutes.

## Screen 2: The sky turns because Earth does (teaching)

The cause is closer to home than the stars. Earth spins once a day, and you spin with it. Because you share the motion you don’t feel it, just as you don’t feel a train gliding along a smooth, straight track. What you notice is the world outside sliding the other way. From a train window the trees slide backwards while the train carries you forwards.

The sky does the same thing. Earth carries you round, and the stars seem to rise in the east, climb, and set in the west. One full turn is 360 degrees, and Earth takes about 24 hours to make it.

The picture shows Earth from high above the North Pole, with you standing on the equator. The dashed line points at one distant star. It keeps the same direction however far Earth turns, because the star is so remote that moving round Earth’s rim makes no visible difference to where it lies.

*Figure (spin): Press Play or move the slider. The arrow on your dot points straight up and sweeps round with Earth.*

Follow the star from your point of view. It begins just above the horizon, climbs until it passes straight overhead, and sinks towards the opposite horizon. Then Earth’s own bulk comes between you and the star, and it has set. It stays hidden for about twelve hours before you are carried round far enough to see it rise again.

## Screen 3: practice

**If the sky makes one full turn of 360° in 24 hours, through how many degrees does it turn in one hour?**

Answer: 15°

Feedback when right: 15°. A steady full turn shared across 24 hours gives 15° every hour.

## Screen 4: The hub of the wheel (teaching)

That turning has a centre. Every wheel turns about an axle, and the sky’s axle is Earth’s own axis, the line that runs through the North Pole and the South Pole. Extend that line far out into space and it meets the sky at two spots, the north celestial pole and the south celestial pole.

Earth’s axis keeps pointing the same way through the night, so those two spots stay where they are while everything else turns around them. A turntable gives the picture: the spindle in the middle stays put while the record spins around it. From the northern hemisphere you can see the north celestial pole, and it is the hub of the wheel you watched on the first screen.

*Figure (earth, axis): Earth in cross-section, with its axis extended out to the sky.*

A star sits almost exactly on the north celestial pole. Polaris, the North Star, lies within a degree of it, so it barely moves all night, and people have used it for centuries to find north. It is often assumed to be the brightest star in the sky. It is only moderately bright, and its use comes from where it sits.

One more name before moving on. The point straight above your head is the zenith. It is a different spot from the celestial pole, and as Earth turns it slides across the stars.

## Screen 5: practice

**Which describes a celestial pole?**

- The spot the whole sky seems to turn about (correct)
- The brightest star in the northern sky
- The point straight above your head

Feedback when right: A celestial pole is where Earth’s axis points. The stars wheel about it, so it never moves.

## Screen 6: Walking north (teaching)

The next few screens work on one puzzle. The north celestial pole is a fixed spot among the stars, yet its height above your horizon depends on where on Earth you stand.

Suppose you start on the equator on a clear night and look north. The pole sits right on the horizon, so low that a hill or a row of trees would hide it. Now start walking north. With every day’s travel the pole stands a little higher. Keep going, and when you reach the North Pole itself you look up and find it straight overhead, with the whole sky wheeling around the spot above your head.

*Figure (sky): Choose a place, then press Play. On the equator the pole sits on the horizon and stars rise and set. At the North Pole it stands overhead and every star circles without setting.*

Somewhere between those two places is your own, and the pole stands at some height there. To work out that height we need two ways of measuring: one for how far north you have walked, and one for how high up in the sky something is. The next two screens supply them.

## Screen 7: Latitude: how far north you are (teaching)

The first measure is latitude. It is the angle, taken at Earth’s centre, between the equator and the line out to where you stand. The equator is at 0°, the North Pole is at 90° N, and Amsterdam, which this lesson uses as its example, is at about 52° N. Astronomers write latitude as φ, the Greek letter phi.

*Figure (earth, latitude): Move the slider to walk the observer north. The angle at the centre is the latitude.*

A degree of latitude is about 111 km of ground, so the walk from the equator to Amsterdam is roughly 5,800 km.

## Screen 8: Altitude: how high in the sky (teaching)

The second measure is altitude, the angle between the horizon and whatever you are looking at. Something sitting on the horizon has an altitude of 0°. Something straight overhead, at the zenith, has an altitude of 90°, and everything else falls in between.

Because altitude is an angle, it says nothing about distance. A star and an aircraft in the same direction have the same altitude. You can estimate it with your own hand: held at arm’s length, a fist covers about 10° of sky, so a star two fists above the horizon is at roughly 20°.

*Figure (altitude): Drag the slider to raise the star and watch the angle grow.*

Now the walk can be put into numbers. On the equator, at latitude 0°, the north celestial pole stands at altitude 0°. At the North Pole, at latitude 90°, it stands at altitude 90°. What remains is everything in between.

## Screen 9: practice

Along the walk north, the pole’s altitude rises steadily from 0° to 90°, just as the latitude does.

**Amsterdam is at latitude 52° N. What is the altitude of the north celestial pole there, in degrees?**

Answer: 52°

Feedback when right: 52°. The pole stands as high above the horizon as your latitude. The next screen shows why.

## Screen 10: The pole stands as high as your latitude (teaching)

The numbers matched, and a slice through Earth along its axis shows why. You stand on the surface at latitude φ. Draw a line from Earth’s centre to your feet and it makes an angle φ with the equator. That is what latitude means.

Now think about your horizon. It is the flat line you see at your feet, where the ground meets the sky. It touches Earth at the spot where you stand and meets the line to Earth’s centre at a right angle. The arrow pointing straight up from your head is that same line carried outwards.

The pole is the last piece. It is so far away that it lies in the same direction from every place on Earth, parallel to the axis. Wherever you stand, you find it by looking along an arrow parallel to the axis. In the picture that is the gold arrow.

Now compare angles. The line to Earth’s centre is 90° − φ from the axis, and your horizon is 90° from that line, so the angle between your horizon and the gold arrow is 90° − (90° − φ) = φ. That angle is the pole’s altitude. In the northern hemisphere the pole stands as many degrees above your horizon as your latitude, wherever you walk. Try other latitudes with the slider.

*Figure (earth, geometry): Earth in cross-section. Step through the diagram, then move the slider.*

## Screen 11: practice

For centuries sailors relied on exactly this fact. With a simple instrument for measuring angles and a clear night, a navigator could read the ship’s latitude from the height of the pole star.

**A navigator measures Polaris 41° above the horizon. Taking Polaris to be at the north celestial pole, what is the ship’s latitude, in degrees north?**

Answer: 41°

Feedback when right: 41° N. Measure the pole star’s altitude and you have your latitude.

## Screen 12: Declination: latitude on the sky (teaching)

So far we have followed the hub of the wheel. The stars that circle it need an address too, and astronomers borrow the idea from the map. Extend Earth’s equator out into space and you get the celestial equator, a circle in the sky halfway between the two celestial poles.

A star’s declination, written δ (delta), is its latitude on that sky: the angle between the celestial equator and the star, measured at Earth’s centre. A star on the celestial equator has declination 0°. A star at the north celestial pole has +90°, and one at the south celestial pole −90°. Stars north of the celestial equator carry a plus sign and stars south of it a minus sign.

*Figure (earth, celestial): Drag the slider to move the star north or south of the celestial equator.*

A star keeps the same declination as Earth turns, which makes it a dependable address.

## Screen 13: Declination sets the size of a star’s circle (teaching)

Declination also tells you how big a star’s circle is. A star at declination δ lies 90° − δ from the north celestial pole, and as Earth turns it carries the star round the sky in a circle about the pole with exactly that radius.

Compare two stars. A star at declination +70° is 20° from the pole and traces a small circle. A star at declination 0° is 90° from the pole and traces the largest circle of all. Both complete their circle in the same 24 hours, so the second star has much further to travel and moves faster across the sky, the way a child at the edge of a roundabout covers more ground than one near the middle.

*Figure (sky): Drag the slider to compare stars, and press Play to watch the highlighted star go round.*

## Screen 14: practice

**A star has a declination of +90°. Where is it in the sky?**

- At the north celestial pole (correct)
- On the celestial equator
- At the south celestial pole

Feedback when right: +90° is the north celestial pole, the point the whole sky turns about, so a star there does not circle at all.

## Screen 15: Which stars never set (teaching)

Go back to your place on the walk, at latitude φ, where the pole stands φ above your horizon, and follow one star as it circles the pole. The lowest point of its circle lies directly below the pole, a distance 90° − δ lower, so the star’s lowest altitude is φ − (90° − δ). Move both sliders and watch the marker on the circle.

Whether the star ever sets depends on the sign of that number. If the lowest point is above 0°, the whole circle clears the horizon and the star stays in the sky all night, every night of the year. Astronomers call a star like that circumpolar, meaning “around the pole”. Setting φ − (90° − δ) greater than 0° and rearranging gives δ > 90° − φ. At latitude 60° N, for example, every star north of declination +30° is circumpolar. The further north you walk, the lower that limit falls, and by the North Pole it has reached 0°.

*Figure (sky): The marker on the circle shows its lowest point. It turns green when the point is above the horizon.*

## Screen 16: practice

**From latitude 40° N, what is the smallest declination, in degrees, that makes a star circumpolar?**

## Screen 17: Skill check (teaching)

That completes the walk north. Three questions will show what stuck. There are no hints and no figures, and you get one try at each. After each answer you’ll see the reasoning.

## Screen 18: skill check

**At 15° an hour, how many hours does the sky take to turn through 135°?**

Answer: 9h

Feedback when right: 9 hours. 135° ÷ 15° per hour = 9 h.

## Screen 19: skill check

**From latitude 30° N, what is the smallest declination, in degrees, that makes a star circumpolar?**

Answer: 60°

Feedback when right: 60°. The limit is 90° − φ = 90° − 30° = 60°.

## Screen 20: skill check

**On the equator the north celestial pole sits on the horizon. How many stars are circumpolar there?**

- About half of them
- Only the stars very near the north celestial pole
- None (correct)

Feedback when right: None. At latitude 0° a star would need a declination above 90°, and none has one. Every star rises and sets.
