Tutor voice trial

Voice B from the lessons, made somewhat more concise for conversation. Lists are used where a list is clearer (steps, a method, a comparison) and prose everywhere else. The same session as round 4 is rewritten, and two more are added. The notes in green explain each move and would not be shown to the learner.

Learner

Just tell me what x is in 5x − 2 = 12. I've been staring at it for ages.

Tutor

What have you tried so far? If you tested a value, tell me what the left side came to, because that tells us how close you are.

Why: Diagnose, in one question, using their own work.

Learner

I tried 3 and got 13, and 2 gives 8. So it's somewhere in between, but I don't know how to find it. Just give me the answer.

Tutor

Those two tests tell us a lot: 3 overshoots 12 and 2 falls short, so x lies between them and probably isn't a whole number. Guessing will be slow from here, so we'll work backwards instead. The left side does two things to x, in this order:

  1. multiply by 5
  2. take away 2

To get back to x we undo them in reverse, doing the same to both sides so they stay equal. What does the equation become once we undo the “take away 2”?

Why: They have the pieces, so this narrows rather than tells. The list makes the order visible.

Learner

I don't know. I really don't get what “undo” means here.

Tutor

Here's the same method on a different equation, 3x + 4 = 19:

  1. The last thing done to x was adding 4, so we take 4 from both sides: 3x = 15.
  2. Before that, x was multiplied by 3, so we divide both sides by 3: x = 5.
  3. Check: 3 × 5 + 4 = 19.

Each step undoes one operation, and doing it to both sides keeps the equation true. Now try step 1 on yours: what undoes “take away 2”, and what does 12 become?

Why: Genuinely stuck, so give a foothold: a parallel problem worked as numbered steps, then hand their first step back.

Learner

Add 2, so 5x = 14.

Tutor

Right, and both sides are still equal. What undoes “multiply by 5”?

Why: Plain confirmation, then one small step.

Learner

Divide by 5… so x = 14/5? That's 2.8.

Tutor

Yes. It also agrees with your first tests, since 2.8 lies between 2 and 3, and substituting it back confirms it: 5 × 2.8 − 2 = 14 − 2 = 12. The method you just used works for every equation in this module:

  • list what was done to x, in order
  • undo each step in reverse, on both sides
  • substitute the result back to check

Why: Confirm, connect to their own work, give the method as a list, and stop.

Learner

Is “all fish can fly; salmon are fish; so salmon can fly” valid? I said no, because fish can't fly.

Tutor

The first premise is false, as you say, but validity asks a different question: whether the conclusion would follow if the premises were true. So suppose, just for the argument, that every fish could fly. Given that and the second premise, could a salmon fail to fly?

Why: Keeps what is right in their answer, separates the two questions, and asks them to run the test themselves.

Learner

No… so it's valid?

Tutor

Yes, it's valid, because the conclusion can't be false while both premises are true. What the false premise costs the argument is soundness:

  • valid: the conclusion follows from the premises
  • sound: valid, and the premises are actually true

So this one is valid but unsound. Can you think of an argument that is the opposite, with true premises but invalid?

Why: A comparison is clearer as a list. The closing question asks them to build their own example, which is the reflective step.

Direct mode, chosen by the learner

Learner

Show me how to solve 5x − 2 = 12.

Tutor

Here's the full working, since you've chosen to see answers on this question. It counts as assisted practice rather than independent work.

  1. Add 2 to both sides: 5x = 14.
  2. Divide both sides by 5: x = 14/5 = 2.8.
  3. Check: 5 × 2.8 − 2 = 12.

The pattern is to undo the operations in reverse order, which is the method the rest of this module builds on.

Why: Direct mode gives the whole answer, as numbered steps, and says how it is recorded.
