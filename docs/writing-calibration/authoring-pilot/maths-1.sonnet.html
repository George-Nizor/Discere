What a letter stands for

Sonnet pilot, with emphasis. Underlined terms show a note on hover or tap. Written from the curriculum, not from the old lesson. Questions answer back: try them.

The author's plan

Goal. By the end the learner can read an expression such as 4x + 3 as a calculation on an unknown number, evaluate it for a given value in the right order, and decide whether a given number solves an equation by substituting it into both sides and comparing.

Starting point. An adult who is comfortable with whole-number arithmetic and has met letters in algebra years ago, but may remember them as something to be afraid of. They know the order of operations only loosely and have no secure sense that a letter is just a number whose value has not been given.

Misconceptions.

  • 4x is the digits 4 and x side by side, so x = 6 gives 46 rather than 24.
  • Calculations run strictly left to right, so 2 + 3x with x = 4 is (2 + 3) × 4 = 20.
  • Different occurrences of the same letter may take different values, or a letter stands for a particular number fixed by its position in the alphabet.
  • A value that makes the two sides nearly equal is a good enough solution.
  • Two-step subtraction: in 20 − 2x the 2x is glued together as 26, or the subtraction is done before the multiplication.

Sequence.

  • Start from one equation (4x + 3 = 27) and a number machine the learner can drive, so that the first act is finding a number by trying values and the letter is already meaningful before it is named.
  • Give the reason for a letter (shorter than words, carried between lines, one letter per unknown), name it a variable, and fix the rule that one letter means one number throughout an equation.
  • Reading notation: 4x is 4 × x, multiplication before addition, and name substitution at the moment it is shown.
  • A worked evaluation with the order trap (2 + 3x at x = 4), then the learner evaluates 20 − 2x.
  • The equals sign as a claim; checking a value by substituting into both sides; name solution. Two checks, one with subtraction, to make the learner do the whole routine.
  • A check where the letter appears on both sides (x + 6 = 2x), which tests the one-letter-one-number rule and is awkward to solve by guessing.
  • A self-check on how to find x in 7x + 5 = 2014, which exposes how slow trial is and points to running the machine backwards without teaching it.

Notes. No opener story: the lesson opens on the equation itself. 1. figure

Here is an equation: 4x + 3 = 27. The letter x stands for a number that has not been given to you, and the equation says something definite about it: multiply it by 4, add 3, and the result is 27. The line does not tell you the number, but it does limit which numbers it can be, and the figure lets you see how.

Move x along the machine and watch what comes out at the end. For small inputs the output is below 27, for large ones it is above, and there is one input at which it lands exactly on 27. Finding that input by trying values is a sound method to begin with, because it makes you do precisely what the equation describes.

2. teach

Before letters were used for unknowns, the statement above had to be written out as "a number, multiplied by four and increased by three, makes twenty-seven", and a long statement of that kind is hard to carry from one step of working to the next. A letter is far shorter, it stays the same from line to line, and when a problem involves two unknown numbers each can have its own letter, so that the two are never confused. René Descartes, in La Géométrie of 1637, helped to fix the habit of taking letters from the end of the alphabet, such as x, y and z, for the numbers still to be found.

A letter used in this way is called a variable, because the same letter can stand for one number in one problem and a different number in the next. Within a single equation, however, it stands for the same number wherever it appears. In x + x = 12 both letters are 6, and it would be a mistake to take 5 for the first and 7 for the second, even though 5 + 7 is also 12.

3. figure

Read 4x + 3 aloud and the calculation is already in it. In algebra a number written directly beside a letter is multiplied by it, so 4x means 4 × x; the multiplication sign is left out partly to save space and partly because × looks too much like the letter x. That is why 4x with x = 6 is 4 × 6, or 24, and not 46: the 4 and the x are not the digits of a single number.

The rest of the expression follows the usual order of operationsMultiplication and division are done before addition and subtraction., in which multiplication is done before addition and subtraction. In 4x + 3 the 4x is therefore worked out first and the 3 is added to it afterwards. Replacing the letter by a number and then working out the result is called substitution, and the figure shows it for x = 6, where 4x becomes 4 × 6, which is 24, and the 3 then brings the total to 27.

6. teach

An equation joins two calculations with an equals sign, and the sign is a claim: that the calculation on the left comes to the same number as the one on the right. Like any claim it may be true or false, and which it is depends on the number put in place of x. This gives a way of checking any proposed value without having to trust how it was found: substituteReplace the letter with a number. Met in 'Reading 4x + 3'. it for the letter wherever the letter appears, work out the left side and the right side separately, and compare them.

In 4x + 3 = 27, putting 6 in for x makes the left side 4 × 6 + 3, which is 27, the same as the right side, so the claim holds and 6 is called a solution of the equation. Putting in 7 makes the left side 4 × 7 + 3, which is 31, so the claim fails and 7 is not a solution. The test allows no partial credit, because the equation says that the two sides are equal and not that they are close, and a value that gives 28 has failed just as completely as one that gives 280.
