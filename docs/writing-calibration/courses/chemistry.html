About this course

Everything you can touch, from the water in a glass to the metal in a spoon, is built from a little over a hundred kinds of atom, and chemistry starts from two plain facts about them. The atoms of one element all share a single count, the number of protons in the nucleus, and in a chemical change atoms are rearranged and never lost. Those two facts are enough to explain why salt dissolves, why a flame needs air, and why a given amount of one substance reacts with a fixed amount of another.

The course has twelve lessons in four parts. Meet the particles looks inside a single atom, at its protons, neutrons and electrons, at isotopes and at ions. Build a connection begins with the outer electrons, which decide how an atom behaves, and then shows how atoms join, by handing electrons over to make ionic compounds or by sharing them in pairs. Count a substance reads a formula as a count of atoms, adds atomic masses to find the mass of a formula, and introduces the mole, the unit that links a mass you can weigh to a number of particles you cannot count. Follow a reaction then balances equations, reads them as ratios of moles and finds which reactant runs out first.

Chemistry here is quantitative, so most lessons have you work something out with numbers, and many have figures of atoms and molecules to explore. Each lesson explains first and asks afterwards, and the arithmetic never goes beyond adding, multiplying and dividing.

  • You'll be able to tell which element an atom is, and work out its neutrons, electrons and charge from a few counts.
  • You'll be able to write the formula of an ionic compound from the charges on its ions.
  • You'll be able to find the mass of a given amount of a substance, and the amount from a mass, using atomic masses and the mole.
  • You'll be able to balance a chemical equation so that every atom is accounted for.
  • You'll be able to say which reactant runs out first and how much product a reaction can make.

Teaching

An atom is a small, dense nucleus with lighter particles around it

Almost all of an atom's mass is packed into a nucleus at its centre, which is tens of thousands of times narrower than the atom itself, and the rest of the atom is the region in which much lighter particles move around it.

This arrangement was worked out in 1911 by Ernest Rutherford, from experiments in which Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired fast, positively charged particles at a thin sheet of gold foil. Nearly all of the particles passed straight through, so most of an atom's volume puts up almost no resistance, but a very few were turned back, and only a small, dense, positively charged centre could have done that.

Teaching

Protons and neutrons make up the nucleus, and electrons surround it

The three kinds of particle differ in electric charge. A proton carries a charge of +1, an electron carries a charge of −1, and a neutron carries none. Protons and neutrons together make up the nucleus, while the electrons stay around it because opposite charges attract.

Protons and neutrons have almost the same mass, and each is about 1,800 times as massive as an electron, so the mass of an atom is in effect the mass of its nucleus. The electron was identified in 1897 by J. J. Thomson, and the neutron not until 1932, by James Chadwick, because a particle with no charge leaves almost no trace in the instruments that detect the others.

Lithium has 3 protons and 4 neutrons, which makes 7 particles in the nucleus. Only protons and neutrons are counted as particles of the nucleus, because the electrons are not part of it.

Protons, neutrons and electrons

The nucleus is made of protons and neutrons. A proton carries a small positive electric charge, which we write as +1. A neutron carries no charge at all. The particles outside the nucleus are electrons, and each one carries a negative charge of −1. Electrons are far lighter than the other two. It takes nearly two thousand of them to weigh as much as one proton.

Opposite charges pull on each other, and that pull keeps the electrons around the nucleus. The charges also give us something to count, which is how we will tell atoms apart.

Look at the nucleus of each atom in the picture. It holds protons and neutrons together, and the electrons are all outside it. So when we count the particles in a nucleus we add the protons to the neutrons and leave the electrons out. The carbon atom here has six protons and six neutrons, which makes twelve particles in its nucleus. The lithium atom has three protons and four neutrons, which makes seven.

The number of protons decides which element an atom is

Atoms differ from one another in how many protonsParticles in the nucleus, each with a charge of +1. Met earlier in this lesson. they have, and this count alone makes one element different from another. The number of protons in the nucleus is the atomic number, and an element is a substance whose atoms all have the same atomic number. Every atom with 6 protons is carbon, every atom with 8 is oxygen and every atom with 3 is lithium, however many neutrons or electrons it happens to have.

That the proton count and nothing else sets the identity of an element was shown by Henry Moseley in 1913. He measured the X-rays given off by one element after another and found that their frequencies rose in regular steps, which he tied to a whole number for each element and identified with the positive charge of its nucleus. Arranging the elements by that number reproduces the order of the periodic table, including places where arranging them by mass gets the order wrong.

The count that names an atom

Now we can ask what makes carbon carbon. Put the carbon atom next to an oxygen atom and a lithium atom, and count the particles of each kind. All three counts differ from one atom to the next, so the task is to find which count goes with the name. Carbon has six protons, oxygen has eight and lithium has three.

Every carbon atom ever found has exactly six protons, and every atom with six protons is carbon. Add one proton to a carbon nucleus and you have nitrogen, a different substance with different behaviour. The number of protons is what names the atom.

This count has its own name, the atomic number, written Z. Carbon has Z = 6, oxygen has Z = 8 and lithium has Z = 3. An element is a substance made only of atoms with the same atomic number. Chemists list the elements in order of Z, and that list is the periodic table. To turn carbon into any other element you would have to change the number of protons in its nucleus, and the next screens test whether changing anything else could do it.

Changing a neutron or an electron

Take the carbon atom and add one neutron to its nucleus. The nucleus now holds seven neutrons and six protons. A neutron has weight but no charge, so the atom is heavier and carries the same charge as before. It still has six protons, so it is still carbon. Atoms like this exist, and a later lesson looks at them closely.

Now change an electron instead. Take one away, and the atom is left with a positive charge, since an electron was carrying −1 and is gone. Add one and the atom becomes negative. An atom that has gained or lost electrons in this way is called an ion. The nucleus has not been touched, so a carbon atom that has lost an electron is still carbon.

The picture shows both changes. Compare each atom with the plain carbon atom before it. The proton count is six every time, and what differs is the weight or the charge. So the proton count names the element, while the neutron count changes the weight and the electron count changes the charge.

Teaching

A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons

An atom with no net charge is neutral, and it is neutral because each proton's +1 is cancelled by an electron's −1. The cancellation works only if the two counts are equal. A neutral chlorine atom has 17 protons, which carry a charge of +17 between them, and so it needs 17 electrons to carry −17 and bring the total to zero.

Give that atom one more electron and 17 protons face 18 electrons, so the charge is +17 − 18 = −1, which is the charge of a chloride ion. The neutrons play no part in any of these sums, since they carry no charge and are never counted when charges are added.

Worked example

Counting the electrons in a neutral lithium atom

  1. Lithium has atomic number 3, so its nucleus holds 3 protons, and these contribute a total charge of +3. 3 × (+1) = +3
  2. A neutral atom has a total charge of zero, so its electrons must contribute −3 to cancel the nucleus. +3 + (−3) = 0
  3. Each electron carries −1, so it takes 3 electrons to make −3, and a neutral lithium atom has 3 electrons. 3 × (−1) = −3

Opener

Boron is listed in the periodic table with an atomic mass of 10.8 u, yet if you could weigh boron atoms one at a time, not one of them would come out at 10.8 u. Each would weigh very nearly 10 u or very nearly 11 u, and the figure in the table is the average of a mixture.

Teaching

Atoms are weighed in atomic mass units

An atom is far too light to be weighed in everyday units, since a carbon atom has a mass of only about 2 × 10⁻²⁶ kg. Chemists therefore use a unit of their own, the atomic mass unit, written u, which is defined as one twelfth of the mass of a carbon atom with 6 protons and 6 neutrons. That atom has a mass of exactly 12 u, by this definition.

A nucleus of 12 particles weighs 12 u, so each proton and each neutron has a mass close to 1 u, and measurement agrees: a proton is 1.007 u and a neutron 1.009 u, while an electron is only about 1/1,800 of 1 u. The mass of an atom in u is therefore very nearly the number of particles in its nucleus, because the electrons add almost nothing, and that count turns out to be the most useful way of labelling an atom.

A unit for weighing atoms

A single atom is far too light for the gram to be a useful unit. One carbon atom weighs roughly 0.00000000000000000000002 g, and numbers like that are awkward to write, let alone compare. Chemists use a unit made for the job, the atomic mass unit, written u. It is defined so that a carbon atom with six protons and six neutrons weighs exactly 12 u.

That makes the weight of an atom easy to estimate. Count the protons and neutrons in its nucleus and the total is its weight in u, near enough. The mass number

Counting the heavy particles gives every nucleus a whole number, and that number has a name. The mass number, written A, is the number of protons plus the number of neutrons in a nucleus. It counts particles, so it is always a whole number. Chemists write it after the name of the element, as in carbon-12 or sodium-23.

Keep two counts apart. The atomic number Z counts protons only and names the element. The mass number A counts protons and neutrons together and tells you the weight. Writing N for the number of neutrons, A = Z + N.

Isotopes are atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons

The atomic numberThe number of protons in the nucleus, which names the element. Met in “What makes an element?”. fixes the element and only the neutron count is free to vary, so the atoms of a single element can have different mass numbers. Atoms with the same atomic number and different mass numbers are called isotopes of that element. Chlorine is a familiar case: chlorine-35 has 17 protons and 18 neutrons, and chlorine-37 has 17 protons and 20 neutrons.

The first evidence came in 1913, when J. J. Thomson passed a beam of neon ions through electric and magnetic fields and found that it divided into two paths, as though the gas held two kinds of atom with different masses. Frederick Soddy, who had been studying radioactive elements that no chemical process could separate, named such atoms isotopes that year, from Greek words meaning "the same place", because they occupy the same place in the periodic table.

Gaining and losing electrons

Opener

Neither pure water nor a dry crystal of salt carries an electric current, yet a solution of salt in water carries it well. In the 1830s Michael Faraday, who was studying how current passes through solutions, concluded that such a solution must contain particles that move through it carrying electric charge, and in 1834 he named them ions, from a Greek word meaning "going".

An ion is an atom, or a group of atoms, that has become charged, and the first lesson showed that it differs from its atom only in its electrons. What remains is to find how large the charge is and whether it is positive or negative, and both can be read off from nothing more than a count of protons and a count of electrons.

By the end you'll be able to work out the signed charge of an ion from its protons and electrons.

Teaching

Electrons occupy shells, and each shell holds a limited number

The electrons of an atom are arranged in shells, which can be pictured as rings of increasing size around the nucleus. Each shell can hold only so many electrons: the first, nearest the nucleus, holds at most 2, the second holds at most 8, and for the first eighteen elements the third takes whatever remains, up to 8.

The electrons fill the shells in order, and the nearest shell is filled completely before any electron goes into the next. Nitrogen, with 7 electrons, therefore has 2 in the first shell and the other 5 in the second, an arrangement written 2, 5, while sodium, with 11, has 2, 8 and 1, written 2, 8, 1. The picture of shells goes back to Niels Bohr's atom of 1913, and it works as a counting rule: it does not show where an electron really is, but it gives the right number of outer electrons for these small atoms.

Opener

Table salt is written NaCl, which looks like a molecule made of one sodium atom joined to one chlorine atom. Begin with the elements that each appear in one substance on each side, and leave until last any element that appears by itself, such as the O₂ in a burning reaction, because changing the coefficient of a free element does not upset any other element. Once you know how many batches happen, the product follows. Each batch makes the product's coefficient in moles, so the amount of product is the number of batches times that coefficient. The leftover is what the limiting reactant did not use up

The limiting reactant is used up completely, and the excess reactant leaves a remainder that is found in two steps: the amount used is the number of batches times its coefficient, and the amount left is the amount supplied minus the amount used. The number of batches need not be a whole number. Checking the answer

A limiting reactant sum can end in only three ways. The two batch counts differ and one reactant limits. The counts are equal and both reactants run out together. Whichever happens, the amount of product is the smaller batch count times the product's coefficient.

A quick check catches most slips. Add up what the batches used and compare it with what you had. In the water example the 3 batches used 6 mol of hydrogen and 3 mol of oxygen, and you started with 8 and 3, so nothing was used that did not exist. If a sum says more was used than you started with, the batch count came from the wrong reactant.

Supplies are often given as masses. Batches count moles, so change each mass to moles first with n = m ÷ M. For example, 16 g of O₂ is 16 ÷ 32 = 0.5 mol, and that is the number to divide by the coefficient.

Skill check

How do you identify a limiting reactant from given molar amounts?

  • Choose the smaller coefficient
  • Choose the smaller amount divided by its coefficient
  • Choose the heavier molecule

After a right answer: Dividing each amount by its coefficient gives the number of batches it supports, and the smallest number belongs to the limiting reactant.

After a wrong answer: The coefficient says how much a batch uses and not how much is supplied. The amount available has to be compared as well, which the division does.

After a wrong answer: Mass is not the test. Each reactant is compared by the number of batches its amount supports, and a heavy molecule can be plentiful.

If the supplies are in exactly the ratio of the equation and react completely, what is left over?

  • A fixed half of each reactant
  • No excess reactant
  • The reactant with the larger molar mass

After a right answer: When the supplies are in exactly the ratio of the equation, every reactant is used up together, so nothing is left over.

After a wrong answer: No fixed fraction remains. When the supplies match the ratio, each reactant supports the same number of batches, and all of both is used.

After a wrong answer: Molar mass plays no part. Both reactants support the same number of batches, so both are used up completely.

Recap

The reactant that supports the fewest batches limits the reaction, and it sets the theoretical yield, while the other reactants leave a remainder.

A reaction runs in batches of the amounts in the equation. Dividing each reactant's amount by its coefficient gives the batches it supports, and the smallest number identifies the limiting reactant, which sets the theoretical yield: batches times the coefficient of the product.

Every other reactant is in excess, and what remains is the amount supplied minus the amount used, while supplies in exactly the ratio of the equation leave nothing. This is the end of the chain that the course built: counts of particles give formulas, formulas give masses, masses give moles, and moles with a balanced equation give the amounts of everything a reaction uses and makes.
