About this course

A 9 V battery, a 450 Ω resistor and a wire joining them make the simplest working circuit, and what flows in it can be calculated from two numbers: the current is 9 divided by 450, which is 0.02 A. The same arithmetic, extended a step at a time, describes torches, phone chargers and every board in an Arduino starter kit, and it explains why a part burns out when it is asked to carry more than it was made for.

The course has five lessons, each resting on the one before, and all of them use low-voltage direct current from a battery or a USB supply. The first follows the current round a single loop and arrives at Ohm's law; the next two add a second resistor, first one after the other and then side by side; the fourth turns to the energy a circuit moves each second, which decides how hot a part becomes; and the last looks at parts such as the LED that do not obey Ohm's law, ending with the resistor that keeps an LED safe.

Each lesson explains an idea first, works one example through, and then asks you to do the same, so numbers are part of the work and a calculator is worth keeping nearby.

  • You'll be able to find the current in a loop from its voltage and resistance, using Ohm's law.
  • You'll be able to find the total resistance of a series circuit and the voltage across each part.
  • You'll be able to find the current in each branch of a parallel circuit and its equivalent resistance.
  • You'll be able to work out the power of a component and the energy it transfers in a given time.
  • You'll be able to choose the resistor that sets the current through an LED.

Current in a single loop

Opener

Take a closed loop of pipe with a pump in one side of it, and let the water circulate. If you count the litres that pass a mark on the pipe in one second, and then count them again at a mark beyond a narrow neck, the two counts agree, because the loop is full and the water arriving at the neck has nowhere to go except through it. The neck does limit how much the pump can move round the whole loop, since a narrow pipe holds the flow back everywhere at once, but at any moment the same amount passes every mark.

A battery joined by wire to a single component makes a loop of the same kind, with the battery in place of the pump and the wire in place of the pipe. What circulates in it cannot be seen, yet a meter placed in the wire can count it, and the readings behave just as the litre counts do, which is the first thing this lesson sets out to show.

It matters because every circuit later in this course, however many parts it has, is built from loops like this one. Once you can say how much flows round a single loop, given the battery and the part in it, you can also say whether a part from an Arduino kit will cope with a given supply, and that decision separates a circuit that works from one that burns out a component.

By the end you'll know why the same current passes every point of a single loop, and you'll calculate that current from the voltage and the resistance.

Teaching

Current is the rate at which charge passes a point

A metal wire is full of electrons that are free to drift along it, and each electron carries a very small amount of negative electric charge. Charge is counted in coulombs, and because a single electron carries only about 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, one coulomb is the charge of roughly 6.2 × 10¹⁸ electrons.

What a circuit does depends on how quickly that charge moves past a point, and not only on how much of it there is, so the quantity that matters is called current. It is the charge passing a point in one second: if 2 coulombs pass in 4 seconds the current is 2 ÷ 4, which is 0.5 coulombs each second. A current of one coulomb each second has a unit of its own, the ampere (A), named for the French physicist André-Marie Ampère, and so this current is written 0.5 A.

Because current is a rate of flow, the meter that reads it, marked A, has to be placed in the wire itself so that all the flow passes through it. The next screen uses two such meters to compare the flow at different points of one loop.

What flows round a circuit

Go back to the water loop. A flow meter on the pipe would count the litres that pass it each second. A copper wire is similar. It is full of tiny particles that carry electric charge and are free to drift along the metal. Join the two ends of a battery with a wire and the battery pushes that charge round the loop.

This flow of charge is called electric current. Where you counted litres each second in the pipe, here you count the charge that passes a point each second. Charge is measured in coulombs, and a current of one coulomb each second is one ampere, written 1 A. A small torch bulb takes about 0.3 A.

Current is a rate, like speed or litres per second, and it is the first number we need to describe any circuit. The next thing to find out is what happens to the flow when something gets in its way.

The same current at every point

Something that gets in the way of the flow is called a resistor. It is a small part, often a little cylinder with a wire coming out of each end, and its job is to hold the flow back, like the narrow neck in the pipe. Put one in the loop and ask what a meter would read on each side of it.

A loop of wire is a single path. Charge that enters one end of the resistor has to leave by the other, because it has nowhere else to go and nowhere to be stored. If more charge left each second than entered, the resistor would be making charge from nothing. If less left, charge would pile up inside it. Neither can happen, so the same amount of charge passes every point of the loop each second.

People often expect the resistor to use up some of the current, the way a bulb seems to use up a battery. The current is smaller everywhere in the loop than it would be without the resistor, but it has the same size on both sides. That rule holds for any circuit made of a single path.

What pushes the charge: voltage

In the water loop the pump is what makes the water move. In a circuit that job belongs to the battery, and the size of its push is called voltage. Voltage is measured in volts, written V. A bigger voltage pushes harder, so the same part carries more charge each second.

Voltage is always a push between two points, here the two ends of the battery. A AA cell gives 1.5 V and the square 9 V battery gives 9 V, so the 9 V battery pushes six times as hard. Every circuit in this course runs on low voltages like these, from batteries or a USB supply.

A push alone does not settle how much flows. Two loops can have the same battery and still carry very different currents, depending on what is in the way. That second ingredient needs a name of its own.

What holds the charge back: resistance

The name for how strongly a part holds the flow back is resistance. It is measured in ohms, written with the Greek capital omega, Ω. In the water picture a thin neck has a high resistance and a wide pipe has a low one. A 200 Ω resistor holds the flow back twice as strongly as a 100 Ω one.

That gives us three quantities to keep apart. The voltage is the push and the resistance is the hold-back. The current is what results: how much charge gets through each second. More push means more current, and more resistance means less. With the same 5 V battery, a 250 Ω resistor lets through less current than a 100 Ω resistor, because the same push meets a stronger hold-back.

To get actual numbers we need an exact rule that links the three. The German physicist Georg Ohm found one in the 1820s, and it is simple enough to use in your head.

Teaching

Ohm's law: the current is the voltage divided by the resistance

Georg Simon Ohm measured this relationship in metal wires in the 1820s and published it in 1827, and it can be written as a single equation, I = V ÷ R, in which I is the current in amperes, V is the voltage in volts and R is the resistance in ohms. The equation is called Ohm's law, and it puts the voltage on top of the division and the resistance underneath.

The figure shows three loops. A 9 V battery across 90 Ω gives 9 ÷ 90, which is 0.1 A. Doubling the voltage to 18 V across the same 90 Ω gives 0.2 A, so the current doubles with the voltage, while keeping 9 V and doubling the resistance to 180 Ω gives 9 ÷ 180, which is 0.05 A, so the current is halved.

Placing the resistance underneath is what makes a larger resistance give a smaller current, and the units agree, since volts divided by ohms leave amperes. The law describes ordinary resistors, whose resistance stays the same whatever the voltage across them, and the last lesson of this course meets parts that do not behave in this way.

Ohm's law: current from voltage and resistance

Ohm's law says that the current equals the voltage divided by the resistance. In symbols it is I = V ÷ R, where I is the current in amperes, V is the voltage in volts and R is the resistance in ohms. Volts divided by ohms come out in amperes, which gives you a quick check that you have the equation the right way up.

Try it with 9 V across 90 Ω. The current is 9 ÷ 90 = 0.1 A. Double the voltage to 18 V and the current doubles to 0.2 A, because there is more push against the same hold-back. Go back to 9 V and double the resistance to 180 Ω, and the current halves to 0.05 A. Voltage sits on top of the division and resistance underneath, so one raises the current and the other lowers it.

Ohm's law works for resistors, where doubling the voltage doubles the current. Later in the course we meet parts that break that pattern. For now every part follows it, and the next screens put numbers through the equation.

Resistances in series add

Charge going round a series loop has to push through the first resistor and then through the second, so the hold-back builds up along the way. The two resistors together hold the flow back as strongly as one resistor whose resistance is the sum of theirs.

We call that sum the total resistance of the loop, and write it R = R₁ + R₂. A 100 Ω resistor followed by a 150 Ω resistor behaves like one 250 Ω resistor. A third resistor in the chain would add its own value as well, so a longer chain has a larger total than any single part in it.

Once the total is known, last lesson's rule does the rest. The battery's voltage divided by the total resistance gives the current for the whole loop. A 9 V battery across 250 Ω, for example, drives 9 ÷ 250 = 0.036 A through every part of the chain.

The reciprocal rule gives the equivalent resistance directly

Ohm's law then gives the current from the resistor alone. 3 V ÷ 300 Ω is 0.01 A, which is 10 mA, while a 600 Ω resistor still has 3 V across it but gives 3 ÷ 600 = 0.005 A, or 5 mA. The LED has no say in the current, which is set by the resistor that takes the voltage the LED leaves over, and a larger resistor therefore gives a smaller current and a dimmer light.

To choose the resistor for a wanted current, the same relation is turned round: R = (supply voltage − forward voltage) ÷ current, with the current in amperes. A small indicator LED is commonly run at somewhere between 10 mA and 20 mA.

A series resistor sets the current

The answer is a resistor in series with the LED. Both sit on one path, so the same current passes through each, and as in the series lesson the voltage drops around the loop add up to the battery's voltage. The LED takes its forward voltage and the resistor takes whatever is left.

The leftover voltage and the current you want fix the resistor through Ohm's law. Take a 5 V supply and a red LED with a 2 V forward voltage that should carry 10 mA. The resistor has 5 − 2 = 3 V across it. Ten milliamperes is 0.01 A, so R = 3 ÷ 0.01 = 300 Ω.

A different resistor gives a different current, which is exactly the control the LED lacked. The next screens take the same steps with other numbers.

Worked example

Choosing the resistor for a red LED on 9 V at 20 mA

A 9 V battery is to light a red LED, which has a forward voltage of 2 V, at a current of 20 mA. The resistor follows in three steps.

  1. Take the LED's forward voltage from the supply to find the voltage the resistor must take. 9 V − 2 V = 7 V
  2. Divide that voltage by the wanted current, with the 20 mA written as 0.02 A. R = 7 V ÷ 0.02 A
  3. Divide, and volts divided by amperes leave ohms. R = 350 Ω

Applying it

A blue LED has a higher forward voltage than a red one, which changes the voltage the resistor takes, and the rest of the method stays the same.

A 9 V battery lights a blue LED with a forward voltage of 3 V at 10 mA. Calculate the series resistor, in ohms.

After a right answer: The resistor takes 9 − 3 = 6 V, and 6 ÷ 0.01 is 600, so the resistor is 600 Ω.

After a wrong answer: That divides the whole 9 V by the current. The LED keeps its 3 V, and the resistor takes only the rest.

After a wrong answer: That divides by 10 as if the current were 10 A. Convert 10 mA to 0.01 A before dividing.

Skill check

Why is an LED normally wired with a resistor in series with it?

  • The resistor makes the LED conduct in both directions
  • Past its forward voltage the LED cannot limit its own current, so the resistor sets it
  • The resistor raises the forward voltage of the LED

After a right answer: The LED's current climbs steeply past its forward voltage, so the resistor, which takes the rest of the supply, is what sets the current.

After a wrong answer: A diode still passes current in one direction only, and a resistor does not change that. The resistor's job is to set the current in the forward direction.

After a wrong answer: The forward voltage belongs to the LED itself and does not change. The resistor takes the voltage the LED leaves over, and so sets the current.

A component carries 0.01 A with 1 V across it and 0.04 A with 2 V across it. What is its resistance at 2 V, in ohms?

After a right answer: 2 ÷ 0.04 is 50 Ω, whereas at 1 V the same division gives 100 Ω, so the resistance changes and the part is not ohmic.

After a wrong answer: That is the resistance at 1 V. At 2 V the voltage divided by the current is different, which is what shows that the part is not ohmic.

After a wrong answer: That divides the current by the voltage, which turns the relation upside down. Resistance is voltage divided by current.

After a wrong answer: That divides by 25 as if the current were 25 A. Convert 25 mA to 0.025 A before dividing.

Recap

Ohm's law fits only ohmic parts; an LED conducts one way and only past its forward voltage, so a series resistor must set its current.

A part is ohmic when its graph of current against voltage is a straight line through the origin, as a resistor's is, and then Ohm's law gives its current at any voltage. A diode passes current one way only, and an LED conducts only past its forward voltage, with a current that rises steeply beyond it, so the part itself cannot limit the current.
