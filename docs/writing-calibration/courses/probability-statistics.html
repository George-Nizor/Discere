About this course

Probability and statistics are two ways of dealing with uncertainty. Probability starts from a known setup, such as a fair die, and says how often each result should turn up, while statistics starts from results, such as a list of bus delays or a survey of shoppers, and asks what they tell you. Most decisions, from which bus to catch to whether a new medicine works, rest on numbers that vary, which is why both are worth knowing.

The course has six lessons in two parts. The first part, reasoning about chance, counts what can happen with coins and dice, shows how a clue changes the count, and finds the chance of several separate events happening together. The second part, reading data, describes a list of numbers by its centre and its spread, and ends with what a sample of people or things can and cannot tell you about the whole group.

Each lesson explains first and asks afterwards, so a question only comes once you have been shown what it needs.

  • You'll be able to find the probability of an event by counting equally likely outcomes.
  • You'll be able to say how a clue changes a probability, and work out the new value.
  • You'll be able to multiply the chances of independent events and tell them apart from events that cannot both happen.
  • You'll be able to find the mean and median of a list and say which one an extreme value moves.
  • You'll be able to measure how spread out a list is with the range and the standard deviation.

Count what can happen

Opener

Flip two fair coins and count the heads. The count can be 0, 1 or 2, which makes three possible answers, and it is natural to expect each of them to turn up a third of the time. Yet if you repeat the flip many times, exactly one head appears about half the time, and no heads and two heads about a quarter of the time each.

The three answers are real, but they are not three equal shares of what can happen, and the reason is the subject of this lesson. If you can list what can happen so that every item on the list has the same chance, finding a chance becomes a matter of counting, and the same counting works for coins, for dice and for any other fair device.

By the end you'll count what can happen with coins and dice, and turn the count into a probability.

Pick up almost any board game and you will find two dice in the box. You shake them and let go, and everyone leans in to see the total. After a few games you start to notice something. Teaching

When outcomes are equally likely, a probability is a count: the outcomes that fit, out of all of them

The way out is to list the results so finely that none of them can be split. With two coins, the finest list records what each coin shows, first coin and then second: HH, HT, TH and TT. Each item on this list is called an outcome, and since a fair coin has no reason to prefer one face to the other, the four outcomes are equally likely, each turning up a quarter of the time. The count of heads is a different matter, because it gathers these four outcomes into groups of unequal size, which is why 0, 1 and 2 are not three equal shares.

A group of outcomes that we ask about is called an event. "At least one head" is the event made of HH, HT and TH, and "two tails" is the event made of TT alone. When the outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of outcomes in the event divided by the number of all the outcomes, so "at least one head" has probability 3/4, or 0.75, and "two tails" has probability 1/4, or 0.25.

The words equally likely carry the weight of this rule. Dividing by the number of outcomes treats each outcome as worth the same, and if they are not worth the same the division gives a wrong answer, however carefully the count was made.

Two coins, four results

Start with the smallest case. Flip a coin twice and write down what you see, using H for heads and T for tails. The first flip can be H or T, and whichever it is, the second flip can be H or T as well. Written in order, the possible results are HH, HT, TH and TT. HT means heads on the first flip and tails on the second, so it is a different result from TH.

Each of those four results is an outcome: one complete way for the flips to turn out. A group of outcomes you care about is an event. “At least one head” is an event, and it holds three outcomes, HH, HT and TH.

Suppose the coin is fair, so that no outcome is favoured over another. The outcomes are then equally likely, and the chance of an event is its share of the list. Three of the four outcomes are in our event, so the probability of at least one head is 3 out of 4. Whenever every outcome is equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of outcomes in it divided by the number of outcomes altogether.

That sentence is the method for the whole lesson. The only hard part is the counting, and the rest of the lesson is about counting when the list is too long to write out.

Teaching

Two dice give pairs of rolls, and a pair read in order is one outcome

Two dice make a longer list than two coins, and the list is easiest to keep in order if it is built from pairs. Roll one die and then the other, and write the two numbers as a pair, first roll and then second, so that a 2 followed by a 5 is (2, 5). The pair (5, 2) is a different result, because there the 5 came first, and each such pair is called an ordered outcome.

Both dice are fair and neither affects the other, so every ordered outcome is equally likely, and the counting rule from the coins applies again. It applies correctly only if every pair is counted, including both orders of a pair such as (2, 5) and (5, 2).

Two dice make a grid

Coins give a list short enough to write on one line. Dice do not. Roll one die and then another, and the first can show any of six numbers, with the second free to show any of six after it. Writing every pair out would be slow, so we draw a grid: the first roll runs down the side and the second runs along the top, so each cell holds one pair.

The shaded cells in the picture are the pairs that add up to 7. We come back to them shortly. For now the useful fact is the shape of the grid. It always has one row for each face of the first die and one column for each face of the second, so dice with a different number of faces make a grid of a different size. Count along a row and down a column, and you know how many outcomes there are.

The complement is everything an event leaves out, and its probability is 1 minus the event's

Counting the pairs an event leaves out is sometimes shorter than counting the pairs it holds. The pairs that are not in an event make up its complement, and every pair is in exactly one of the two, either the event or its complement. When the condition changes

Opener

Flip two fair coins. Before you look, the chance that both land heads is 1 in 4, since HH is one of the four equally likely outcomes. Now suppose you learn that at least one of the coins is a head. Teaching

A clue crosses outcomes off the list, and the probability is counted among those that remain

Take the four outcomes of two coins again: HH, HT, TH and TT. The clue "at least one head" rules out TT, which has no head, so the three outcomes HH, HT and TH remain. They keep the same chance as one another, and HH is one of the three, so the probability of two heads is now 1/3 and not 1/2.

A probability worked out after a clue is known is called a conditional probability, and the clue is the condition: the probability of two heads given at least one head is 1/3. The method is the one from the last lesson, in which the outcomes in the event are counted and divided by the number of all the outcomes, with a single change: "all the outcomes" now means only the outcomes the clue leaves possible.

A different clue leaves a different list. If you are told that the first coin is a head, then HH and HT remain, and the probability of two heads given that clue is 1/2. The two clues sound alike, but they cross off different outcomes, and the count follows the outcomes.

Teaching

The rule extends to any number of independent events, with one factor for each

The rule does not stop at two events. If three events are independent, the chance that all three happen is the product of their three chances, and so on for any number. Four flips of a fair coin that all land heads have probability 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/16, since each further flip multiplies the chance by another 1/2, so that the chance of a run of heads falls from 1/2 to 1/4, to 1/8 and then to 1/16.

The flips are independent because an earlier flip does not alter the coin that is flipped next. That is why a run of heads does not make tails due: the chance of five heads in a row is 1/32, yet if four heads have already come up, the chance that the fifth is also a head is still 1/2.

The mean is the total shared out equally among all the values

Imagine that the values in a list are amounts of money, put into one pot and then shared out equally. What each share comes to is called the mean of the list. To find it, add every value to get the total, and divide the total by how many values there are. For the list 6, 9 and 12 the total is 27, and 27 shared among 3 is 9.

Every value goes into the pot, so every value affects the mean, and a value that appears twice is added twice. The mean is the number most people have in mind when they say "the average", and it has a useful property: multiplying the mean by the number of values gives the total back, so 9 × 3 is 27.

The variance is the average of the squared distances from the mean

The distance of a value from the mean is the value minus the mean, positive above the mean and negative below it. For the list 1, 4, 7, whose mean is 4, these differences are −3, 0 and +3. Their sum is 0, and this always happens, because the mean is the point at which the values below and the values above balance, so added together the differences cancel.

To stop them cancelling, each difference is squared first, which gives 9, 0 and 9. Squaring makes every term positive, and it makes a far-off value count for much more than a near one. The average of the squares, 18 ÷ 3 = 6, is called the variance. Here the list is the whole group that we want to describe, which is called a population, so the squares are averaged by dividing by how many values there are.

Distance from the mean

The mean is the balance point of a list, so we measure each value's distance from it. Take the list 1, 1, 1 and 9. The total is 12 and there are four values, so the mean is 3. The values lie 2, 2, 2 and 6 away from the mean. Counting a distance below the mean as negative gives −2, −2, −2 and +6.

A survey can reach only the people it has some means of contacting, and if that leaves out part of the population, a bigger sample from the same method keeps the same bias.

Teaching

A probability sample is chosen by chance, with a known, non-zero chance for every member

The balanced sample matched the population mean exactly, but that does not make balancing the cure. Here we knew the two groups and their sizes, and in a real survey we do not know in advance which parts of the population matter, so a surveyor cannot balance them by hand. A sample made up of volunteers, or of whoever is easiest to reach, is chosen by the people in it or by convenience, and nothing stops it from leaning one way.

A probability sample is chosen by chance, in such a way that every member of the population has a known chance of being selected and that chance is above zero. The chances need not be equal, but they must be known and none may be zero. The method is trusted because of how it picks members, which favours no part of the population, and not because one sample happened to land on the right number.

Choosing by chance

The balanced sample hit the population mean exactly, but it had an advantage. We knew the two groups in advance and built the sample to match. The owner usually doesn't know the groups, and a sample picked by hand tends to lean towards the people who are easy to ask.

The usual remedy is to let chance do the picking. Suppose she wrote every customer's name on a card, shuffled the cards and drew some. Every customer would have a known chance of being asked, and nobody would have a chance of zero. A sample chosen like that is a probability sample. The chances don't have to be equal. What matters is that they are known and that every member has some chance, so the method itself can't shut a group out.

Chance doesn't promise a perfect result. One probability sample can still land away from the population mean through plain luck, and one match in a made-up example, like our balanced sample, shows only that it worked there. What chance removes is the systematic lean. It also explains why a very large sample isn't a cure by itself: if the method excludes a group, more answers just repeat the same lean.

Recap

A sample is only the part of a population that is measured, and a sample that leaves part of the population out stays biased however large it is.

The population is the whole group a question is about, and a sample is the part of it that is measured. The population mean divides the total of every member by the number of members, and the sample mean does the same for the members sampled, so the second is only as good as the way the sample was chosen.

A method that leaves part of the population out gives a biased sample, and a larger sample from the same method keeps the bias. Selection by chance, with a known and non-zero chance for every member, is what a probability sample provides.
