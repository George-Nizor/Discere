About this course

Algebra is arithmetic with one number left blank. A letter stands in for the number that is not known, and reasoning about the letter lets a single line describe every case of something at once, such as the height of a burning candle after any number of hours or the amount in a filling container after any number of minutes. The same shorthand runs through science and computing, which makes it hard to read much further in either without it.

The course has six lessons in two parts. The first three, called Rearranging, begin with a letter as a number that has not been given, show that an equation stays true when the same thing is done to both sides, and then use that to undo a calculation one step at a time, in the right order, even when part of it sits inside brackets. The last three, called Straight lines, put pairs of numbers on a grid, measure how steep a line is from two of its points, and end by linking a line on the page to the equation that draws it.

Each lesson opens with a question that you can answer from what you already know, teaches in short screens with a question wherever an idea needs trying, and closes with three questions on its main skill.

  • You'll be able to read an expression such as 3x − 4 as a calculation and work out its value for any number.
  • You'll be able to solve an equation such as 3x + 2 = 23 and say why each step keeps it true.
  • You'll be able to undo a calculation in the right order, including one with brackets.
  • You'll be able to find the gradient of a straight line from two points on it.
  • You'll be able to draw the line for y = mx + c and read its equation back off the line.

Opener

Here is an equation: 4x + 3 = 27. The letter x stands for a number that has not been given to you, and the equation says something definite about it: multiply it by 4, add 3, and the result is 27. The line does not tell you the number, but it does limit which numbers it can be, and the figure lets you see how.

Teaching

A variable is a letter standing for one number, wherever it appears

Before letters were used for unknowns, the statement above had to be written out as "a number, multiplied by four and increased by three, makes twenty-seven", and a long statement of that kind is hard to carry from one step of working to the next. A letter is far shorter, it stays the same from line to line, and when a problem involves two unknown numbers each can have its own letter, so that the two are never confused. René Descartes, in La Géométrie of 1637, helped to fix the habit of taking letters from the end of the alphabet, such as x, y and z, for the numbers still to be found.

A letter used in this way is called a variable, because the same letter can stand for one number in one problem and a different number in the next. Within a single equation, however, it stands for the same number wherever it appears. In x + x = 12 both letters are 6, and it would be a mistake to take 5 for the first and 7 for the second, even though 5 + 7 is also 12.

Teaching

In algebra a number written directly beside a letter is multiplied by it, so 4x means 4 × x; the multiplication sign is left out partly to save space and partly because × looks too much like the letter x. That is why 4x with x = 6 is 4 × 6, or 24, and not 46: the 4 and the x are not the digits of a single number.

An equation is a claim, and substituting a number tests it

An equation joins two calculations with an equals sign, and the sign is a claim: that the calculation on the left comes to the same number as the one on the right. Like any claim it may be true or false, and which it is depends on the number put in place of x. This gives a way of checking any proposed value without having to trust how it was found: substituteReplace the letter with a number. Met earlier in this lesson. it for the letter wherever the letter appears, work out the left side and the right side separately, and compare them.

In 4x + 3 = 27, putting 6 in for x makes the left side 4 × 6 + 3, which is 27, the same as the right side, so the claim holds and 6 is called a solution of the equation. Putting in 7 makes the left side 4 × 7 + 3, which is 31, so the claim fails and 7 is not a solution. The test allows no partial credit, because the equation says that the two sides are equal and not that they are close, and a value that gives 28 has failed just as completely as one that gives 280.

Opener

What keeps the scales level

Start with scales that are level. Say the left pan holds a bag of marbles and 6 loose ones, and the right pan holds 10 marbles. Lift the 6 loose marbles out of the left pan and that pan rises, so the scales tip. Take 6 marbles out of the right pan as well and they settle level again.

Now the left pan holds only the bag, and the right pan holds the 4 marbles left over from the 10. The scales are level, so the bag weighs the same as 4 marbles. You never opened it.

The method comes down to one rule. If two amounts are equal and you take the same amount from each, what is left is still equal. The same goes for adding the same amount to each pan, or for doubling or halving both. A change to one pan alone breaks the balance, and then what you work out about the bag is wrong.

To undo an operation on x, apply its inverse operation to both sides

A move is chosen to undo whatever has been done to x. In 3x = 12 the x has been multiplied by 3, and dividing by 3 undoes that, so dividing both sides by 3 gives 3x ÷ 3 = 12 ÷ 3, which is x = 4. Two operations that undo each other in this way are called inverse operations: adding and subtracting are inverses, and so are multiplying and dividing.

Undoing with the opposite move

The scales say what you may do. To use them you need a plan for getting x on its own, and the plan is to look at what has been done to x and undo it.

If 5 has been added to a number, taking 5 away puts the number back, so adding and subtracting are opposites. Multiplying and dividing are opposites in the same way: a number trebled and then divided by 3 is the number you began with.

An opposite move is called an inverse operation. On the scales it is something you can picture. Say three identical bags balance 12 marbles. Split each pan into three equal parts, which is dividing both sides by 3. One bag then balances 4 marbles.

To get x alone, find what has been done to it and apply the inverse operation to both sides. One move is never allowed. You cannot divide by 0, because nothing undoes multiplying by 0: every number multiplied by 0 gives 0, so there is no way back to the number you started with.

When the numbers go below zero

So far every number in the equations has been positive. Equations can have minus signs and negative numbers too, and the same two ideas apply. Take 2x − 4 = −10. The 4 has been taken away from 2x, and the opposite of taking away is adding, so add 4 to both sides.

The left side becomes 2x. The right side becomes −10 + 4. To work that out, think of a temperature of 10 degrees below zero that rises by 4 degrees. It is now 6 below zero, so −10 + 4 = −6. The equation reads 2x = −6.

The expression 2x + 4 is a short calculation: start with x, double it, and then add 4. If the result is to be 20, the number you start with is the solution of 2x + 4 = 20, and the figure draws the calculation as a machine that you can run for any starting value.

The last lesson solved equations like this by working on both sides, and it gave the moves in a fixed order without saying why that order is the right one. A number is doubled, and then 4 is added, giving 20. What is the number?

After a right answer: Taking 4 off 20 leaves 16, and half of 16 is 8. You undid the steps in reverse, last step first, which is the habit this lesson builds.

After a wrong answer: You added 4. The last step was adding 4, so take it off.

After a wrong answer: That undoes the + 4 but not the doubling. One more step back.

Running a calculation backwards

Take a round of the number game with two jobs in it. “I'm thinking of a number. I treble it and then take away 9. I get 21.” The last job was taking away 9, so that comes off first: 21 plus 9 is 30. The job before it was trebling, so that comes off next: 30 divided by 3 is 10.

It is the same idea as the shoes and socks. Whatever was done last has to be undone first, and each job is undone by its opposite. Read from the result back to the start, a calculation gives you the number you began with. That is what solving an equation with several moves comes to.

A bracket is one package, and the operation outside it is undone first

Brackets change the order in which a calculation is carried out. In 2x + 5 the x is doubled and then 5 is added, but in 2(x + 5) the brackets mean that 5 is added to x first and the whole result is then doubled, so the same two operations appear in the opposite order. With x = 4 the first gives 13 and the second gives 18.

[Figure]

Because the 2 multiplies everything inside the brackets, the 2 is the last step in 2(x + 5), and the working treats the bracket as one package that stays closed until that step is undone. In 2(x + 5) = 18, dividing both sides by 2 gives x + 5 = 9, which opens the package, and subtracting 5 then gives x = 4.

Brackets make a package

In every equation so far the last job was easy to spot. Brackets can hide it. They are the curved marks around part of an expression, as in 3(x + 2), and they tell you to work out what is inside first and treat the result as one package. Steepness as a number

To compare the two paths, share each climb out evenly over its ground. On the short path, 30 metres of climb happens over 15 metres of ground, so each metre along brings 30 ÷ 15 = 2 metres of climb. On the winding path the same 30 metres is spread over 60 metres of ground, which gives 30 ÷ 60 = 0.5 metres of climb for each metre along.

Now the comparison is easy. 2 is four times as big as 0.5, so the short path is four times as steep. The number you get by dividing the climb by the distance along the ground is called the gradient. A gradient of 2 says the path climbs 2 metres for every 1 metre you cover.

Dividing is the whole method. It works for a hill, a ramp, a roof or a line on a grid, as long as you can find the climb and the distance along.

Subtracting in the same order

The picture helps when the points are close together, but the subtraction works anywhere. To get the rise, take the second point's y-coordinate and subtract the first point's.
