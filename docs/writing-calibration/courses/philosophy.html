About this course

Why spend time on questions that facts cannot settle? Whether a true belief counts as knowledge, or whether it can ever be right to harm one person to save five, will not be answered by a better telescope or a larger survey, and yet the answers affect how we weigh evidence and how we treat each other. Philosophy is the study of such questions, and it proceeds by giving reasons that other people can examine.

So the course begins with the skill that everything else uses, which is reading an argument, asking whether its conclusion has to follow, and answering it fairly. We then apply that skill in three areas. The first asks what it takes to know something, and how strongly to believe it when the evidence falls short of certainty. The second asks what a mind is, whether a machine could have one, and what makes you the same person from one year to the next. The third asks how to choose between acts, from the arithmetic of consequences through duty and character to what a society owes its members.

Each lesson works through one case or thought experiment, with questions along the way and a short check at the end, so that you meet each idea in use before it is given a name.

  • You'll be able to set an argument out in standard form and say which premise a speaker left unstated.
  • You'll be able to test whether a conclusion has to follow from its premises by looking for a counterexample.
  • You'll be able to explain why a Gettier case, a justified true belief that is true by luck, troubles the definition of knowledge.
  • You'll be able to say how dualism and physicalism differ and what the Chinese Room is meant to show about minds.
  • You'll be able to compare what a consequentialist, a Kantian and a virtue ethicist would each say about the same moral choice.

Finding the argument

Opener

Suppose a friend says, “We should eat early. The film starts at seven, and the restaurant is a half-hour walk away.” Is she simply telling you three things she happens to know? She isn't, and you would hear that at once: one of the three is the point of what she said, and the other two are there to back it up. Most of what people say when they want to persuade us comes out like this, as ordinary talk with the point and its backing mixed together.

Philosophy depends on being able to separate them. A philosopher doesn't ask you to take a view on trust but gives reasons for it, and before you can ask whether the reasons are any good you have to see what is being claimed and what is being offered for it. That is harder than it sounds when the reasons arrive in the middle of a paragraph, or when the most important one is never said at all.

By the end you'll be able to pick the conclusion and premises out of an ordinary passage, set them out in standard form, and say which premise a speaker left unstated.

Teaching

An argument is a claim together with the reasons given for it

Look again at what your friend said. The claim she wants you to accept is that we should eat early, and the film starting at seven and the restaurant being half an hour's walk away are offered as reasons for accepting it. Philosophers call statements arranged in this way an argument, and the word has nothing to do with quarrelling: an argument is a claim together with the reasons put forward for it. The claim being argued for is the conclusion, and each reason given for it is a premise.

Nothing in a sentence itself marks it as one or the other. “The film starts at seven” would be a conclusion if someone were defending it by pointing at the cinema's listings, and it is a premise only because here it is being used to support something else. What decides the matter is the job a statement does in the passage, so the question to ask of any statement is whether it is being argued for or being used to argue.

Indicator words show which way the support runs, wherever the conclusion sits

Your friend put her point first, but people just as often put it last or in the middle. “The pitch is flooded, so the match will be called off”, “The match will be called off, because the pitch is flooded” and “Since the pitch is flooded, the match will be called off” say the same thing in three orders, with the same reason behind the same claim, so where a sentence stands can't tell us whether it is the conclusion.

What does tell us is a small word that speakers use to point the reasoning in a direction. “Because”, “since”, “after all” and “given that” sit next to a reason, while “so”, “therefore”, “hence” and “it follows that” sit next to a conclusion. We call them indicator words, and they are the first thing to look for when you are sorting an unfamiliar passage.

Teaching

Standard form lists the premises, draws a line, and states the conclusion

Once you know which statement does which job, you can write the argument out in a layout that no longer depends on how anyone happened to say it. Each premise goes on its own numbered line, a line is drawn beneath them, and the conclusion goes under it. This layout is called standard form, and it makes you settle everything that the original wording left loose: how many premises there are, what exactly each one claims, and which statement is the conclusion.

To write a passage in standard form, take the indicator words out and let the layout do their work, since the line already means “so”. Then tidy each statement until it can stand on its own. “The next one” becomes “the next train”, and “it” becomes whatever “it” was.

Speakers leave out premises they expect their listeners to supply

Go back to your friend's remark. The film starts at seven and the restaurant is a half-hour walk away, so we should eat early. Is that enough? It is only if we also take for granted that we want to be in our seats by seven and that dinner takes a good while, and neither was said, although without them the reasons would have nothing to do with the conclusion. People leave such things out because everyone present already accepts them, which means that a great deal of everyday reasoning contains a premise that is unstated: absent from the words, but needed for the reasoning to work. An argument with a gap of this kind is traditionally called an enthymeme, from a Greek word for something kept in the mind.

Supplying the missing premise is a careful job, because you may not add whatever helps the conclusion along. Teaching

A conclusion can serve as a premise in the next step

Arguments are rarely a single step. Take the film again: it starts at seven and the restaurant is a half-hour walk away, so we must leave the restaurant by half past six. That claim has just been reached from two premises. Now suppose the reasoning goes on: a meal takes about an hour, so we should be sitting down by half past five. The claim about half past six, which was a conclusion a moment ago, is now being used as a premise.

A claim that is reached from earlier premises and then used to support a later claim is an intermediate conclusion, and the claim the whole argument is aimed at is the final conclusion.

Valid, sound and counterexamples

Opener

Suppose Ruth comes in carrying a fresh carton of milk, and you reason like this: whoever uses the last of the milk buys more, Ruth has bought milk, so Ruth must be the one who used the last of it. It seems sensible, and the conclusion may well be true. Is there anything wrong with the argument?

There is, and you can find it by asking a different question from the usual one. Rather than asking whether the conclusion is true, ask whether the premises leave any way for it to be false. Someone else might have used the last of the milk and bought more, and Ruth might have bought a carton for no reason at all, and then both premises would hold while the conclusion failed. Testing an argument comes down to a hunt for a case like that, and it is a hunt you can learn to carry out systematically.

By the end you'll be able to test an argument for validity by looking for a counterexample, check a form with a truth table, and say whether a valid argument is also sound.

Opener

Knowledge and Gettier cases

Opener

Two people say that the coin landed heads. One of them stood beside it and watched it land. The other called heads before it was tossed, for no reason at all, and turned out to be right. Both believe that it landed heads, and both are correct, yet we wouldn't say that the second one knew. What does the first have that the second lacks?

On the traditional analysis, knowing is having a justified true belief

A Gettier case is a justified true belief that is true by luck

Gettier's cases show the three conditions are not sufficient

Two words help us say exactly what the cases show. A condition is necessary for something if the thing cannot happen without it, and sufficient if having it guarantees the thing. Having four sides is necessary for being a square, since a square without them is impossible, but it is not sufficient, because a rectangle has four sides without being a square. Being a square, on the other hand, is sufficient for having four sides.

Gettier did not claim that any of the three conditions can be dropped. In his cases the person meets all of them, so nothing he said stops belief, truth and justification from being necessary for knowledge. What the cases attack is the claim that the three are sufficient: if one can have all three and still not know, then something more is needed, and the search for it has occupied epistemologists ever since.

Teaching

Descartes asks how we could tell waking from dreaming

In the First Meditation, published in 1641, René Descartes looks for a belief that no reasoning could undermine. The senses sometimes deceive us, but only about things that are small or far away, he thinks, so he cannot doubt that he is sitting by the fire in his dressing gown, holding a piece of paper. Then he remembers that he has often dreamed that very thing, and that nothing he can name, neither the vividness nor the detail, reliably separates being awake from being asleep. If a dream can contain every sign we use to confirm that we are awake, no such sign confirms it.

That does not make everything doubtful. The sceptic argues from a principle of closure

A sceptical hypothesis would not matter if it only concerned remote possibilities, so how does the sceptic get from it to a claim about what you know? The argument uses a principle that sounds hard to deny. Having hands entails that you are not a handless brain in a vat, or in Descartes's version, that you are not being deceived by a demon. Dretske and Nozick give up closure

The other way to answer the sceptic is to reject the first premise, which means giving up closure. That sounds desperate, but two philosophers did it with care. In his paper “Epistemic Operators” (1970), Fred Dretske asked about someone at the zoo who knows that the animal in the pen is a zebra. Does she thereby know that it is not a mule cleverly painted to look like a zebra? Dretske thought she doesn't, since nothing she has could tell the two apart, and yet being a zebra entails not being a disguised mule.

Robert Nozick, in Philosophical Explanations (1981), reached the same place by a different route. He said that you know p only if your belief tracks the truth, so that if p were false you would not believe it. If you had no hands you would not believe that you did, so you know you have hands. If you were a handless brain in a vat you would still believe that you were not, so you do not know that you aren't. Both philosophers can therefore say that we know ordinary things and still lack knowledge of the sceptic's denial.

The price is that closure is intuitive. Saying “I know I have hands, but I don't know I'm not a handless brain in a vat” sounds odd even to those who defend it, and critics think that oddness counts against the reply.

Odds and a likelihood ratio give a shortcut

There is a shorter way to reach the same answers. The odds of a hypothesis compare the cases in which it is true with those in which it is false, so a prior of 10% corresponds to odds of 1 to 9. The strength of a piece of evidence can be summed up in one number, the likelihood ratio, which is the hit rate divided by the false alarm rate. For a hit rate of 90% and a false alarm rate of 10% it is 0.9 ÷ 0.1 = 9.

To update, multiply the prior odds by the likelihood ratio, and the result is the posterior odds. With odds of 1 to 9 and a ratio of 9, the posterior odds are 9 to 9, or 1 to 1, which is a probability of one half, and that agrees with the screening of 1,000 people with a 10% prevalence. The same test applied to a 1% prior, with odds of 1 to 99, gives 9 to 99, which is the 9 in 108 we counted a moment ago.

The practical lesson is that the likelihood ratio belongs to the test and not to the person tested. Whether one positive result is strong evidence depends on whether the prior odds start close to even, as in the first case, or far from it, as in the second.

Bayes' theorem says the same thing in symbols

Elisabeth asked how an unextended mind could move an extended body

Suppose Descartes is right that mind and body are different substances. A body is extended, which means that it takes up space, with a length, a breadth and a depth, while a mind on his view is not extended at all. Yet a thought can lead to an action, since deciding to raise your arm is followed by your arm rising, and so the mind seems to act on the body.

In 1643 Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia wrote to Descartes pressing exactly this point. She asked how the soul, being only a thinking and unextended thing, could determine the movements of the body, given that all movement we know of involves one thing pushing another by contact. Descartes replied that the union of mind and body is something we know through ordinary life more than through reasoning, and Elisabeth was not satisfied. The difficulty is now called the interaction problem.

It is a problem about explanation as much as about physics. A dualist who says that a non-spatial mind moves a spatial body owes some account of how, and Descartes's reply gives none.

Physicalists say everything is physical

The other way out is to give up dualism. Physicalism is the view that everything there is, minds included, is physical. A modern argument for it starts from the thought that every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, so that a movement of your arm is already fully caused by events in your nerves and muscles. If some mental events cause physical events as well, and the physical events are not caused twice over, then those mental events must themselves be physical.

Physicalists differ about how. In the 1950s U. T. Place and J. J. C. Smart proposed the identity theory: each type of mental state, such as being in pain, simply is a type of brain state, in the way that lightning is an electrical discharge. Property dualism takes a middle path. It agrees that there is only one kind of substance, the physical, but holds that mental properties, such as how pain feels, are not physical properties.

The systems reply says the man is only a part

The commonest reply says that Searle is looking in the wrong place. Counting overlapping chains of memory repairs the criterion

Identity has a property that the memory criterion lacks. It is transitive: if A is the same as B and B is the same as C, then A is the same as C. Consequentialism ranks acts by the expected value of their outcomes

A large part of ethics asks how to choose between acts, and one answer has an appealing simplicity. Consequentialism says that the rightness of an act depends only on how good its consequences are, so that the right act is the one with the best results. To compare results we need a measure of how good they are, and consequentialists usually take it to be welfare, how well people's lives go: their happiness, their health, the absence of pain. Imagine that we can put a number of welfare units on each outcome.

Real choices have a complication, which is that we seldom know how an act will turn out. A vaccination programme may work or may cause side effects, and so the theory has to rank acts under uncertainty. The standard way is to weight each possible outcome by its probability and add the results, and the total is the act's expected utility. A simple game shows how it works. Suppose a game pays 12 points if you roll a six and nothing otherwise. A six comes up one time in six, so over many rolls you win on average 1/6 × 12 = 2 points per roll, even though no single roll ever pays exactly 2. That average is the game's expected utility, and a consequentialist ranks acts by it.

A harm counts as a negative number

Most acts risk harm as well as promising benefit, and the theory handles harm in the simplest possible way, by giving it a negative value. The harm is multiplied by its probability like any other outcome and added to the total, where it pulls the total down. A harm of 30 units with a probability of 0.1 contributes 0.1 × (−30) = −3.

This means that a likely small benefit can be outweighed by an unlikely large harm. An act that gives 10 units with probability 0.9 and costs 100 units with probability 0.1 has an expected utility of 9 − 10 = −1, and the consequentialist should decline it although it will usually turn out well.

The sign shows direction and not importance: a harm written with a minus sign is not being treated as less important than a benefit, only as pointing the other way.

Utilitarianism takes happiness as the good and counts everyone alike

Where do the welfare units come from? The classical answer is utilitarianism, the consequentialist view that the good is happiness and that everyone's happiness counts equally. Jeremy Bentham opened his Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789) by saying that nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. John Stuart Mill, whose Utilitarianism appeared in 1861, called the standard the greatest happiness principle: actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, and wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness. He reports Bentham's maxim that everybody is to count for one and nobody for more than one.

Mill disagreed with Bentham on one important point, which is that pleasures differ in quality as well as in amount. “It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied,” he wrote, “better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.” This answers the charge that the theory makes the best life one of mere enjoyment, but it also makes counting harder, since quality has to be weighed against quantity.

How does anyone come to hit the mean? Aristotle distinguishes the virtues of thought, which we acquire by teaching, from the virtues of character, which come from habituation. We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, and brave by doing brave acts, in the way that people become builders by building and lyre players by playing. Practical wisdom finds the mean, and flourishing is what it serves

If the mean is relative to the person and the situation, no formula can tell us where it lies. Aristotle's answer is practical wisdom, in Greek phronesis: the ability, built up by experience, to see in a particular case what the right amount is. He defines a virtue as a state lying in the mean, as determined by the reasoning of the practically wise person.

Why does any of this matter? Aristotle held that the highest human good is eudaimonia, usually translated as happiness or flourishing, which he described as an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue. A virtue is not a tool for getting something else, such as pleasure, but is part of what living well consists in. That is a different answer from the consequentialist's or Kant's, since it begins from the kind of person to be and not from the act.

The social contract

Opener

What gives anyone the right to make rules for you, and what would things be like if nobody did? Suppose every government vanished tomorrow, and with it every police force, court and law. It is tempting to say that people would carry on much as before, or that they would fall upon one another, and which of those you find more believable says a good deal about what you think people are like.

Hobbes: without a common power, life is a war of all against all

Suppose every government vanished tomorrow, with no police, no courts and no law. What would people do? Philosophers call the condition of people with no government a state of nature, and the first great answer to what it would be like comes from Thomas Hobbes, in Leviathan (1651). Hobbes thought that people are roughly equal in strength, in the sense that even the weakest can kill the strongest, by plotting or by joining with others. He also thought that competition for scarce goods, fear of one another and the desire for reputation would set them against one another, and that where nothing keeps them all in awe, the result is a war of every man against every man. Life in such a condition would be, in his famous words, “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.”

His remedy is for people to make an agreement with one another, each giving up the freedom to do whatever they think necessary to survive, in return for safety. They agree to obey a sovereign, a common power with the authority to enforce the peace, and the agreement is a social contract. Because the alternative is so bad, Hobbes thought the sovereign's power should be very great.

Locke: government rests on consent, and has limits

John Locke, in the Second Treatise of Government (published in 1689), imagined the state of nature very differently. It is not lawless, he said: a law of nature, which reason can discover, already tells everyone that no one ought to harm another in life, health, liberty or possessions. The trouble is that each person is the judge in their own case, which makes disputes hard to settle fairly. People therefore agree to form a government, to protect their lives, liberties and property, and its authority rests on their consent.

That has a consequence that Hobbes's sovereign does not share. A government's power reaches only as far as the purpose for which people agreed to it, and if it turns against the lives, liberties and property that it was set up to protect, Locke argued, the people may replace it.

Rawls: choose the rules without knowing who you will be

The contract of Hobbes and Locke was supposed to have happened, or at least to be a story about why governments are legitimate. In A Theory of Justice (1971) John Rawls used the idea differently, as a test of fairness. Imagine that you are choosing the basic rules of a society, but that you do not know what your place in it will be: whether you will be rich or poor, strong or weak, talented or not. Rawls calls this ignorance the veil of ignorance, and the imagined situation of choice behind it the original position.

The idea is that rules chosen behind the veil cannot be tailored to the advantage of the person choosing, since the chooser does not know who that will be. A principle that you would accept without knowing whether you would gain or lose by it has a good claim to be fair, and this gives a way to argue about justice without first agreeing about who deserves what.

Behind the veil you can maximise the average or protect the worst-off

Suppose that behind the veil you are comparing two societies, and all you know about each is the income of each of three equal groups, from the lowest third to the top third, and that you are equally likely to land in any of them. One reasonable rule is to prefer the society with the higher expected income, which with equal chances of each group is simply the average of the three incomes. Another is the maximin rule, which looks at the worst position in each society and chooses the society whose worst position is best. The name is short for maximising the minimum.

The two rules can agree or disagree. In the figure, society P pays its groups 20, 30 and 40, and society Q pays 12, 30 and 48. Both averages are 30, so the average cannot choose between them, but maximin does, since P's worst-off group gets 20 and Q's gets 12.

Worked example

Two rules, one society chosen

  1. Compare society A, which pays its three equal groups 10, 40 and 70, with society C, which pays 20, 45 and 95. First the averages: A gives (10 + 40 + 70) ÷ 3 = 40, and C gives (20 + 45 + 95) ÷ 3 = 160 ÷ 3, which is about 53.
  2. Now the worst positions: A's lowest group gets 10 and C's gets 20. Maximin therefore chooses C, and so does the average.
  3. Both rules choose the same society, so there is no tension between them here. Ask yourself why.
  4. A dilemma arises only when one society has the higher average and the other has the higher worst position, which is what happens when A is compared with a flatter society.

When the groups differ in size, the average is weighted by share

So far the three groups have been equal in size. If they are not, an equal chance of landing in each group is no longer right, and the expected income has to weight each income by the share of people who receive it. This gives a weighted average. In the figure, a quarter of the people receive 20 units in society X and three quarters receive 60, so the expected income is 0.25 × 20 + 0.75 × 60 = 5 + 45 = 50. Maximin takes no notice of the shares: it still looks only at the lowest income, 20 in X against 28 in Y.

Rawls argued that parties behind the veil would choose maximin, and his rule for society is the difference principle: social and economic inequalities are permitted only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. It does not demand equal incomes, but it allows an inequality only when the worst-off gain from it. He did not say that maximin is the right rule for every choice under uncertainty, but that it is reasonable here, where the parties cannot assign probabilities, care little for gains above the minimum, and face outcomes that would be intolerable.
