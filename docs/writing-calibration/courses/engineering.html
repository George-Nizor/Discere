About this course

Before any bridge, crane or gearbox is built, an engineer has worked out the forces on each of its parts and checked that every part can carry them. Most of that checking is arithmetic with a small number of ideas, applied to one part after another, and this course teaches those ideas in the order in which a designer needs them, starting from the physics of forces, work and energy that you already know.

The twelve lessons fall into four parts. Loads and equilibrium splits slanted forces into parts, measures the turning effect of a force and finds the forces that hold a beam up. Structures finds the force in every bar of a truss, turns a force into a stress and a stretch, and sizes a member with a margin of safety. Beams and stiffness looks inside a loaded beam, at the shear force and bending moment in it, at the stress that bending causes and at how far the beam sags. Machines follows force, speed and power through levers, pulleys, gears and a hoist, counting what is lost on the way.

Each lesson takes one object, such as a mast held by a guy rope, a steel beam on two supports or a rod hanging from a roof beam, and explains an idea on it before asking you to use the idea.

  • You'll be able to split a slanted force into parts and find the force that balances a set of forces.
  • You'll be able to find the reactions at the supports of a loaded beam.
  • You'll be able to work out the force in each member of a simple truss and say whether it is pulled or pushed.
  • You'll be able to turn a load into a stress, and a stress into a stretch.
  • You'll be able to find the speed, force and power at the output of a lever, pulley or gear train.

Forces as vectors

Opener

A guy rope runs from the top of a mast to a peg in the ground, and it pulls on the mast with a force of 8 kN along a line at 45° to the ground. The 8 kN describes the rope, but it is not what the mast feels: one part of the pull tries to push the mast sideways, another part presses it into the ground, and the foundation has to be designed for each part separately.

Splitting a slanted force into parts along two chosen directions, and adding such parts back together, is the first skill of structural engineering. Every later calculation in this course, for beams, trusses and cables, begins with the forces on a body written in that form.

By the end you'll split a slanted force into horizontal and vertical parts and find the force that balances a set of forces.

Picture a strong post in the corner of a yard with a rope tied to its top. One person pulls on the rope and the post leans a little. A second person joins in on the same rope and it leans further. Nothing surprising yet, since two pulls the same way make a bigger pull.

Now a third person takes a second rope and pulls from the side. Someone else runs a rope from the top of the post down to a ring in the ground, so it slants. Each rope pulls in its own direction, and the post feels all of them at once. Bridges, cranes and roof frames face the same situation, with cables and bars pulling at every angle.

This lesson follows the post as the ropes pile on. We start with two pulls along a line and then tilt one, which leads to splitting a slanted pull into two straight ones. After that, pulls at right angles go back together, and the last job is finding the rope that would hold the post perfectly still.

By the end you'll split a slanted force into two straight parts and combine forces at right angles.

Teaching

Forces on one line add or subtract, according to which way they point

Begin with the simplest case, two ropes pulling a trolley along a straight rail. A force is not described by its size alone, since a pull of 6 kN to the right does something different from a pull of 6 kN to the left, so every force has both a size and a direction, and a quantity of that kind is called a vector. When two forces act along the same line they combine by ordinary arithmetic, with the direction deciding the sign.

In the figure, pull A is 6 kN to the right. If pull B adds 2 kN to the right as well, the two work together, and the resultant, the single force that has the same effect as both, is 8 kN to the right. If B pulls 2 kN to the left instead, it works against A and the resultant is 6 − 2, or 4 kN to the right.

[Figure]

Taking right as positive, the second case is +6 + (−2), so both answers come from adding signed numbers, and the direction decides the sign. The same bookkeeping will carry over to forces at an angle once each of them has been split into a horizontal part and a vertical part, which is the next problem.

Pulls along one line

Take the post in the yard and start with the simplest arrangement, in which every rope runs along one straight line. Each pull has a size and a direction. We measure size in newtons, and because structures carry big loads we use kilonewtons (kN) instead, where 1 kN is 1,000 N, about the weight of a 100 kg load.

When two people pull the same way, their efforts team up and the sizes add. If one pulls with 6 kN and the other with 2 kN, the post feels 8 kN in that direction. If the second person pulls the opposite way instead, the two pulls fight each other and the post feels only the difference. Both cases are in the picture below.

Adding and subtracting is all we need while everything lies on one line, and that is also where the method stops. A rope that runs off at a slant cannot simply be added, because only part of its pull acts in any one direction. The next few screens deal with exactly that.

Teaching

A slanted force can be replaced by a horizontal part and a vertical part

Return to the guy rope, which pulls on the mast with 8 kN along a line at 45° to the ground. That direction is neither along the ground nor along the mast, so neither of the one-line rules applies directly. What does work is to replace the single slanted force by two forces at right angles to each other, one horizontal and one vertical, that together have exactly the same effect. These two forces are the components of the original.

The force and its components form a right-angled triangle, with the force as the sloping side. If θ is the angle between the force and the horizontal, the side that touches the angle is the horizontal component, F cos θ, and the side opposite the angle is the vertical component, F sin θ. For 8 kN at 45° both are 8 × 0.7071, which is 5.66 kN, so the rope pulls the mast sideways with 5.66 kN and presses it into the ground with 5.66 kN. At 30° the triangle is flatter, and the horizontal share grows to 6.93 kN while the vertical share falls to 4.00 kN.

[Figure]

Two checks are worth making every time. A component can never be larger than the force it comes from, and the shares must agree with the picture, because a flat force is mostly horizontal and a steep one mostly vertical. The usual slip is to swap sine and cosine, and the picture catches it, since the side that touches the angle is the one that takes the cosine.

A pull at a slant

Now tie a rope to the top of the post and run it to a hook on a tall tree, some way off and higher up. The rope pulls the post towards the tree, and it also pulls the post upwards. One rope is doing two jobs at once.

We can describe that pull in two pieces. One piece acts across, along the horizontal, and the other acts up, along the vertical. The two pieces are at right angles to each other, and together they have exactly the effect of the original pull. Each piece is called a component of the force. A component is the same pull described in two parts, with nothing extra added. The picture below shows the pieces for two different slants.

The angle is measured up from the horizontal. The steeper the rope, the larger the upward piece and the smaller the sideways piece. A rope lying flat along the ground has no upward piece at all, and a rope pulling straight up has no sideways piece. The next screen turns this into numbers.

Worked example

Splitting a 20 kN force at 60° into its components

A cable pulls on a fixing with 20 kN at 60° above the horizontal. Keep the calculator in degrees mode.

  1. Draw the force as the sloping side of a right-angled triangle, with θ = 60° between it and the horizontal. F = 20 kN, θ = 60°
  2. The horizontal side touches the angle, so it takes the cosine. horizontal = 20 cos 60° = 20 × 0.5 = 10 kN
  3. The vertical side is opposite the angle, so it takes the sine. vertical = 20 sin 60° = 20 × 0.8660 = 17.32 kN
  4. As a check, the two parts must rebuild the force. Squaring and adding them gives back 20². 10² + 17.32² ≈ 100 + 300 = 400 = 20²

Reading a slope instead of an angle

Drawings of real structures often give a slope instead of an angle, such as a cable that rises 3 m for every 4 m it runs across. A slope like that is already a right-angled triangle, with the rise as the upright side and the run as the flat side. The cable itself is the sloping side.

Pythagoras gives its length: √(3² + 4²) = 5. The sine and cosine are only ratios of sides, so the up piece is the force times rise ÷ length and the across piece is the force times run ÷ length. A 10 kN pull on that cable has an up piece of 10 × 3/5 = 6 kN and an across piece of 10 × 4/5 = 8 kN, with no calculator needed.

The shortcut works whenever the sides come out as whole numbers, as with 3, 4, 5 or 5, 12, 13. When they do not, fall back on the angle and your calculator.

Forces at right angles combine by Pythagoras, not by addition

Putting pulls together again

Splitting a force is half the job. The other half is putting forces back together. Suppose one person pulls the post east with 8 kN and another pulls it north with 15 kN. The post feels a single combined pull, called the resultant: the one force that does the work of all the others.

Forces on one line just add. These two are at right angles, so adding their sizes would overstate the result. Instead, treat the two pulls as the sides of a right-angled triangle. The resultant is the long side. If Rx is the pull across and Ry the pull up, Pythagoras gives R = √(Rx² + Ry²). For our post the squares add up to 64 + 225 = 289, and the square root of 289 is 17 kN.

Notice that 17 kN is less than 8 + 15 = 23 kN. Two pulls at right angles partly pull in different directions, so they never team up as well as two pulls along one line. This is the same triangle we used to split a force, read in the other direction.

Turning effect: moments

Opener

A tight wheel nut will not turn when you push on a short spanner, yet it gives way on a spanner twice as long, with exactly the same push. The force on the handle has not changed, and what has changed is how far from the nut it is applied. A force acting on a body away from a fixed point does more than push it, because it also tends to turn it, and how strongly it turns depends on the distance as well as on the size of the force.

Engineers need that turning effect for everything that turns on a hinge or an axle, from a door to a crane arm, and above all for beams, whose supports have to balance the loads on them in the same way. This lesson shows how to measure the turning effect and how to balance one against another.

By the end you'll find the moment of a force about a pivot, even a slanted one, and balance clockwise moments against anticlockwise ones.

Teaching

The moment of a force is the force times its distance from the pivot

A force applied away from a fixed point tends to turn the body about that point, and the quantity that measures the tendency is the moment of the force. For a push at right angles to a spanner it is the size of the push multiplied by its distance from the bolt, the fixed point about which the spanner turns, which is called the pivot. Physics courses call the same quantity torque.

A force is measured in newtons and a distance in metres, so a moment is measured in newton metres, written N·m. In the figure, a 100 N push at right angles to the spanner, 0.2 m from the pivot, has a moment of 100 × 0.2 = 20 N·m. The same push applied twice as far out, at 0.4 m, has twice the moment, 40 N·m, which is why a longer handle turns a stubborn nut with less effort.

[Figure]

A moment is not a force, so it can never be added to one. A moment of 40 N·m says how strongly something turns, and it can come from a small force acting far from the pivot or from a large one acting close to it, which is exactly the trade that a lever makes.

Turning a bolt

Put a spanner on a stuck bolt and push on the end of the handle, at right angles to it. The bolt turns more easily when you push harder, and it turns more easily when the handle is longer. Engineers call the turning effect of a force about a point a moment. The point it turns about is the pivot, which here is the centre of the bolt.

A moment is found by multiplying the force by the distance from the pivot, measured at right angles to the push: M = F × d. With the force in newtons and the distance in metres, the moment comes out in newton-metres (N·m). A 100 N push 0.2 m from the bolt gives 20 N·m, and the same push 0.4 m from the bolt gives 40 N·m. Doubling the distance doubles the moment. Both cases are in the picture below.

Moments can turn either way, so they carry a sign. Here anticlockwise is positive and clockwise is negative. For a single push the sign hardly matters, but it will once several forces act on the same body. In practice, distance helps as much as force does. To loosen a tight nut with the least effort, push as far from it as you can, at right angles to the handle.

A push at a slant

So far the push has been at right angles to the handle. Real pushes are rarely so tidy, and a hand may push the spanner at a slant. As the first lesson showed, the force then has two components: one across the handle and one along it. The part along the handle only shoves the spanner towards the bolt or away from it, and does nothing to turn the bolt. Only the part across the handle turns it.

For a force F at an angle θ to the handle, the turning part is F sin θ. The moment is then M = F sin θ × d, where d is still the full distance from the bolt along the handle. A quick check is the right-angled case. At θ = 90° the sine is 1, and the formula gives F × d again. As the push swings round towards the handle, the moment shrinks.

The picture compares a push at right angles with a slanted push of the same size at the same place. The slanted one turns the bolt less, because less of it acts across the handle.

Teaching

The perpendicular distance to the line of action does the same job

There is a second way to reach the same result. Extend the arrow of a force in both directions into a long dashed line, which is the force's line of action, and measure from the pivot to that line by the shortest route, which meets it at a right angle. That length is the perpendicular distance, and the moment is the force times the perpendicular distance.

[Figure]

For the push at 60°, the perpendicular distance is 0.4 × sin 60° = 0.346 m, so the moment is 100 × 0.346 = 34.64 N·m, the same as before. The two methods are one calculation grouped in two ways, F sin θ × d against F × (d sin θ), and the second is easier whenever a drawing shows where the line of action lies. It also shows that sliding a force along its own line of action does not change its moment, because the perpendicular distance stays the same.

The line the force acts along

There is a second way to see the same rule. Draw the line that the force acts along, called its line of action, and measure from the pivot straight across to that line. The shortest distance, at right angles to the line, is the perpendicular distance. That is the d in M = F × d. For a push at right angles to the handle it equals the distance along the handle. For a slanted push it is shorter, which is why the slanted push turns less.

One consequence is worth stating plainly. If the line of action passes through the pivot, the perpendicular distance is zero, and so is the moment, whatever the size of the force. Try to open a door by pushing on its hinge and it will not budge. Engineers use this the other way round. An unknown force is given a line through the point they take moments about, and it drops out of the equation.

Finding the moment of 80 N at 40° on a spanner 0.35 m long

A mechanic pushes a spanner with 80 N at 40° to the handle, at a point 0.35 m from the bolt.

  1. Only the part of the push across the handle turns the bolt, and it is the force times the sine of the angle to the handle. across = 80 sin 40°
  2. Work out the force across the spanner. across = 80 × 0.6428 = 51.4 N
  3. Multiply that force by its distance from the bolt. M = 51.4 × 0.35 ≈ 18.0 N·m
  4. As a check, the perpendicular distance from the bolt to the line of action is 0.35 sin 40° = 0.225 m, and the force times that gives the same moment. M = 80 × 0.225 = 18.0 N·m

A body balances when the clockwise moments equal the anticlockwise ones

A moment can turn a body in either of two senses, clockwise or anticlockwise, and engineers give one of them a plus sign and the other a minus sign so that they can be added. A see-saw shows what happens when the two senses meet. In the figure, a 200 N child sits 1.5 m to the left of the pivot, which gives an anticlockwise moment of 200 × 1.5 = 300 N·m, and a 300 N child sits 1 m to the right, which gives a clockwise moment of 300 × 1 = 300 N·m. The two are equal, so the see-saw stays level.

Move the heavier child to 1.5 m and the clockwise moment grows to 300 × 1.5 = 450 N·m, which is 150 N·m more than the anticlockwise one, and that difference is the net moment that tips the see-saw clockwise.

Archimedes proved the law of the lever in the third century BC, and in these terms it says that a body at rest has zero net moment about any point. The heavier child balances the lighter one by sitting nearer the pivot, in inverse proportion to the weights, and the same rule will find the forces that hold up a beam in the next lesson.

Teaching

A support pushes back on the beam with a reaction

Every support holds a beam up by pushing on it, and the force with which it does so is called a reaction. It needs a name of its own because, unlike a load, it is not given in advance: the load is known, while the reaction is whatever the support has to supply for the beam to stay at rest.

Two kinds of support are used in this lesson. A pin holds the beam in place both vertically and horizontally but lets it turn, like the axle of a hinge, while a roller resists only vertical movement, so the beam can slide along it and turn on it. A beam is usually pinned at one end and rested on a roller at the other, so that it can lengthen as it warms without pushing its supports apart. A fixed support, such as a wall that grips a cantilever, stops the turning as well and is met later in the course.

Taking moments about a support

Now put the load nearer one end, and call the left support A and the right support B. The beam is at rest, so the moments about any point must cancel as well as the forces, as the last lesson showed. The trick is to choose the point carefully. Take moments about the support at A. The reaction at A acts through A itself, so its distance from A is zero and it has no moment about A. That leaves the moment of the load and the moment of the reaction at B, which is the only unknown.

Let the beam be 8 m between supports, carrying 16 kN at 3 m from A. About A the load turns the beam one way with a moment of 16 × 3 = 48 kN·m. The reaction at B, written RB, turns it the other way with a moment of RB × 8. Balance needs 8 × RB = 48, so RB = 6 kN. The load is 16 kN in all, so the reaction at A is 16 − 6 = 10 kN.

The support nearer the load carries more, which gives a useful check on any answer. The two reactions add up to the load, and the bigger one belongs to the closer support.

Worked example

Finding both reactions for 18 kN at 3 m and 9 kN at 6 m on a 9 m beam

A 9 m beam has a pin at A and a roller at B, one at each end. It carries 18 kN at 3 m from A and 9 kN at 6 m from A.

  1. Take moments about A, so that RA drops out. The two loads turn the beam clockwise and RB turns it anticlockwise, and the two sides are equal. RB × 9 = 18 × 3 + 9 × 6
  2. Work out the right-hand side, then divide by the span. RB × 9 = 54 + 54 = 108, so RB = 12 kN
  3. The vertical forces add to zero, so RA is whatever remains of the 27 kN of load. RA = 27 − 12 = 15 kN
  4. As a check, take moments about B instead and compare. RA × 9 = 18 × 6 + 9 × 3 = 135, so RA = 15 kN

A spread load acts as one force at the middle of its length

Not every load is concentrated at a point. The weight of a deck, snow on a roof or water in a channel is spread over a length of beam, and its size is given per metre, as in 2 kN/m. Trusses: the method of joints

Opener

Three bars pinned together at their ends make a triangle, and a triangle is the one shape that cannot be pushed out of shape without changing the length of a bar. That is why roofs, cranes and bridges are built from frames of bars joined in triangles. Teaching

A member is pulled or pushed along its own length

A truss is a frame of straight bars, called members, pinned together at their ends, which are called joints, with the loads applied at the joints. Because a pin lets a member turn freely, nothing can bend a member sideways, and the only force it can carry is a pull or a push along its own length. Timber roofs and bridges were built as trusses long before they were analysed: Andrea Palladio drew truss bridges in 1570, and the American engineer Squire Whipple published a method for calculating the forces in a truss in 1847.

A member that is stretched is in tension and pulls on the joints at its ends, while a member that is squeezed is in compression and pushes on them. A rope can work only in tension, and a short strut mainly in compression, and the figure shows the arrows that each of them exerts on its joints.

To find the force in a member, imagine cutting the frame apart at one joint and drawing that joint alone with every force on it, as in a free-body diagram. Start at the apex, since only three things act there: the load pulling down and the two sloping members. The truss is symmetrical, so the two sloping members carry equal forces, F.

From the first lesson, a member's upward share is its force times rise ÷ length. Take a truss with a span of 8 m and an apex 3 m high, so that each sloping member is 5 m long. A 30 kN load hangs from the apex. The two members together must hold up the load, so 2 × F × 3/5 = 30, which gives F = 25 kN.

That is more than the 15 kN half of the load, because a sloping member only gives 3/5 of its force upwards. The flatter the truss, the harder each sloping member works.

Teaching

The bottom member holds the feet of the frame together

Move on to joint A. Its forces are the support's reaction, pushing up with half of the load by symmetry, the sloping member AC, pushing on the joint down and outward, and the bottom member AB. The reaction exactly balances the vertical part of AC, which checks the apex calculation, and that leaves the horizontal forces: the outward push of AC has to be cancelled by AB, so AB pulls the foot back towards B and is in tension.

The first case of the figure has a load of 36 kN on a frame 8 m wide and 3 m high, with sloping members 5 m long. Each foot carries a reaction of 18 kN, so a sloping member has 18 ÷ (3/5) = 30 kN in compression, and the bottom member has 30 × 4/5 = 24 kN in tension. In the second case the frame is narrower and steeper, 6 m wide and 4 m high, and the same load gives smaller forces, 22.5 kN in each sloping member and 13.5 kN in the bottom one.

Without its bottom member, the feet of the frame would slide apart on the roller, so AB does the work that the support cannot do. It is also why the tension in AB, and the compression in the sloping members, grow as the frame is made flatter.

Worked example

Finding every member force for 10 kN on a 12 m roof truss

A symmetrical roof truss spans 12 m, with its apex 2.5 m above the supports, so each sloping member is 6.5 m long. A 10 kN load hangs at the apex.

  1. The truss is symmetrical, so the two supports share the load equally. RA = RB = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 kN
  2. At the apex C, the vertical parts of the two sloping members, each F × 2.5/6.5, together carry the load. 2 × F × 2.5/6.5 = 10
  3. Solve for F. The members push up on C, so they are in compression. F = 10 × 6.5 ÷ 5 = 13 kN
  4. At the foot A, the bottom member balances the horizontal part of the sloping member, which is its force times run over length. It pulls on the joint, so it is in tension. FAB = 13 × 6/6.5 = 12 kN
  5. As a check, the vertical forces at A must balance, so the reaction equals the vertical part of the sloping member. 5 = 13 × 2.5/6.5 = 5 kN

A joint with no load and two members in line leaves the third member with no force

Some members carry no force at all, and spotting them saves a joint of arithmetic. Look at the joint in the figure, where members P and Q lie along one horizontal line and member R leaves at an angle. Take the vertical forces there. P and Q are horizontal, so neither has a vertical part, and with no load at the joint the only force with a vertical part is R. It has nothing to balance, so R must carry no force.

Hang a load from the joint, as in the second case, and R has a load to help carry, so it is no longer zero. The rule therefore holds only when the joint carries no load. A member of this kind is called a zero-force member. It still has a job, since it keeps the frame stable and takes force if the loading changes, but it can be left out of the calculation for the loading given.

Teaching

Stress is the force on each square millimetre of a cross-section

Suppose a steel rod carries an axial pull of 24 kN, that is, a pull along its length. Whether it survives depends on how much steel carries the pull, because the same 24 kN is easy for a thick rod and fatal to a thin wire. The measure that matters is the force on each square millimetre of the rod's cross-section, the cut across its width, which is called stress and written σ (sigma): σ = F ÷ A, with F the force and A the cross-sectional area.

With the force in newtons and the area in square millimetres, stress comes out in N/mm², which is called a megapascal, or MPa, because a pascal is a newton on a square metre and a square metre is a million square millimetres. Loads are usually quoted in kN, so convert to newtons first: 24 kN is 24,000 N.

[Figure]

On a rod of 200 mm², which is about 16 mm across, the 24 kN gives 24,000 ÷ 200 = 120 MPa, while on a rod with three times the area it gives 24,000 ÷ 600 = 40 MPa. Tripling the area cuts the stress to a third, and the same happens when identical rods share a load, since each carries the total divided by the number of rods.

Force on each square millimetre

Engineers combine force and area into one number called stress: the force carried by each square millimetre of the cross-section. In symbols σ = F ÷ A, where σ (sigma) is the stress, F is the force and A is the cross-sectional area. Take a rod of 200 mm² that holds the 24 kN load alone. That is 24,000 N ÷ 200 mm² = 120 N/mm².

One newton per square millimetre is called a megapascal (MPa), so that rod is at 120 MPa. Using newtons and square millimetres gives megapascals directly, which is why kilonewtons must be converted to newtons first. Hang the same load from three such rods and each carries 8,000 N on 200 mm², which is only 40 MPa. The picture compares a thin rod with a thick one.

Stress decides whether steel gives way, and force alone does not. A rod that is comfortable at 40 MPa may be in trouble at 400 MPa, whatever force produced it. The same force on twice the area gives half the stress, which is why a thick bar is safer than a thin one.

Strain is the stretch as a fraction of the original length

Stress says how hard the material is loaded, and the matching measure of how far it stretches is strain. A rod 5,000 mm long that stretches by 4 mm has not stretched much, while a rod 500 mm long that stretches by the same 4 mm has stretched ten times as much for its size, so the useful quantity is the extension divided by the original length. That ratio is the strain, written ε (epsilon): ε = δ ÷ L, where δ (delta) is the extension and L is the original length, both in the same unit.

[Figure]

For this rod, 4 ÷ 5,000 = 0.0008. Strain has no unit, because it is one length divided by another, and strains in structures are tiny, typically a fraction of one percent, which is why a loaded steel member does not visibly stretch.

Young's modulus ties stress to strain

Within a certain range, stress and strain rise together in proportion, so doubling the load doubles the stretch. Robert Hooke published this proportionality in 1678, and the constant that connects the two is a property of the material, called Young's modulus after Thomas Young, who described it in 1807, and written E. It makes σ = E × ε. Strain has no unit, so E has the unit of stress, and it is large: structural steel has an E of about 200 GPa, which is 200,000 MPa, and aluminium alloys about 70 GPa.

In the figure, a 16 kN pull on a rod of 400 mm² and 1,000 mm long gives a stress of 16,000 ÷ 400 = 40 MPa, whatever the metal. For steel the strain is 40 ÷ 200,000 = 0.0002 and the rod stretches by 0.2 mm, while for aluminium the strain is 40 ÷ 70,000 = 0.00057 and the rod stretches by 0.57 mm, almost three times as much. The aluminium is no weaker under this load; it is less stiff.

Stiffness and Young's modulus

Stress and strain are linked. While the stress stays low enough for the rod to spring back when released, the strain is proportional to the stress. Double the stress and the strain doubles. The ratio between them belongs to the material and is called Young's modulus, written E. So stress = E × strain, or strain = stress ÷ E.

E measures stiffness: how much stress it takes to produce a given stretch. Structural steel has an E of about 200 GPa. Aluminium alloys have about 70 GPa, so under the same stress an aluminium bar stretches nearly three times as much as a steel bar of the same length. The picture shows the two side by side.

Put E in the same unit as the stress before dividing. 1 GPa is 1,000 MPa, so steel is 200,000 MPa. A steel bar at 80 MPa has a strain of 80 ÷ 200,000 = 0.0004. E says how much a bar stretches. How much stress it can survive is a separate property, covered at the end of the lesson.

Worked example

Finding the stretch of a steel rod 3 m long that carries 27 kN on 300 mm²

A steel rod with E = 200 GPa carries an axial pull of 27 kN. The calculation goes from force to stress, from stress to strain, and from strain to stretch.

  1. Convert the force to newtons, and divide by the area to get the stress. σ = 27,000 ÷ 300 = 90 MPa
  2. Write E in MPa, so that it matches the unit of the stress. E = 200 GPa = 200,000 MPa
  3. The strain is the stress divided by E. ε = 90 ÷ 200,000 = 0.00045
  4. The stretch is the strain times the original length, in millimetres, so that the answer is in millimetres. δ = 0.00045 × 3,000 = 1.35 mm

Beyond the yield stress a rod keeps part of its stretch

The proportion between stress and strain does not go on for ever. Up to the yield stress the rod is elastic, which means that it returns exactly to its original length when the load is taken away. Beyond the yield stress it becomes plastic, which means that part of the stretch stays when the load is removed. Mild steel shows this plainly: its stress–strain curve is a straight line up to the yield stress, then flattens as the steel yields, rises again as it hardens up to its ultimate strength, and finally the rod thins at one point and fractures.

In the first case of the figure, 100 kN on 400 mm² is 250 MPa, below the yield stress of 355 MPa, so the strain is 250 ÷ 200,000 = 0.00125 and the 2,400 mm rod stretches by 3 mm, all of which comes back. In the second case, 160 kN gives 400 MPa, above the yield stress and below the ultimate strength of 510 MPa: the rod survives but is stretched for good, and the straight-line formula no longer says by how much.

Where the straight line ends

So far every bar has sprung back to its original length when the load was removed. That holds only up to a limit. Plot stress against strain for a steel bar and the line is straight at first, at a slope of E. Where the straight part ends is the yield stress. Beyond it the bar stretches a lot for a small rise in stress, and when the load is taken off it does not return all the way. It keeps a permanent stretch, called permanent set.

A factor of safety is how many times stronger a member is than its load requires

A member is designed so that it could carry far more than it ever will. The ratio between the load that would make it fail and the load it is meant to carry is called the factor of safety, and for the chain in the opening it is 150 ÷ 50 = 3.

The reserve is needed because nothing in an engineering calculation is known exactly. The loads are estimates, and the real ones may be larger or may act in ways nobody allowed for; the steel supplied may be a little weaker than its specification, a bar may be a little thinner than drawn, and a weld may be worse than the test piece. The Quebec Bridge collapsed while it was being built, in 1907, after the weight of its own steelwork had been underestimated, which shows what a reserve has to cover.

How large the factor should be is a matter of judgement and of design codes, higher where failure would endanger lives or where the loads are uncertain, lower where they are well known. Here the factor is taken as given, and the lesson shows how it decides the size of a member.

Teaching

Turning the factor around gives the allowable stress and the area a load needs

Design runs the calculation the other way round. The factor of safety is chosen first, and the largest stress that the member may be allowed to carry follows from it: the allowable stress is the yield stress divided by the factor of safety. Steel that yields at 300 MPa, designed with a factor of 2.5, may be worked to 300 ÷ 2.5 = 120 MPa and no further. Since stress is force divided by area, the smallest area that keeps the stress at or below the allowable value is the force divided by the allowable stress. For a tie carrying 48 kN in that steel the area is 48,000 ÷ 120 = 400 mm², with the force in newtons so that the area comes out in square millimetres.

Bars come only in standard sizes, so the area found is a minimum. The bar chosen is the smallest stock size whose area is at least that minimum, which means rounding up and never down, because a bar below the minimum would be working above its allowable stress.

Shear force and bending moment

Opener

A beam 10 m long rests on a support at each end and carries a 35 kN load 4 m from the left end, so the supports push up with 21 kN on the left and 14 kN on the right. Teaching

A cut through a beam shows what the material has to carry

Take the beam of the opening, with its reactions of 21 kN and 14 kN, and imagine cutting it through 2 m from the left support. The left piece is now a body of its own, and like any body it must be in equilibriumThe state of a body at rest, in which the resultant of the forces on it is zero. Met in 'Forces as vectors'.. Only one force acts on it from outside, the 21 kN push of the support, and something has to balance it. The only thing left is the cut face, where the right half of the beam holds the left half, so the cut face must supply a downward force of 21 kN. The vertical force carried across a cut is called the shear force, V, because the two sides of the cut tend to slide past each other.

A second condition has to be met. The 21 kN reaction acts 2 m from the cut, so it tends to turn the left piece about the cut with a moment of 21 × 2 = 42 kN·m, and the cut face must supply an equal moment the other way. That moment is the bending moment, M, and it is what the material across the cut has to resist as the beam bends. A moment that makes the beam curve like a smile, with its bottom stretched and its top squeezed, is called sagging, and it is counted as positive.

At any cut, both are found from the forces on one side of it: V is the sum of the vertical forces on the left piece, with upward counted as positive, and M is the sum of their moments about the cut, with sagging positive. Bending stress and the I-beam

Opener

A timber plank 50 mm thick and 200 mm wide, used as a floor joist, bends easily when it lies flat and hardly at all when it stands on its edge, although it is the same piece of timber with the same load and the same span. On edge it resists bending sixteen times as well, and the only thing that has changed is how far the wood lies from the middle of the plank's depth, about which it bends.

A steel beam goes further, since rolled into an I-shape, with its metal gathered into a wide strip at the top and another at the bottom, it carries the same bending as a solid bar with a fraction of the material. Both effects come from the way the stress in a bent beam is spread over its cross-section, and this lesson works that stress out and uses it to explain the shape.

By the end you'll find the largest bending stress in a rectangular or I-shaped beam, and see why depth and flanges matter.

Teaching

A bent beam is squeezed on one side and stretched on the other

Bend a ruler between your hands and look along its edge. On the inside of the bend the material is squeezed, and on the outside it is stretched, and a loaded beam does the same. When it sags, its top surface becomes shorter and its bottom surface longer, and somewhere between them is a layer whose length does not change at all. That layer is the neutral axis, and in a symmetric section it runs through the middle of the depth. Galileo analysed the strength of a cantilever in his Two New Sciences of 1638, though he took the beam to turn about its lower edge and not about the neutral axis.

A layer twice as far from the neutral axis changes length twice as much, so its strain, and in the elastic range its stress, is twice as large. The layer farthest from the neutral axis, at the top or the bottom surface, is the outer fibre, and it carries the largest stress, compression above the neutral axis and tension below it for a sagging beam.

[Figure]

The stress is therefore not the same over the whole cross-section, as it was in a tie. It is zero on the neutral axis and rises in a straight line to its largest value at the outer fibre, which means that the material close to the neutral axis hardly works at all.

The position of the fulcrum gives three classes of lever

The fulcrum, the effort and the load can be arranged along a bar in three ways. In a first-class lever the fulcrum is between the effort and the load, as in a crowbar or a see-saw. In a third-class lever the effort is between the fulcrum and the load, as in a pair of tweezers or a shovel.

The same balance of moments holds in all three, but the arms compare differently. Engineers describe this with efficiency, the fraction of the power put in that comes out as useful power. A mesh with an efficiency of 0.95, or 95%, passes on 95 W of every 100 W it receives.

A gearbox usually has several meshes in a row, and each pair of gears is called a stage. Each stage takes its share of what the one before delivered. Two stages at 90% leave 90% of 90%, which is 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81, or 81%. Taking 10% off twice would give 80%, which is wrong, because the second loss applies to what is left after the first.

The efficiencies multiply, so each added stage costs a few more per cent. That is the price of the extra torque. A third reduction stage lets the same motor turn the output with more torque, at a lower output speed and with a little less of the power arriving.

Teaching

A motor must supply more power than the load receives

The useful power of a hoist is the load times the speed at which it is lifted, but the motor has to supply more than that, because the efficiency of the drive takes its share first: motor power = useful power ÷ efficiency. In the figure, a hoist with 3 supporting strands lifts 1,800 N at 0.4 m/s. The useful power is 1,800 × 0.4 = 720 W, and if the pulleys are 75% efficient the motor must supply 720 ÷ 0.75 = 960 W, the 240 W difference being lost as heat.

Friction also raises the effort. Recap

Power is force times speed, or torque times angular speed, and the efficiencies of the stages multiply, so the motor has to supply the useful power divided by the overall efficiency.
