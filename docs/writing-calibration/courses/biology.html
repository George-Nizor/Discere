About this course

Biology is the study of living things, from a single bacterium to a whole meadow, and it begins from the fact that every one of them is made of cells that carry and copy their own instructions. People study it to understand how bodies work, why illnesses spread, where food comes from and what happens to wild places when something in them changes.

The course starts small and works outwards in four parts. It opens inside a cell, with its parts, the way substances cross its boundary and the enzymes that keep its chemistry running. Next it follows energy and information, from the way a leaf catches light and a cell uses fuel to the way DNA is copied and cells divide. The third part asks how features pass from parents to offspring, where new variants come from and how a population changes over generations, and the last widens to food webs, the energy that reaches each level, the limits on how far a population can grow and the standard of evidence that claims about living things must meet.

Each lesson begins from a real observation or experiment, such as Robert Hooke's slice of cork or Gregor Mendel's pea plants, explains the idea in connected prose with a figure beside it, and asks you to predict or calculate before the answer is shown. The calculations use small numbers, percentages and simple ratios, and no biology is assumed.

  • You'll be able to tell a bacterium from an animal or plant cell and say what each main part does.
  • You'll be able to predict which way water and dissolved substances move across a membrane.
  • You'll be able to follow energy and carbon through a leaf and through a cell that uses fuel.
  • You'll be able to work out the chance that offspring inherit a trait from a simple cross.
  • You'll be able to read a food web and calculate how much energy reaches each level.

A world inside a cell

Opener

In 1665 Robert Hooke cut a thin slice of cork, put it under a microscope and saw that it was divided into rows of tiny box-shaped compartments, which he called cells because they reminded him of the small rooms in a monastery. What he saw were the empty walls of dead plant tissue, but the name stuck, and it now describes the unit of which every living thing is built.

Cells differ greatly in size. A bacterium such as Escherichia coli is about two micrometres long, so five hundred of them laid end to end would span only a millimetre, while a typical animal cell measures 10 to 20 micrometres across, and only fifty to a hundred of those would span the same distance. Inside that small space each cell holds a boundary, a store of instructions and the machinery to use fuel, and it arranges them in one of two basic ways. This lesson compares the two arrangements and gives each main part its name and its job.

By the end you'll be able to tell a bacterium from an animal or plant cell and say what each main part does.

Teaching

Every cell is wrapped in a plasma membrane

Whatever its size, a cell has to stay separate from its surroundings and still take in food and give out waste. The structure that makes both possible is a thin film around the whole cell called the plasma membrane, which is built from two layers of fatty molecules with larger molecules set into them and is only a few nanometres thick. It works like a doorkeeper, because oxygen and water cross it easily while many other substances can pass only through specific carrier molecules in the membrane, or not at all.

The figure shows an animal cell and a bacterium, and the outline picked out in gold on each is the plasma membrane. Both cells have one, as does every other living cell, so the membrane is a feature that all cells share and cannot be used to tell them apart.

[Figure]

The watery interior that the membrane encloses is the cytoplasm, a mixture of dissolved substances in which the cell's chemical reactions take place and its other parts are suspended. Because the membrane controls what crosses, the cytoplasm can hold a different mixture of substances from the fluid outside, and keeping that difference is part of what it means for a cell to be alive.

Eukaryotic cells keep their DNA in a nucleus, and bacteria do not

A cell also needs instructions, and these are carried by DNA, a long molecule whose sequence of chemical units spells out how to make each of the cell's proteins, the large molecules that do most of its work. An animal cell packs most of its DNA into a nucleus, a compartment of its own wrapped in a membrane, and any cell built this way is called eukaryotic, from the Greek for "true kernel". Plant and animal cells are both eukaryotic.

A bacterium has no nucleus. Its DNA, usually a single circular molecule, lies coiled in one region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, which has no membrane around it, so the instructions and everything else share the same open space.

Where the instructions are kept

Inside the membrane, the first thing to find is the cell's instructions. Every cell carries a complete set, written in a long molecule called DNA, and it uses them to build its parts and to make copies of itself. Where a cell keeps that molecule is the clearest way to tell one kind of cell from another.

Animal cells and plant cells are eukaryotic (say yoo-KARR-ee-OT-ik). In a eukaryotic cell most of the DNA sits inside a nucleus, a compartment with a membrane of its own. A good picture is a locked office in the middle of a busy workshop. The master plans stay safe in one place, away from the noise and the chemistry going on outside. Find the nucleus in the picture of the animal cell.

Now switch to the bacterium. A bacterium has no nucleus. Its DNA lies in a region called the nucleoid, with no membrane round it, more like plans pinned to the wall of one open room. The DNA does the same job, and it simply isn't shut away.

The working parts of a eukaryotic cell

The nucleus is only one of several parts packed into a eukaryotic cell, and the parts are not interchangeable. Each is built for one job, and a part like that is called an organelle, which means 'little organ'. A cell works rather like a workshop with separate rooms, each set up for one task.

The first room to know is the power station. Mitochondria (one is a mitochondrion) take the energy stored in fuel such as sugar and hand it over in a form the cell can spend. A cell that spends a lot of energy, like a muscle cell, is packed with them. Find the mitochondria in the picture to see where they sit.

Then there are the ribosomes, tiny benches where the cell builds proteins by following instructions copied from its DNA. Proteins are the molecules that do most of the cell's work, from building its parts to speeding up its chemistry. Ribosomes are so basic that bacteria have them too. A bacterium has no mitochondria, though, because it has no compartments with membranes of their own.

An animal cell has all of these. A leaf cell has one more organelle, and that is the next stop.

Which way will molecules move?

Opener

In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown watched pollen grains suspended in water through a microscope and saw them jitter about without pause, as if each were being pushed by something invisible. The cause was not settled until 1905, when Albert Einstein showed that the grains were being struck from every side by water molecules, which are themselves in constant, random motion.

That restless motion is what moves dissolved substances around and into cells. A single molecule has no sense of where it is going, yet when many of them are packed more densely in one place than in another, the random movements alone carry more of them away from the crowded region than into it. This lesson uses two compartments separated by a membrane to work out which way the movement goes, where it ends, and how the result changes when the membrane will not let a substance through.

By the end you'll be able to predict which way a dissolved substance moves across a membrane and how much ends up on each side.

Teaching

Random movement carries molecules from where there are more to where there are fewer

Take two compartments of equal size, joined by a membrane that a dissolved substance can cross, and put 16 units of the substance on the left and 4 on the right, where a unit stands for a fixed number of molecules. Because the volumes are equal, the left side has the higher concentration, meaning that it holds more of the substance in the same space.

Every molecule moves at random, and one beside the membrane is as likely to cross to the right as to the left. Nothing pushes the molecules from one side to the other. There are simply more of them on the left, so in any short time more molecules cross from left to right than back again. The result is a net movement from the side of higher concentration to the side of lower concentration, which is called diffusion.

Why nothing seems to change

Return to the two rooms once they hold 12 people each. A visitor looking in would see no change, because the counts stay put. The wandering has not stopped, though. A quarter of each room, which is 3 people, still goes through the door every minute, in each direction.

The traffic through the door is as busy as ever, but the two crossings are equal and cancel out. That is what equilibrium means: the net movement is zero while the people, or the particles, go on moving. In the compartments, individual particles keep crossing both ways at the same rate, and the amount on each side stays the same. In the picture the first case is at equilibrium and the second is not.

This matters to a living cell. A cell that keeps using up the oxygen reaching it holds less oxygen per unit of space than its surroundings, so the net movement never falls to zero. Oxygen keeps arriving through the membrane, and the cell spends no effort bringing it in.

A substance can diffuse across a membrane only if the membrane lets it through

So far the solute could always cross, but a cell's plasma membrane does not let everything through. A membrane is permeable to a substance when that substance can cross it, and impermeable when it cannot.

What the membrane lets through

Everything so far assumed that the solute could cross. A real membrane has a say in that, as the doorkeeper from the first lesson does. A membrane is permeable to a substance if that substance can cross it, and impermeable to it if it cannot.

Those words describe a membrane's behaviour towards one substance at a time. The same membrane can be permeable to water and impermeable to a dye.

Compare the cases. When the dye can cross, the sides level out. When it cannot, each side keeps the amount it started with, no matter how different they are, because nothing crosses to change them. A difference in concentration produces movement only when the substance can get through.

So the first question to ask about any membrane is what can cross it. Water often can when a solute cannot, and the next lesson follows what happens then.

Concentration is the amount of solute divided by the volume it fills

The amount of a dissolved substance, the solute, is not the same quantity as its concentration. Testing an enzyme fairly

Suppose someone adds an enzyme to a mixture and product appears quickly. That alone does not show the enzyme did it, because the mixture might have made product anyway, or the room might have been warmer that day. To judge the enzyme you need a comparison.

The comparison is a control: a second sample matched to the first in every way except that it has no enzyme. It has the same amounts, the same temperature and the same time. Only one thing differs, so any difference in the results can be put down to that one thing. Repeating the test on several samples helps too, because it shows whether a difference is a one-off.

To measure the effect, subtract the control from the enzyme sample at the same moment. If the enzyme sample has made 20 units after two minutes and the control has made 8, the enzyme accounts for the extra 12. Rates add up over time in the same way. Opener

Opener

Each base in DNA has exactly one partner

A DNA molecule consists of two long strands wound around each other. Each strand is a chain of units, and each unit carries one of four chemical groups called bases, named adenine, thymine, cytosine and guanine and abbreviated A, T, C and G. Natural selection changes a population when heritable variants differ in reproductive success

Natural selection follows when three conditions hold. The individuals of a population differ from one another, as the mutations of the last lesson ensure. Some of the differences are heritable, which means they pass from parents to offspring through DNA. And individuals with some variants leave more surviving offspring than others in their environment. Then the variants of the more successful individuals make up a larger share of the next generation, and their frequency rises generation after generation. This process is natural selection.

Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace set the idea out together in papers read at the Linnean Society in 1858, and Darwin published it in full the next year in On the Origin of Species. The change is in the population. No individual changes its heritable makeup during its life, and the change is not planned: it is what the differences in survival and reproduction add up to.

The peppered moth is the best-known example. A dark form was first recorded near Manchester in 1848, and by 1895 about 98% of the moths there were dark, when the trunks they rested on were blackened by soot. Field experiments in the 1950s by Bernard Kettlewell, repeated in the 2000s by Michael Majerus, found that birds ate more of the moths that contrasted with the bark, which is selection at work.

To credit natural selection you need evidence that the variants really differ in the offspring they leave, and a shift in the shares cannot supply that on its own.

Opener

Teaching

Matter is recycled through an ecosystem, and energy flows through it

The arrows carry two different things. Energy enters the web as light, is captured by producers and passes along the arrows as the energy of food. At every step the organisms use some of it to stay alive, and it leaves as heat, which no organism can use to make food. Energy therefore flows through an ecosystem in one direction and has to be supplied again by light.

Matter, the atoms of carbon, nitrogen and the rest, behaves differently, because it can be used again. The dead remains and waste of every organism are broken down by decomposers, chiefly bacteria and fungi, which return the nutrients to the soil and water, where producers take them up and build them into new tissue. Energy flows through an ecosystem, while matter goes round.

Opener

In the early 1940s Raymond Lindeman, a young ecologist at the University of Minnesota, measured the energy held by each feeding level of Cedar Bog Lake and found that at every step along the food chain most of the energy was lost. His paper of 1942 appeared just after his death at the age of 27.

Limits to growth

Opener

In 1798 Thomas Malthus, an English clergyman, argued in his Essay on the Principle of Population that a population left unchecked grows in a geometric ratio, multiplying again and again, while food can be increased only by a fixed amount in each period, and that growth is therefore sooner or later held back.

The argument holds the two halves of this lesson: growth that multiplies, and limits that stop it. A patch of pond lilies, a colony of bacteria or a herd of deer will all grow by multiplying while resources are plentiful, and all slow as they run short. A population changes by its births minus its deaths

Whatever the curve looks like, a population changes through the individuals that are added and those that are lost. In a closed population, one with no individuals arriving or leaving, the only additions are births and the only losses are deaths, so the net change is births minus deaths, and the new size is the old size plus the net change.

A population that begins at 200, with 30 births and 20 deaths in a season, ends at 200 + 30 − 20 = 210. The population grows when births exceed deaths, shrinks when deaths exceed births, and stays the same when the two are equal, even though individuals are born and die all the time. As a population approaches its carrying capacity, births tend to fall and deaths tend to rise, and the two come close to balance.

Births and deaths

The curve is a record of numbers, and the numbers change for two main reasons. Individuals are born, and individuals die. To keep the count simple we use a closed population, one that nothing joins or leaves except through birth and death, so no animals arrive from elsewhere and none walk away. The difference between the births and the deaths in a period is the population's net change.

Take a closed population of 50, with 12 births and 7 deaths in a season. The net change is 12 − 7 = 5, so the population rises to 50 + 5 = 55. What a smooth curve leaves out

The S-shaped curve is a model: a simplified picture that keeps the main idea and leaves out the detail. It is useful because it shows why growth slows and roughly where it levels off. The curve explains the general pattern. To know what one particular population is doing you have to count it. A model that gets the shape right is doing its job, and it is asking too much of it to expect every rise and dip.

Replicates and their mean show whether a difference can be trusted

A single pot may be unusual, so a careful experiment uses many independent pots in each group, called replicates, and reports their mean: the sum of the measurements divided by how many there are. Four control pots that grow 2, 5, 5 and 8 cm have a mean of (2 + 5 + 5 + 8) ÷ 4 = 20 ÷ 4 = 5 cm.

The mean should be reported with the spread of the measurements, because two sets of pots can share a mean and still differ a great deal. Pots that grow 9, 9 and 9 cm and pots that grow 3, 9 and 15 cm both have a mean of 9 cm, but only the first set is consistent. If the difference between the group means is small compared with the spread inside each group, it may be due to chance.

Many pots, not one

One treated pot and one control pot can still mislead, because individual plants vary. One seed may be weak, or one pot may drain badly. To see how much results vary, the test needs replicates: separate pots, each treated independently. Measuring the same pot ten times is not replication, since it only tells you more about that one pot.

With several pots we report the mean, which is the total of the growths divided by the number of pots. Three control pots that grow 5, 8 and 11 cm have a mean of (5 + 8 + 11) ÷ 3 = 8 cm. Three that grow 7, 8 and 9 cm also have a mean of 8 cm, but they tell a different story, because the first set is spread widely and the second sits close together. How far results are spread is their variability. A mean on its own can hide wide differences between pots, and a small gap between the treated and control means is hard to trust when the pots within each group differ a lot.

A pattern seen in the wild shows an association, not a cause

Ecologists cannot always assign treatments, and much evidence comes from observing places as they are. If taller plants are found at wetter sites, rainfall and height are associated. That does not show that rainfall causes the height, because wet and dry sites may differ in soil, shade or grazing, and these other factors, called confounding variables, could produce the pattern, or the cause might run the other way.

A pattern in the wild

An experiment lets the experimenter decide which plants get the treatment. A survey does not. In a survey we go out and measure what is already there. Suppose surveyors visit 40 ponds and find that the ponds with more reeds also have more frogs. That is an association: the two rise and fall together. It is a fair reason to suspect that reeds help frogs, perhaps by giving them shelter, so it gives us a hypothesis worth testing.

An association does not show a cause by itself, because nobody assigned the reeds. Something else could lie behind both. In the figure the frogs rise with the reeds in sunny ponds and in shaded ones, yet the shaded ponds hold fewer frogs at every reed cover, so something other than reeds, such as light, matters too. To show that reeds cause the difference you would need an experiment that adds reeds to some ponds chosen at random and keeps the others as controls.

Skill check

Why report variability across independent pots as well as the mean?

  • It makes a control unnecessary
  • It shows how consistent the results are
  • It proves every plant behaves identically

After a right answer: It shows how consistent the results are. A mean with a wide spread is a less reliable guide than one with a narrow spread.

After a wrong answer: Variability tells you about the spread among replicates. A control is still needed to show what would have happened without the treatment.

After a wrong answer: Variability exists because plants differ. Reporting it shows how much, and it does not prove that they behave identically.

Recap

A fair test measures change from a baseline, compares a treated group with a control allocated by chance, and uses replicates, while an association alone does not show a cause.

A claim becomes testable when it names a quantity to measure, and growth is the change from the baseline. Comparing a treated group with a control group, allocated by random assignment, lets the difference in growth be credited to the treatment, and the mean of several replicates, with its spread, shows whether that difference can be trusted.

A pattern observed in the wild is an association, and unless other factors are controlled it does not show a cause. The same standard applies to the claims met earlier in the course, from the effect of an enzyme to the cause of a change in frequency.
