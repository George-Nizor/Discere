About this course

Psychology is the scientific study of how minds work and why people think, feel and act as they do, and the difficulty is that people are very hard to study. We differ from one another, we forget, we guess what an experiment is about, and we see patterns that aren't there. How, then, does the field find out anything we can trust? That is the question the course keeps returning to, and the answers are practical, because they tell you how to judge a headline about sleep, memory or screens, and why a crowd can stand by while someone needs help.

The course has twelve lessons in four parts. In How we know, we ask what a correlation can and cannot show, why random assignment makes a fair comparison, and how to tell a large effect from a lucky one. Perception and attention looks at how we detect a faint signal, what attention leaves out, and how the mind weighs noisy cues. Memory and learning follows what we can hold in mind, how forgetting works and how to slow it, and how rewards and consequences shape behaviour. Thinking and the social mind finishes with base rates, the pull of anchors and fast intuition, and the classic studies of conformity, obedience and bystanders, read alongside the replications and the critics.

Each lesson explains first and asks afterwards, and most put numbers on an idea so that you can check the result yourself.

  • You'll be able to say why a correlation does not show a cause and name other explanations for it.
  • You'll be able to judge how big a research finding is and how many people a study needs to find it.
  • You'll be able to separate how well someone detects a faint signal from how readily they say yes.
  • You'll be able to work out how likely a positive result is to be right once the base rate is counted.
  • You'll be able to explain how forgetting, conditioning and the pull of a crowd shape what people remember and do.

Correlation is not a cause

Opener

A seaside town keeps two records through the summer: the daily takings of its ice-cream van and the number of swimmers its lifeguards pull out of the pool. Set one beside the other and they rise and fall together, so that a week of big takings is nearly always a week of many rescues.

It's tempting to say that one is behind the other, and a newspaper might well say it for you. Before agreeing, ask what else could produce exactly this pattern. That question is the difference between reading research on people well and being misled by it, and it is the one this lesson trains you to ask.

Ice-cream takings and pool rescues in a seaside town rise and fall together through the summer. What best explains why?

  • Eating ice cream makes swimmers more likely to need rescuing.
  • Hot weather brings more people to both the ice-cream van and the pool.
  • Rescues make onlookers want ice cream.

After a right answer: Something outside both lists can move them together, and the weather is the obvious candidate. We'll give that idea a name once we've seen how to measure a pattern like this.

After a wrong answer: That could be so in principle, but nothing in the pattern shows it. Ask instead what else changes through the summer that could affect both lists.

After a wrong answer: The pattern doesn't point that way either. Look for something that changes across the summer and could affect both lists at once.

By the end you'll read the sign and strength of a correlation, say how much of the variation two measures share, and name the other explanations to rule out before you accept a cause.

A number for the pattern

A picture shows a pattern, but two people can disagree about how strong it looks. Statisticians settle that with one number, the correlation coefficient, written r. It is worked out from the dots, and it always lies between −1 and +1.

Two things about r matter. Its sign gives the direction. A plus sign means the measures rise together, as in the town, where r is +0.75. A minus sign means one falls as the other rises. Its size, with the sign ignored, gives the strength. At +1 or −1 every dot sits exactly on a straight line, and at 0 there is no line to see, only a cloud. The nearer r is to either end of the scale, the more tightly the dots follow a line.

Compare the two pictures. The town's dots hug a rising line, and r = +0.75. The dots for practice and test score are more spread out, and r = +0.4. Both slope upward, and the larger number marks the tighter pattern.

So r tells you which way the dots lean and how closely they follow a line. It says nothing about why they lean that way, and that question is where the rest of the lesson goes.

Teaching

Squaring r tells us what share of the variation two measures have in common

A coefficient of 0.4 sounds like 40% of something, but it isn't, and to see what it does measure we need the idea of variation. People differ in how many hours they practise and in the scores they get, and variation is simply how much a measure differs from one case to the next. When two measures are correlated, some of the differences in one go along with differences in the other, and we find that shared variation by squaring rThe correlation coefficient, from −1 to +1. For practice and test score, r = 0.4 gives 0.4² = 0.16, so the two share 16% of their variation. Squaring also removes the sign, as it should, because a share cannot be negative: r = −0.4 would give the same 16%. The other 84% of the differences in test scores goes with everything else, such as earlier schooling, sleep, nerves and plain luck.

That is why a modest correlation, however dependable, accounts for only a small slice of what varies, and why we should not read r as a percentage.

How much two lists share

Strength can also be put another way. The kiosk's takings change from day to day, and that spread is the variation in the takings. The rescues vary too. Where two measures are correlated, part of the variation in one goes along with the variation in the other.

To find out how large that part is, square r. The result is the share of the variation the two measures have in common, called the shared variation. With r = 0.4 the square is 0.4 × 0.4 = 0.16, so the two lists share 16% of their variation. The other 84% belongs to things that do not line up with the other list.

Squaring also removes the sign, because a negative number times itself is positive. A correlation of −0.4 shares exactly as much as +0.4, since direction has no bearing on how much two lists have in common. A correlation that looks respectable at 0.4 still leaves most of the variation unshared.

Patterns that memory builds

The third way a match can arise is in the mind of the person looking. A driver says it always rains the day after he washes his car. He remembers the rainy times because they annoyed him, and he forgets the dry times, which he never gave a thought. To test his belief you have to count every day, not only the memorable ones.

Say he washed the car on 10 days and it rained after 4 of them. On the other 90 days it rained after 36. The counts are different sizes, so turn each into a percentage first: 4 out of 10 is 40%, and 36 out of 90 is also 40%. Rain followed a wash no more often than it followed anything else.

Believing in a link that the full counts do not show is called an illusory correlation. It grows from vivid cases, which are easy to remember. The cure is to count the dull cases too and compare rates in every group.

Recap

A correlation tells us that two measures vary together, with a sign for the direction and a size for the strength, but it cannot tell us why.

A correlation coefficient r gives the direction and strength of a pattern, and r² gives the share of variation the two measures have in common. Behind a correlation there may be a confound that moves both measures, reverse causation that flips the arrow, or an illusory pattern that memory has built, and in each case comparing rates or holding the suspect factor still shows whether the link survives.

Random assignment

Opener

Random assignment puts chance, not choice, in charge of who joins which group

The cure is to take the choice away from the participants. In an experiment the researchers change one thing themselves and then compare groups, and what makes the comparison fair is random assignment: every participant has an equal chance of landing in any group, decided by something like a coin toss or names drawn from a hat. Now the keen exerciser and the reluctant one are equally likely to end up in either group, so whatever trait we worry about, whether exercise, motivation or money, tends to be spread evenly between them.

That is why a gain of 6 points can't be judged until we know the spread, and the next step turns the idea into a number.

Teaching

Statistical power is the chance that a study finds an effect that is really there

Chance alone produces some gap between any two groups, so researchers ask whether the gap they found is bigger than chance usually makes it. They call a result statistically significant when a gap that large would turn up less than 5% of the time if there were really no effect. That is a signal that the gap is probably not just chance, but it says nothing yet about how big the effect is.

The other side of the question is statistical power, the chance that a study will come out significant when the effect is real. It depends on the size of the effect and on the number of people, and researchers usually aim for 80%. A small study chasing a small effect has low power, so it often misses an effect that exists. When it does find one, the estimate tends to be too big, because among many small studies the ones that reach significance are the ones that happened to overshoot.

Teaching

Perception is an inference from cues that are never perfectly clean

Your senses do not hand you the world. They hand you signals, and each signal is a piece of evidence about the world that is never perfectly clean. Ask your eyes to judge the height of the same block ten times, in slightly different light and from slightly different angles, and the ten answers scatter round some typical value; the spread of that scatter measures how noisy the evidence is. We call any piece of sensory evidence that bears on a property of the world a cue, so the look of the block and the feel of the block are two cues to its height.

Since no cue is certain, working out what is out there means judging what is most likely given the evidence, and we call that process inference. Hermann von Helmholtz proposed in the nineteenth century that perception works in this way, and he called it unconscious inference, because it is finished before we are aware of any result. It feels like seeing, but it is closer to concluding.

Previous text

One sense gives a wobbly reading

The readings gather around the true height, yet no single look is exact. Over many looks they average out near 50 mm and typically stray from that average by about 2 mm.

That typical stray is the standard deviation, or SD, the usual size of a scatter around an average. A small SD means the readings bunch tightly, so one look is nearly as good as any other. A large SD means they are scattered, so any one look could be well off.

Every source of information about the world wobbles in this way and has an SD of its own. Psychologists call such a source a cue. Sight is a cue to the block's height. Touch is another. Because no cue is exact, working out the height is a matter of inference: a best guess drawn from the evidence you have.

The dog and the bell

In the 1890s the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov was studying digestion in dogs when he noticed that they began to drool before any food reached them, at the sight of the person who usually brought it. He turned that observation into an experiment. Food makes a hungry dog drool without any training, and a bell makes it prick up its ears and do little else.

Pavlov rang the bell just before the food arrived, again and again. After enough of these pairings the dog drooled at the bell alone. The bell had become a conditioned stimulus, a signal that has gained its power through experience, and drooling at it was learned. Learning in which a signal comes to predict an outcome through repeated pairing is called classical conditioning.

The pairing matters. A bell that rings at random times, with no link to the food, teaches the dog nothing, because the bell tells it nothing about what comes next. That leaves a question about pace. The first pairing is the biggest surprise to the dog and later ones surprise it less, and a simple rule describes how the learning grows.

Teaching

Each pairing adds to the bell's associative strength, but by less and less

How strongly does the bell predict food after a few pairings? We call the strength of the association associative strength, written V. It runs from 0, where the bell predicts nothing, up to a maximum, which we take as 1, where the bell predicts food completely. In 1972 Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner proposed a rule for how V changes on each trial: V goes up by the rate times the gap that remains, so ΔV = rate × (maximum − V). The idea is that a pairing teaches in proportion to how much there is still to learn.

[Figure]

Take a rate of 0.25 and a start of V = 0. The first pairing adds 0.25 × (1 − 0) = 0.25. The second adds 0.25 × (1 − 0.25) = 0.1875, which makes V = 0.4375, and each pairing after that adds a little less, because the gap that is left keeps shrinking. The figure shows this for two rates: the higher rate climbs faster, but both curves flatten as V approaches the maximum.

How much each pairing teaches

In 1972 Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner proposed that rule. Call the strength of the link between bell and food V, the associative strength. It starts at 0 and can rise towards a maximum, written λ (the Greek letter lambda), which is the most the food can support. With food present, λ = 1.

Each pairing closes a share of the gap between V and λ. The rule is ΔV = rate × (λ − V), where ΔV is the change in V on that trial and the rate says how quickly the animal learns, a number between 0 and 1. Take a rate of 0.25. On the first pairing V is 0, so the gap is 1 and V rises by 0.25 × 1 = 0.25. On the second pairing the gap is 1 − 0.25 = 0.75, so V rises by 0.25 × 0.75 = 0.1875 and reaches 0.4375. When the food stops, the same rule pulls associative strength back down

What happens when the bell keeps sounding and the food stops coming? The same rule applies with one change: with no food the maximum is 0, so the gap, 0 − V, is negative, and V is pulled down by the rate times V. Bell after bell with no food, V falls, and the dog salivates less and less. This weakening of a learned response when the cue is no longer followed by the outcome is called extinction.

When the food stops coming

Suppose the bell keeps ringing after the food has been taken away. The same rule applies, with one change. With no food the most the bell can predict is 0, so λ = 0. If V stands at 0.8 and the rate is 0.25, the gap λ − V is −0.8, which is negative, so V moves down by 0.25 × 0.8 = 0.2 to 0.6. A bell that no longer predicts food loses its power, trial by trial. This fading response is called extinction.

Extinction does not wipe the dog's learning clean. If the bell is rung again after a rest, the drooling often returns for a while, which is called spontaneous recovery. It looks as though extinction adds a second piece of learning, that the bell no longer means food, on top of the first and leaves the first in place. The simple rule treats extinction as unlearning, so it is a good first model and an incomplete one.

Teaching

In operant conditioning, behaviour changes according to what follows it

Classical conditioning concerns what comes before a response, and operant conditioning concerns what comes after it. Around 1900 Edward Thorndike found that a cat in a puzzle box, rewarded with escape and food, took less time on each trial to find the way out, and he stated the law of effect: behaviour followed by a satisfying result becomes more likely. B. F. Skinner later studied it in rats and pigeons, in boxes where pressing a lever or pecking a key produced food, and called the learning operant conditioning because the animal operates on its environment. A consequence either raises or lowers the behaviour: reinforcement makes it more likely, and punishment makes it less likely.

A second distinction says whether something is added or taken away. Positive means a stimulus is added and negative means one is removed, which gives four combinations. A rat given a pellet after pressing a lever presses more, which is positive reinforcement. A person who takes a tablet and finds the headache gone takes tablets more readily, which is negative reinforcement, because something unpleasant was removed and the behaviour rose. A scolding that makes a behaviour rarer is positive punishment, and a fine that takes away money and makes a behaviour rarer is negative punishment. The words do not mean good and bad: negative reinforcement is not a punishment, since the behaviour still rises.

Learning from consequences

The dog has so far learned from what comes before an event: the bell. Now ask what happens to an action that is followed by something. The treat for sitting adds something pleasant and sitting rises, so it is positive reinforcement. A car's seat-belt alarm stops once the belt is fastened, so removing an unpleasant sound makes fastening more likely, which is negative reinforcement. A library fine takes money away and late returns fall, which is negative punishment. Touching a hot hob adds pain and the touching falls, which is positive punishment.

Teaching

A schedule of reinforcement sets which responses are rewarded, and it shapes the behaviour

Rewards seldom follow every response, and a schedule of reinforcement says which responses get one. A ratio schedule rewards after a number of responses, and an interval schedule rewards the first response after a stretch of time has passed. Either can be fixed, the same every time, or variable, changing around an average. In a fixed-ratio schedule, such as FR 4, every fourth response is rewarded. In a variable-ratio schedule the reward comes after an unpredictable number of responses that averages, say, four. In a fixed-interval schedule, such as FI 10 s, the first response at least 10 seconds after the last reward is rewarded.

[Figure]

The figure sets a fixed ratio of 4 beside a variable ratio with the same average. In their studies of pigeons and rats, published in 1957, Charles Ferster and B. F. Skinner found that the schedules leave different patterns. A fixed ratio gives bursts of fast responding with a pause after each reward, and a variable ratio gives fast, steady responding that is the hardest to extinguish. A fixed interval gives a pause after each reward and then responding that speeds up as the next one nears.

How often the rewards arrive

A trainer must also decide how often to give the treat. The rule for when a reward is given is a schedule of reinforcement. Some schedules depend on how many responses the animal makes, and some depend on how much time has passed.

A fixed-ratio schedule gives a reward after a set number of responses, such as every fourth peck. Under a variable-ratio schedule the number changes unpredictably around an average, so the reward might follow 2 pecks, then 7, then 3. A fixed-interval schedule rewards the first response after a set time has passed, so the reward lands on a response and responses during the wait earn nothing. A variable-interval schedule makes the wait unpredictable. The figure records responses over time for a fixed ratio of 4 and a variable ratio that averages 4.

The two pay out about equally often, but they differ in what happens when the rewards stop. On a fixed schedule the animal soon sees that the pattern has broken and gives up. On a variable-ratio schedule it cannot tell a long gap from the end of the rewards, so it keeps responding for far longer. An angler whose fish bite after an unpredictable number of casts knows the effect.

Recap

Learning follows prediction and consequence: each pairing adds less than the last, a response weakens when its outcome stops, and the schedule of rewards shapes what an animal does.

In classical conditioning a cue gains associative strength by the rule ΔV = rate × (maximum − V), so each pairing adds less than the one before, and a cue alone pulls V back towards 0 in extinction. In operant conditioning a consequence makes a behaviour more or less likely, and the schedule of rewards, fixed or variable, ratio or interval, shapes the pattern of responding.

Teaching

Ignoring how common the condition is leads to confident mistakes

Return to the first guess. Answering '90 in 100' treats the test's hit rate as though it were the chance that a positive result is right, and in doing so it ignores how common the disease is to begin with. Psychologists call this base-rate neglect.

It is not only an error of arithmetic. In studies that Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described in 1974, people were told that a man had been drawn from a group of 100 professionals, 30 engineers and 70 lawyers, and were given a short description of his personality. They judged the chance that he was an engineer by how well the description fitted an engineer, and gave nearly the same answer when they were told that the group held 70 engineers and 30 lawyers. How many of each there were should have mattered a great deal, and it hardly seemed to matter at all. Anchoring is one of several heuristics, which are quick rules of thumb that the mind uses to judge in a hurry. The careful reading is that obedience to authority is real and weakens with closeness. The exact percentages depend on the setting and on what volunteers believed was happening, so a figure like 65% belongs to one setting and should be quoted with it.

Diffusion of responsibility: the more people who could help, the less each may feel it is theirs to do

In 1964 a New York newspaper reported that 38 people had watched the murder of Kitty Genovese and none had called the police. The story prompted John Darley and Bibb Latané to ask why a person might not help when others are present. Later research, notably by Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins in 2007, found that the 'parable of the 38 witnesses' had been exaggerated: few people had seen much of the attack or understood what was happening, and some neighbours did call the police.

Darley and Latané's own experiment, published in 1968, had students talk over an intercom, each in a separate booth, and hear another student seem to have a seizure. Each student believed they were alone with the victim, or that one other person or four others could also hear. The share who left their booth to report it before the seizure ended fell as the group grew: 85% when alone, 62% with one other, and 31% with four others. Darley and Latané called the effect diffusion of responsibility: each person feels less responsible when others could act.

Why a crowd can mean less help

Back on the platform, a crowd has two effects that pull in opposite directions. More people means more chances that somebody helps. It can also make each person less likely to help, because everyone can see that others could act and responsibility feels shared out. Psychologists call that diffusion of responsibility.

John Darley and Bibb Latané tested it in 1968. Volunteers joined an intercom discussion, each alone in a separate room. One speaker seemed to have a seizure. Volunteers who believed they were the only listener helped 85% of the time. Volunteers who believed four others were listening helped 31% of the time. Every volunteer was alone in a room, so the difference came from what they believed about the other listeners.

The chance that somebody acts

Suppose each person in a crowd decides independently, which means one person's choice does not change another's. The chance that at least one of them acts is easiest to find through the opposite event, that nobody acts. If each of three people acts with probability 0.3, each fails to act with probability 0.7.

Nobody acts only if all three fail, and for independent people you multiply their chances: 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 = 0.343. So the chance that at least one acts is 1 − 0.343 = 0.657, or 65.7%. For n people who each act with probability p, the rule is 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ.

A lone witness with probability 0.85 beats three witnesses at 0.3 each, whose group chance is 65.7%. A larger crowd gives more chances, but the group does better only if each person's own chance does not fall too far.

Laboratory results like these fed a famous story. In 1964 a woman named Kitty Genovese was killed in New York, and a newspaper reported that 38 neighbours watched and did nothing. The story was used for decades to illustrate the bystander effect. The figure of 38 silent witnesses has since been shown to be largely a myth, and some neighbours did call the police.

Recap

The classic studies show real pulls towards the group, authority and the crowd, but their numbers depend on the setup, and later work has qualified each of them.

Conformity, obedience and helping can all be read as rates, compared across conditions in percentage points, and the chance that at least one of n bystanders acts is 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ. Asch's majority moved many people at least once but not most answers, Milgram's rates fell as the victim came closer, and replications and archives have shown that each figure depends on how the study was run.
