About this course

Astronomy is the study of the Sun, the Moon, the planets, the stars and the galaxies beyond them, and it began with practical needs, since the first astronomers kept calendars by the Sun and the Moon and found their way across the sea by the stars. It is pursued today to learn what the universe is made of, how it has changed and how old it is, and it is unusual among the sciences in that almost everything it knows has come from light, because most of what it studies is too far away to be visited or handled.

The course starts with what can be seen from a garden. The stars wheel round the sky through the night, the Sun climbs higher in summer than in winter, and the Moon changes shape over a month, and a little geometry explains each of them. It then moves outward to the orbits of the planets and the gravity that governs them, to what the brightness, colour and spectrum of starlight reveal about a star’s distance, temperature, size and motion, and last to the lives of stars and the expanding universe, ending with an estimate of the age of the universe itself.

Every lesson works with real measurements, and each result is one that you can check yourself with a calculator and the numbers given.

  • You’ll be able to predict how high the Sun or a star climbs from where you live, and which stars never set.
  • You’ll be able to explain the seasons, the phases of the Moon and why eclipses are rare.
  • You’ll be able to work out how long a planet takes to orbit the Sun and how fast a spacecraft must go to escape a planet.
  • You’ll be able to read a star’s temperature, distance and speed from its light.
  • You’ll be able to use the motion of distant galaxies to estimate the age of the universe.

The turning sky

Opener

Leave a camera pointing at the northern sky with the shutter open for a few hours, and every star comes out as a short arc of a circle, all of the arcs curving about one point. Stars close to that point draw small circles and stars far from it draw large ones, but none of them behaves differently from the rest, as though the whole sky were a single wheel turning about a fixed hub.

The wheel goes once round in about a day, and where its hub sits depends on where you stand: from Amsterdam it is a little over halfway from the horizon to the point overhead, and from the North Pole it is directly above you. Working out how the hub’s height follows from your position on Earth gives two practical results, a way to find how far north you are from a single star and a way to say in advance which stars never set.

By the end you’ll be able to work out how high the sky’s hub stands from your latitude, and which stars never set.

The sky turns because Earth does

The turning of the sky is Earth’s own motion seen from the inside. Earth spins once on its axis and carries you round with it, while the stars are so far away that their directions in space hardly change in a night, so what changes is the way you are facing.

Measured against the stars, one turn takes 23 hours 56 minutes, which is four minutes less than the 24 hours from one noon to the next, so a star rises about four minutes earlier each night. The sky turns because Earth does

The cause is closer to home than the stars. Earth spins once a day, and you spin with it. Because you share the motion you don’t feel it, just as you don’t feel a train gliding along a smooth, straight track. What you notice is the world outside sliding the other way. From a train window the trees slide backwards while the train carries you forwards.

The sky does the same thing. Earth carries you round, and the stars seem to rise in the east, climb and set in the west. One full turn is 360 degrees, and Earth takes about 24 hours to make it.

Picture Earth from high above the North Pole, with you standing on the equator. Draw a dashed line from you to one distant star. It keeps the same direction no matter how far Earth turns, because the star is so remote that moving round Earth’s rim makes no visible difference to where it lies.

Follow the star from your point of view. It begins just above the horizon and climbs until it passes straight overhead, then sinks towards the opposite horizon. At last Earth’s own bulk comes between you and the star, and it has set. It stays hidden for about twelve hours before you are carried round far enough to see it rise again.

Extend that axis far out into space in both directions and it meets the sky at two points, the north celestial pole above the North Pole and the south celestial pole above the South Pole. Two angles: latitude on Earth, altitude in the sky

To say how far north you are, geographers use latitude, the angle at the centre of Earth between the equator and the line from the centre to you. It is 0° on the equator, 90° at the North Pole and about 52° in Amsterdam, and since Earth’s radius is 6,371 km each degree corresponds to about 111 km along the ground, which puts Amsterdam some 5,800 km north of the equator.

To say how high something is in the sky, astronomers use altitude, the angle measured at your feet from the horizon up to the object. A star on the horizon has an altitude of 0°, the zenith has an altitude of 90°, and a star halfway up the sky stands at 45°.

Two measures: latitude and altitude

The first measure is latitude. It is the angle, taken at Earth’s centre, between the equator and the line out to where you stand. The equator is at 0°, the North Pole is at 90° N, and Amsterdam, which this lesson uses as its example, is at about 52° N. Astronomers write latitude as φ, the Greek letter phi.

A degree of latitude is about 111 km of ground, so the walk from the equator to Amsterdam is roughly 5,800 km.

The second measure is altitude, the angle between the horizon and whatever you are looking at. Something sitting on the horizon has an altitude of 0°. Something straight overhead, at the zenith, has an altitude of 90°. Everything else falls in between.

Because altitude is an angle, it says nothing about distance. A star and an aircraft in the same direction have the same altitude. You can estimate it with your own hand: held at arm’s length, a fist covers about 10° of sky, so a star two fists above the horizon is at roughly 20°.

Now the walk can be put into numbers. On the equator, at latitude 0°, the north celestial pole stands at altitude 0°. At the North Pole, at latitude 90°, it stands at altitude 90°. What remains is everything in between.

Declination, and the size of a star’s circle

So far we have followed the hub of the wheel. The stars that circle it need an address too, and astronomers borrow the idea from the map. Extend Earth’s equator out into space and you get the celestial equator, a circle in the sky halfway between the two celestial poles.

Declination, written δ (delta), is a star’s latitude on that sky: the angle between the celestial equator and the star, measured at Earth’s centre. A star on the celestial equator has declination 0°. A star at the north celestial pole has +90°, and one at the south celestial pole −90°. Stars north of the celestial equator carry a plus sign and stars south of it a minus sign.

A star keeps the same declination as Earth turns, which makes it a dependable address. The first three pictures show how declination is measured, and the last two show the circles that stars at different declinations trace round the pole.

Declination also tells you how big a star’s circle is. A star at declination δ lies 90° − δ from the north celestial pole, and as Earth turns it carries the star round the sky in a circle about the pole with exactly that radius.

Compare two stars. A star at declination +70° is 20° from the pole and traces a small circle. A star at declination 0° is 90° from the pole and traces the largest circle of all. Both complete their circle in the same 24 hours, so the second star has much further to travel and moves faster across the sky, the way a child at the edge of a roundabout covers more ground than one near the middle.

Why the seasons change

Opener

Push a straight stick upright into level ground in London and measure its shadow at noon on the longest day of the year in June, and the shadow comes out at roughly half the length of the stick. Measure it again at noon in December and it is nearly four times as long as the stick, so the winter shadow is about seven times the length of the summer one.

Nothing about the stick has changed, and the Sun is no nearer or farther in any way that matters, so the difference has to lie in the direction from which its light arrives. Following the Sun’s path through the year with the angles of the first lesson shows that the Sun’s declination, unlike a star’s, is not fixed but swings between two limits, and that this swing is what produces the seasons.

By the end you’ll be able to explain the seasons from Earth’s tilt and work out the Sun’s noon height for any latitude and date.

Earth’s axis is tilted, and it keeps pointing the same way

Opener

Temperature, colour and motion

Opener

A poker left in a fire glows a dull red, then orange, and if it could be heated far enough it would turn yellow-white and then blue-white. Its colour acts as a thermometer, since the hotter the metal, the bluer its light, and the same is true of a star, so that the colour of a star’s light gives the temperature of its surface without any instrument touching it.

Starlight carries a second kind of information. Teaching

A hot body glows, and its colour depends on its temperature

Light is a wave, and its colour is set by its wavelength, the distance from one crest to the next, which for visible light runs from about 400 nm for violet to about 700 nm for red, where one nanometre is a billionth of a metre. A hot, dense body such as a poker or a star gives out light across a whole range of wavelengths, and the shape of that range depends on nothing but its temperature. A body that behaves in this way is called a blackbody.

A blackbody spectrum rises to a maximum and falls away on either side, and the wavelength at the maximum is the peak wavelength. The hotter the body, the shorter its peak wavelength, which is why the poker moves from red towards white as it heats and why the colour of a star tells us how hot it is.

Colour and temperature

Anything hot enough glows, and the light it gives off is a mix of wavelengths. The wavelength of light decides its colour: red light has a long wavelength and blue light a short one. We measure wavelengths in nanometres (nm), a billionth of a metre, and visible light runs from about 400 nm for violet to about 700 nm for red.

A glowing object does not give out all wavelengths equally. Its light has a peak, one wavelength at which it shines most strongly, and the rest tails away on either side. An object that glows only because it is hot is called a blackbody. Stars behave almost like blackbodies.

The peak moves with temperature, as it did in the poker. A hotter object has a peak at a shorter wavelength, so it looks bluer. A cooler one peaks at a longer wavelength and looks redder. The Sun’s peak is in the blue-green, but it also gives out plenty at every other visible wavelength, and the mix looks white.

Spectral lines are fixed, and motion shifts them

Starlight carries more than a colour. When it is spread into a spectrum, narrow dark or bright spectral lines appear at particular wavelengthsThe distance from one wave crest to the next; it sets the colour of light. Met earlier in this lesson., because each kind of atom absorbs and emits light only at wavelengths that belong to it. Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen showed in 1859 that each chemical element makes its own pattern of lines, so a pattern in a star identifies the elements in it, and the line of hydrogen at 656.28 nm is one of the most familiar.

The pattern is fixed in the laboratory but not in the sky. When a star moves away from us its light waves are stretched, and every line shifts to a longer wavelength, which is called a redshift. When a star moves towards us the waves are squeezed, and the lines shift to a shorter wavelength, a blueshift. Christian Doppler proposed the effect in 1842.

Lines at fixed wavelengths

Starlight carries a second message. Pass it through a prism and spread it into a rainbow, called a spectrum, and thin dark or bright spectral lines appear at particular wavelengths. Each kind of atom makes lines at its own wavelengths, which can be measured in a laboratory. Hydrogen has one at 656.28 nm, in the red. The lines are like fingerprints that name the atoms in a star.

Now suppose the star is moving away from us. Each wave is sent out from a point a little farther off than the wave before, so the waves reach us stretched. Every line moves to a longer wavelength. If the star is coming towards us the waves are squeezed, and the lines move to shorter wavelengths.

A shift to longer wavelengths is a redshift, and a shift to shorter ones is a blueshift. It is the same effect as a siren dropping in pitch once the ambulance has passed. The faster the star moves, the bigger the stretch, and what counts is the stretch as a fraction of the original wavelength.

A star shines from its surface, so its power is area times power per square metre

A star is a glowing ball, and the light it gives out leaves through its surface. Each square metre of a surface at temperature T radiates a power of σT⁴, where σ = 5.670 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, a law that Josef Stefan found from experiment in 1879 and Ludwig Boltzmann derived in 1884. A sphere of radius R has a surface area of 4πR², so the luminosityThe total power a star radiates, in watts or in solar luminosities. Met in “Brightness and distance”. is L = 4πR² × σT⁴.

The Sun gives a check. At T = 5,772 K each square metre radiates 5.670 × 10⁻⁸ × 5,772⁴ = 6.29 × 10⁷ W, and its radius of 6.957 × 10⁸ m gives an area of 4π × (6.957 × 10⁸)² = 6.08 × 10¹⁸ m², so its luminosity is 6.29 × 10⁷ × 6.08 × 10¹⁸ = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W, which agrees with the 3.828 × 10²⁶ W found from its flux. How mass sets a star’s life

Opener

The Sun has been on the main sequence for about 4.6 billion years and has fuel for roughly as long again, which makes it a middle-aged star. Other stars live on a very different scale: the most massive burn through their fuel in a few million years, while the least massive will still be shining when the universe is hundreds of times its present age. All of them are made of the same hydrogen, so something about their masses must set their lifetimes.

The surprise is that the stars with the most fuel die first. This lesson follows that puzzle with two rules of thumb, one for how luminosity depends on mass and one for how long a star lasts, and then asks what is left when the fuel is gone, which also depends on mass.

By the end you’ll be able to estimate a star’s lifetime from its mass and say how stars of different masses end.

A star lasts for its fuel divided by how fast it burns it

A main-sequence star shines because fusion in its core joins hydrogen nuclei into helium, and 0.7% of the mass of the hydrogen fused is released as energy, in accordance with E = mc². The Sun’s luminosity of 3.828 × 10²⁶ W therefore requires 3.828 × 10²⁶ ÷ (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 4.3 × 10⁹ kg of mass to be converted each second, which means fusing about 6 × 10¹¹ kg of hydrogen every second.
