About this course

Geometry begins with questions that anyone who has built, painted or packed something has asked: how much turn there is in a corner, how far it is round a field, how many tiles a floor needs and how large a box must be to hold what goes in it. The word comes from the Greek for measuring the earth, and the subject has kept that practical character, since every plan, map and package carries lengths, angles and areas that have to come out right.

The course has 12 lessons in four parts. Angles and measurement starts from the idea that an angle is an amount of turn, uses it to find the angles of a triangle, and separates the distance round a rectangle from the space inside it. Inside the shape finds the areas of triangles, of shapes with a piece cut away or added on, and of circles. Shape and scale asks what happens to lengths and to areas when a shape is enlarged, and ends with the rule that connects the three sides of a right triangle. From a plan to a solid uses coordinates to measure distance on a grid, and then moves into three dimensions to find the volume of a box and the paper that would cover it.

Each lesson develops one idea over a few short screens, explaining it first and asking a question only once you have been shown what the question needs. Where a result has a known history, the lesson says whose it is, and several of them go back to Euclid's Elements, written about 300 BCE.

  • You'll be able to find a missing angle in a corner, on a straight line, round a point and inside a triangle.
  • You'll be able to tell the distance round a shape from the space inside it, and work out both for rectangles.
  • You'll be able to find the area of a triangle, a parallelogram, a trapezoid and a shape with a corner cut away.
  • You'll be able to say how a shape's lengths, areas and volumes change when it is enlarged.
  • You'll be able to find the distance between two points on a grid and the volume and surface area of a box.

Measuring turns

Opener

Stand in the middle of a room facing the door and turn until you face the wall on your right. You have made a quarter of a full turn; a second turn of the same size leaves your back to the door, and two further turns, four in all, bring you round to face the door again.

Geometry measures a corner in exactly this way, as an amount of turn, and that is what makes a missing measurement easy to find. A corner, a straight edge and the point where several lines meet each hold a fixed amount of turn, so a part of one that is missing is whatever the known parts leave over.

By the end you'll be able to find an unknown angle in a corner, on a straight line and round a point.

Nothing in this counting depends on the size of the room, because a turn is a fraction of the way round and not a distance. A half-turn is always worth two quarter-turns, and four quarter-turns always make a whole turn, so any turn built from quarters can be followed simply by counting them.

A whole turn is 360 degrees

Quarter-turns are enough for a turn that ends at a wall, but most turns end somewhere in between, so a whole turn is divided into 360 equal parts and each part is called a degree, written 1°. The figure shows how small one degree is, and how the half-turn and the whole turn compare with it.

[Figure]

Since a whole turn is 360°, a half-turn is half of that, 180°, and a quarter-turn is a quarter of it. The number 360 is generally traced to Babylonian astronomers, whose number system counted in sixties, and it has lasted because it divides exactly by every whole number from 1 to 10 except 7, so that most simple fractions of a turn come out as whole degrees.

The amount of turn between two straight lines that start at the same point is called an angle, and the two lines are its arms. An angle that is a quarter of a whole turn is a right angle, the square corner of a page or a door frame, and a diagram marks it with a small square.

The angles in a corner add up to 90°

A right angle is a quarter of a whole turn, 90°, and a line drawn out of the corner splits it into two smaller angles. Together they still fill the corner, so the two angles add up to 90°. The figure shows a corner with one part marked and the other left as a question mark.

That makes the unknown part the difference between 90° and the known one. If the marked part is 25°, the rest is 90° − 25° = 65°, and if the marked part is 70°, the rest is 90° − 70° = 20°. The same subtraction works for any split, because it uses only the total, and the total belongs to the corner and not to the way it is cut.

Splitting a corner

A right angle measures 90°. Now cut one in two. Draw a line from the corner of a page out across the page, and the single right angle becomes two smaller angles side by side. Together they fill the corner exactly as before, so their sizes must add up to 90°.

That gives a way to find a missing angle with one subtraction. If you know the whole and one part, the other part is what remains. Say one part is 25°. The other is 90° − 25° = 65°. The figure shows two corners, each with one part marked. Switch between them and see how the leftover part changes.

Angles along a straight line add up to 180°

A straight line is a half-turn: stand facing along it, turn until you face the opposite way, and you have turned through 180°. A line drawn up from any point on it makes two angles that sit side by side, and since together they take up the half-turn, they add up to 180°. The half-turn between the two ends of the line is called a straight angle.

Angles round a point add up to 360°

When several lines meet at a single point, the angles between them go all the way round, and the full circuit is a whole turn, so the angles round a point add up to 360°. This is the total to use whenever the angles close up with no gap, as they do at the centre of a clock face.

[Figure]

The figure shows 200° of the turn made and the rest still to come. The two together make 360°, so the rest is 360° − 200° = 160°. A point with three or more angles round it works the same way: add up the angles you know and take the sum from 360°.

The three totals are one idea. A corner holds a quarter-turn, a straight line a half-turn and a point a whole turn, which are 90°, 180° and 360°, and in each case the unknown angle is what the known angles leave over.

An angle does not depend on how long its arms are

Draw the two arms of an angle longer and nothing about the turn between them changes. In the figure both angles are right angles, one drawn with long arms and one with short arms, and they cover the same quarter-turn because the arms point in the same directions.

[Figure]

A degree measures how far one arm has turned from the other, not how far the arms reach across the page, so an angle is fixed by the turn between its arms. Drawing an angle larger makes the picture bigger and leaves the angle as it was, which is why an angle whose arms are too short to reach the scale of a protractor can have them extended before it is measured.

Long arms, same turn

One more point about angles before the check. Compare the minute hand of a clock on a church tower with the minute hand of a wristwatch. From 12 to 3 each hand turns through a right angle, although one is a metre long and the other a few millimetres.

Two equal angles share what the third leaves

A triangle can have two sides of equal length, and it is then called an isosceles triangle. The angles opposite those two sides are equal as well, a fact that Euclid proved as Proposition 5 of Book I, so an isosceles triangle has a pair of equal angles and a third angle that differs from them.

Which sets of angles are possible

Any three positive numbers can be written down as angles, but only some sets are the corners of a real triangle. The test is the angle sum. If the three add up to exactly 180°, a triangle with those angles can be drawn. If they add up to more or to less, no triangle has those angles, because the torn corners would either run past the straight edge or leave a gap.

Tiling the floor

Take the tiling first. A tile is a square and every tile is the same size, so tiles laid edge to edge, with no gaps and no overlaps, cover a floor completely. The number of tiles it takes measures how much floor there is. That amount is called the area of the floor, and it is counted in unit squares. If each tile is 1 metre across, a tile is a square metre, written 1 m², and a floor that takes 12 tiles has an area of 12 m².

Counting tiles one at a time is slow. Tiles lie in rows, and every row has the same number of tiles. A floor with 4 rows of 8 tiles holds 8 + 8 + 8 + 8 = 32 tiles, which is 4 × 8. So the area of a rectangle is the number of rows times the number of tiles in each row, and that is the same as its length times its width.

The small 2 in m² records that the unit is a square with a metre along each side.

The space inside a circle is a different kind of measurement from the distance round it. The boundary is a length and so involves a length once, but an area involves a length twice, once in each direction, as the area of a square does. Working back to the height

So far you have been given the box and asked for its volume. Sometimes it goes the other way: you know how much a box holds and one measurement is missing. The multiplication can be run backwards. Take a box that holds 96 cm³ with a base 8 cm by 3 cm, and suppose its height is the missing piece.

Each layer holds 8 × 3 = 24 cubes, so the question is how many layers of 24 cubes make 96. Fitting equal groups into a total is division: 96 ÷ 24 = 4. The box has 4 layers, so it is 4 cm high.

Check by multiplying forwards: 24 × 4 = 96, the volume you started with. Divide the volume by the base area to get the height, and multiply the base area by the height to get the volume back. Each step undoes the other.

The volume of a cube

A cube is a box whose length, width and height are all equal, so one number, its side, describes it. Take a cube with sides 3 cm. Its bottom layer is 3 × 3 = 9 cubes and there are 3 layers, so its volume is 9 × 3 = 27 cm³. In short that is side × side × side, written 3³ and read as three cubed.

Cubes of different sizes show how quickly the volume grows. A cube with side 1 cm holds 1 cm³, and one with side 2 cm holds 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 cm³. Doubling the side makes room for eight times as many unit cubes, because the layer grows in two directions and the number of layers grows as well.

For a cube with side s, the volume is s × s × s, whatever the size.

Three pairs of faces

A box that is longer than it is wide has faces of different sizes, and the matching pairs still help. Take a pencil case 10 cm long, 4 cm wide and 3 cm high. The top and the bottom are each 10 × 4 = 40 cm². The front and the back are each 10 × 3 = 30 cm². The two ends are each 4 × 3 = 12 cm².

Only three different faces appear, and each one appears twice. Add the three areas to get 40 + 30 + 12 = 82 cm². Then double that total to cover the matching faces: 2 × 82 = 164 cm². The surface area of the pencil case is 164 cm².

As a rule, surface area = 2 × (length × width + length × height + width × height). Doubling once at the end is quicker than adding six areas, and it makes it harder to forget a face.

A box with no lid

Plenty of boxes have no lid, such as a shoebox with its lid off, a fish tank or a bin. The paper or glass only has to cover the faces that are there. So find the surface area of the closed box first, then take away the area of the face that is missing.

Surface area grows with the square of the scale factor, volume with its cube

The same box shows how the covering and the space inside grow when every length is enlarged. The box in the figure measures 2 by 1 by 1, with faces of 2, 2 and 1, so its surface area is 2 × (2 + 2 + 1) = 10 square units and its volume is 2 × 1 × 1 = 2 cubic units. The second view triples every length.

[Figure]

The tripled box is 6 by 3 by 3, with faces of 18, 18 and 9, so its surface area is 2 × (18 + 18 + 9) = 90, which is 9 times as much, and its volume is 6 × 3 × 3 = 54, which is 27 times as much. Surface area grows by the square of the scale factor and volume by its cube, since an area contains two lengths and a volume three, and 3² = 9 while 3³ = 27. Galileo drew the same conclusion for living things in his Two New Sciences of 1638, arguing that a larger animal needs disproportionately thick bones, because its weight grows with its volume while the strength of a bone grows only with its cross-section.

A bigger present

Suppose the same present comes in two sizes of one shape, and every length of the bigger box is double the smaller one's. Take a small box 2 cm long, 1 cm wide and 1 cm high. Its three different faces are 2, 2 and 1 cm², so its surface area is 2 × (2 + 2 + 1) = 10 cm². Its volume is 2 × 1 × 1 = 2 cm³.

Double every length to get a box 4 cm long, 2 cm wide and 2 cm high. Its faces are now 8, 8 and 4 cm², so the surface area is 2 × (8 + 8 + 4) = 40 cm², which is four times as much. The volume is 4 × 2 × 2 = 16 cm³, which is eight times as much.

The reason is how many lengths are multiplied. An area multiplies two lengths, and doubling both makes it 2 × 2 = 4 times as big, as with the tiles earlier in the course. A volume multiplies three lengths, so doubling all three gives 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. In general, area goes up by the scale factor squared and volume by the scale factor cubed.
