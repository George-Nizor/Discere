About this course

Linear algebra studies lists of numbers, called vectors, and the tables of numbers, called matrices, that act on them. A vector such as (4, 1) can describe a position, a displacement or a force, and a matrix can stretch, turn or flatten every vector in the plane at once, so that a single table of four numbers describes what happens to infinitely many arrows. The same few ideas run through computer graphics, physics, statistics and machine learning, which is why the subject is the standard language for reasoning about many numbers together.

The course has twenty lessons in five parts of four. The first part works with vectors, adding and scaling them and using the dot product to measure length and direction. The second builds linear maps from matrices, composes them and undoes them. The third solves systems of equations by row reduction and describes the spaces that their solutions fill, and the fourth measures the structure of a matrix through rank, determinants, projections and the fitting of a line to noisy measurements. The last part builds orthonormal bases and separates a matrix into independent stretches, with eigenvectors and the singular value decomposition.

Each lesson explains first and asks afterwards, so a question comes only once you have been shown what it needs.

  • You'll be able to read a vector, add and scale vectors, and find a length or a dot product.
  • You'll be able to apply a matrix to a vector and say what it does to the plane.
  • You'll be able to solve a small system of equations by row reduction and say how many solutions it has.
  • You'll be able to find a determinant, a projection and a least-squares fit for small examples.
  • You'll be able to find the eigenvectors of a 2 × 2 matrix and read what a singular value decomposition reports.

Read a vector

Opener

The pair (4, 1) can be read as a place: 4 units along from a starting corner and then 1 unit up. Drawn on a grid, it is a route of two straight stretches that ends at a single point, and it is the simplest object in this course, because nearly everything that follows combines, stretches or transforms pairs like it.

Directions as a pair of numbers

Start with the café, 4 blocks east and 1 block north of where you stand. On a map east runs across the page and north runs up it, so the café's directions are 4 across and 1 up. Every direction of this kind has the same two parts, which means we can drop the words and keep the numbers: (4, 1). The first number says how far across to go and the second says how far up.

Each number in the pair is called a coordinate. We name them with letters: the across number is x and the up number is y, so in (4, 1) we have x = 4 and y = 1. A pair of numbers written in a fixed order like this is called a vector. You can picture it as an arrow that starts where you stand and ends at the café.

A minus sign sends the arrow the other way

Coordinates are not always positive. A negative first coordinate means going to the left instead of the right, and a negative second coordinate means going down instead of up. The vector (−3, 2) therefore runs 3 to the left and 2 up, while (5, −1) runs 5 to the right and 1 down, and the explorer shows both.

Going left and down

So far every number has been positive, because the café lies east and north of you. Places do not always lie that way. A kiosk might be 3 blocks west of you and 2 blocks north. Why the order matters

Take the pairs (2, 6) and (6, 2). They use the same two numbers, yet (2, 6) means 2 across and 6 up, and (6, 2) means 6 across and 2 up. One ends high above the starting point and the other ends far along to the side. Teaching

Adding vectors adds their matching coordinates

Take the moves (3, 1) and (2, 2). The first goes 3 across and 1 up, and the second then goes a further 2 across and 2 up, so the total distance across is 3 + 2 = 5 and the total distance up is 1 + 2 = 3. The single move that does the work of both is (5, 3), which is called the sum of the two vectors and is written (3, 1) + (2, 2) = (5, 3). Adding vectors means adding the matching coordinates, the first with the first and the second with the second.

The explorer draws the two moves end to end, with the sum as the arrow from the start to the finish. The second case changes the second move to (−2, 2), which sends the path back towards the left.

[Figure]

A negative coordinate simply subtracts. Across the whole path of the second case, 3 + (−2) = 1, so the path finishes only 1 across, even though the first move alone went 3. Ordinary addition does not depend on order, and neither does this: doing (2, 2) first and (3, 1) second would finish at the same point, (5, 3).

A difference is best read as end minus start: the move from one point to another has the coordinates of where it finishes less those of where it began.

Scaling and adding together

Real journeys mix both operations. A courier might make the leg (1, 3) twice and then add a final stretch of (4, −2). Written as one expression, that is 2(1, 3) + (4, −2). Ordinary arithmetic decides the order: the scaling is done first, and then the vectors are added.

Teaching

The length of a vector comes from Pythagoras's theorem

Take the vector (8, 15). Its two coordinates are the sides of a right-angled triangle, with a horizontal side of 8 and a vertical side of 15, and the arrow itself is the long side opposite the right angle. Pythagoras's theorem, which Euclid proved in Book I of the Elements, says that the square on the long side equals the sum of the squares on the other two, so the arrow's length is the square root of 8² + 15² = 64 + 225 = 289, which is 17.

That number is called the length of the vector, and it is written with double bars, so that in general the vector (a, b) has length:

A product exists only when the inner shapes match

A row of A can be dotted with a column of B only if the two have the same number of entries. A 2 × 2 matrix has an inverse given by a short formula

For a general 2 × 2 matrix A with rows (a, b) and (c, d), the inverse can be written down directly:

A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}

The recipe is to swap the two diagonal entries, a and d, to change the signs of the other two, b and c, and to divide every entry by the number ad − bc, which is called the determinant of A. Multiplying A by the swapped matrix gives ad − bc in both diagonal places and zeros elsewhere, so dividing by ad − bc turns that product into I.

The division is the one place where the recipe can fail. When ad − bc is zero there is nothing to divide by, and no inverse exists. The determinant will come back in a later lesson with a geometric meaning, which explains why zero is the dividing line.

A general matrix with rows (a, b) and (c, d) mixes the across and up values, so its inverse needs more than reciprocals. There is a short recipe. Two equations in two unknowns, such as x₁ + x₂ = 7 and x₁ − x₂ = 1, can be pictured as two straight lines, and a pair of numbers satisfies both equations exactly when it is a point on both lines. The figure draws the two lines, and they cross at a single point, whose coordinates are the answer.

Reading an answer from a graph is slow and inexact, and it breaks down altogether once there are three or more unknowns. This lesson replaces it with a short list of exact steps that turns any system into one whose answer can be read off, and unlike the inverse of the previous lesson, the steps work whether or not the matrix has an inverse.

By the end you'll solve a system of equations with row operations and check the answer by substituting it back.

Teaching

Adding two equations can make an unknown vanish

Take x₁ + x₂ = 7 and x₁ − x₂ = 1. The unknown x₂ has opposite signs in the two equations, so adding them removes it: (x₁ + x₂) + (x₁ − x₂) = 7 + 1 leaves 2x₁ = 8, and x₁ = 4. Putting that into the first equation gives 4 + x₂ = 7, so x₂ = 3, and both original equations hold, since 4 − 3 = 1.

Adding is allowed because equals added to equals are equal. Each equation says that its two sides are the same quantity, so adding the left sides and adding the right sides gives another true equation. The addition has turned a problem with two unknowns into one with a single unknown. The explorer shows this system and a second one, with 2x₂ and −2x₂, which the same addition solves in one step.

Making an unknown vanish

Equations that describe the same situation form a system of equations. Each equation is a fact about the unknowns, the quantities you are trying to find, and a solution is a set of values that makes every equation true at once.

Systems give way to one idea. Suppose a ticket and a snack cost £7 together, and a ticket with three snacks costs £13. The second purchase is the first plus two more snacks, and it costs £6 more. So two snacks cost £6 and one snack costs £3. That makes the ticket £4. Subtracting one equation from the other made the ticket drop out, leaving a fact about snacks alone.

That is the whole method in miniature. Combine the equations so that one unknown disappears, and solve for the one that remains. Then put its value back into an equation to find the other.

A multiple of one equation can be added to another

Adding does not always remove an unknown. In x₁ + x₂ = 5 and 2x₁ + 3x₂ = 12 no unknown has opposite coefficients, but two copies of the first equation, 2x₁ + 2x₂ = 10, can be subtracted from the second. That leaves x₂ = 2, and then x₁ = 5 − 2 = 3, which satisfies both equations, since 2 × 3 + 3 × 2 = 12.

The general step is to add a multiple of one equation to another, choosing the multiple so that an unknown cancels. Here the multiple was −2, so the second equation was replaced by itself plus −2 times the first. The pair of equations after the step, x₁ + x₂ = 5 and x₂ = 2, has exactly the same solutions as the pair before it, and it is far easier to read.

Teaching

A system is recorded as an augmented matrix, and the steps become row operations

Writing x₁ and x₂ in every line is wasteful, because only the coefficients and the right-hand sides change. A system is therefore recorded as a matrix with one row for each equation, one column for each unknown, and a last column, set off by a bar, for the right-hand sides. This is the augmented matrix of the system, and for x₁ + x₂ = 5 and 2x₁ − x₂ = 1 it is:

\left(\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 1 & 5 \\ 2 & -1 & 1\end{array}\right)

The step used above, adding a multiple of one equation to another, becomes a row operation. There are three, and each leaves the solutions of the system unchanged: swap two rows, multiply a row by a nonzero number, or add a multiple of one row to another. The notation R₂ ← R₂ − 2R₁ reads "replace row 2 by row 2 minus twice row 1".

A row operation changes a whole row, including its right-hand entry, because a row stands for an equation, and both sides of an equation must change together. Each operation can also be reversed, which is why none of them can gain or lose a solution: a swap undoes itself, scaling by a nonzero number is undone by scaling by its reciprocal, and adding a multiple of a row is undone by subtracting it.

Writing a system as a matrix

Writing every x₁ and x₂ gets heavy as systems grow. The names of the unknowns carry no information, so we can drop them and keep only the numbers. The system 2x₁ + x₂ = 5 and x₁ + x₂ = 3 becomes a matrix with a row for each equation, with the right-hand sides added as a last column. This is called an augmented matrix.

\begin{pmatrix}2&1&5\\1&1&3\end{pmatrix}

Combining equations now becomes work on rows, and each move is a row operation. Three kinds never change the solution. One is to swap two rows. Another is to scale a row by a nonzero number. The third is to add a multiple of one row to another. Each one acts on the whole row, the right-hand number included, because a row is a whole equation.

Take row 2 from row 1, which is written R₁ ← R₁ − R₂ and means replace row 1 by row 1 minus row 2. Row 1 becomes (1, 0, 2), which says x₁ = 2. Put that into the second equation: 2 + x₂ = 3, so x₂ = 1.

Teaching

Clear the entries below a pivot, then read the answer from the bottom up

To solve a system, take the first nonzero entry of the top row, called its pivot, and use row operations to make every entry below it zero. For x₁ + x₂ = 6 and 3x₁ + 2x₂ = 14 the pivot is the 1 at the top left, and R₂ ← R₂ − 3R₁ turns the second row into (0, −1 | −4):

\left(\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 1 & 6 \\ 3 & 2 & 14\end{array}\right)\ \longrightarrow\ \left(\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & -1 & -4\end{array}\right)

The second row now says −x₂ = −4, so x₂ = 4, and the first row then gives x₁ + 4 = 6, so x₁ = 2. Solving from the last row upwards, putting each value found into the rows above it, is called back-substitution.

The new system is triangular: every row has fewer unknowns than the one above it, which is what makes the bottom-up reading possible. Row reduction is the procedure of making a system triangular with row operations and then back-substituting.

Solving x₁ + 2x₂ = 8 and 3x₁ + x₂ = 9

Teaching

With more unknowns the same two moves repeat

Nothing changes when there are more unknowns: clear below the first pivot, then below the second, and so on, until the system is triangular, and then back-substitute. Take the triangular system x₁ + 2x₂ + x₃ = 9, x₂ − x₃ = 1 and 2x₃ = 6. The last equation gives x₃ = 3, the middle one gives x₂ − 3 = 1, so x₂ = 4, and the first gives x₁ + 8 + 3 = 9, so x₁ = −2.

Each extra unknown adds one more clearing step and one more substitution, so the work grows steadily with the size of the system, which is why computers solve very large systems this way. The procedure is called Gaussian elimination, after Carl Friedrich Gauss, although the Chinese text The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art describes the same elimination on arrays of numbers many centuries earlier.

More unknowns, the same routine

The routine scales up. With three unknowns each row has four numbers, and the aim is a staircase. The top row may hold all three unknowns, the next row holds two, and the last row holds one. A reduced system might read x₁ + x₂ + x₃ = 9 on top, with x₂ + x₃ = 5 under it and x₃ = 2 at the bottom.

Start at the bottom, which already gives x₃ = 2. The middle row says x₂ + 2 = 5, so x₂ = 3. The top row says x₁ + 3 + 2 = 9, so x₁ = 4. Each solved unknown goes into the row above it.

Reaching the staircase takes row operations column by column: clear the entries under the first unknown, and then those under the second. Rows can also be swapped. If the equation you want at the top is missing, swap rows to bring it up.

Recap

A system is solved by row operations that clear the entries below each pivot, followed by back-substitution.

Each equation is a row of the augmented matrix, and swapping rows, scaling a row by a nonzero number, or adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the solutions unchanged. Using them to make the system triangular, and then reading the unknowns from the last row upwards, solves it, and the answer can always be checked in the original equations.

Next, the same procedure shows when a system has exactly one solution, when it has none, and when it has infinitely many.

One, none or many solutions

Opener

A system of two equations in two unknowns asks where two straight lines meet, and two lines in a plane can do one of three things. They can cross at a single point, they can run side by side and never meet, or they can lie exactly on top of each other and share every point. In the first case the system has one solution, in the second none, and in the third infinitely many.

Teaching

A row that reads 0 = c, with c not zero, means there is no solution

Row operations can produce a row whose coefficients are all zero. Take x₁ + x₂ = 5 and 2x₁ + 2x₂ = 12. The operation R₂ ← R₂ − 2R₁ turns the second row into (0, 0 | 2):

\left(\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 1 & 5 \\ 2 & 2 & 12\end{array}\right)\ \longrightarrow\ \left(\begin{array}{cc|c}1 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 2\end{array}\right)

That row says 0x₁ + 0x₂ = 2, which is 0 = 2, and no values of x₁ and x₂ can make it true, since the left side is zero whatever they are. A solution has to satisfy every row, so the system has none. A system with no solution is called inconsistent, and one with at least one solution is consistent.

The picture is two parallel lines. The second equation says 2x₁ + 2x₂ = 12, which is x₁ + x₂ = 6, and that line runs beside x₁ + x₂ = 5 without ever meeting it. The sign to look for is a row of zeros on the left with a nonzero number on the right.

A row like this is the signal to stop. Whatever the other rows say, no solution exists. The same reading holds for 0 = 5, or for 0 equal to any nonzero number.

Teaching

A consistent system has as many free variables as unknowns minus pivots

Every unknown either has a pivot or is free, so in a consistent system the number of free variables is the number of unknowns less the number of pivots. Take x₁ + 2x₂ + x₃ = 6 and x₃ = 2. There are three unknowns, and the pivots are in the first and third columns, so there is one free variable, x₂. Setting x₂ = t gives x₃ = 2 and x₁ = 6 − 2t − 2 = 4 − 2t, so (4 − 2t, t, 2) is a solution for every t, and t = 0 gives (4, 0, 2), where 4 + 0 + 2 = 6.

Each free variable is a degree of freedom in the solution set. One free variable gives a line of solutions, as here, two give a plane, and so on, and four unknowns with two pivots would leave two free. The count of pivots, which fixes the count of free variables, is the quantity a later lesson calls the rank.

Counting free variables

Free variables can be counted. A reduced system has some number of unknowns and some number of pivots, and each pivot pins one unknown. Whatever is left over is free, so a consistent system has (unknowns − pivots) free variables.

Take four unknowns with x₁ + x₂ + x₄ = 6 and x₃ + x₄ = 2. The pivots are in columns 1 and 3, so x₂ and x₄ are free: 4 − 2 = 2. Choose x₂ = s and x₄ = t, and the rest follow: x₃ = 2 − t and x₁ = 6 − s − t. Every choice of s and t gives a solution.

When there are no free variables, a consistent system has exactly one solution. Each free variable adds a direction in which you can move without breaking any equation.

Teaching

A system with zeros on the right always has the zero solution

A system whose right-hand sides are all zero, written Ax = 0, is called homogeneous. Putting every unknown equal to zero makes every left side zero, so the zero vector always solves it, and it is called the trivial solution. A homogeneous system is therefore never inconsistent: the row 0 = c with c not zero cannot appear, because row operations on a column of zeros leave it zero.

Only two outcomes remain. If every unknown has a pivot, the trivial solution is the only one. If some unknown is free, there are infinitely many solutions, such as (t, t) for the single equation x₁ − x₂ = 0, whose second unknown is free. Homogeneous systems return in the lesson on rank and the null space, where their solutions form an important set of their own.

Opener

A subspace is a set that combining its members cannot leave

A set of vectors is a subspace of the plane if it passes three tests: it contains the zero vector, the sum of any two of its members is again a member, and any multiple of a member is again a member. The points (x, y) with y = 3x pass all three. The point (0, 0) is on the line, the sum (x, 3x) + (x′, 3x′) = (x + x′, 3(x + x′)) is on the line, and the multiple c(x, 3x) = (cx, 3cx) is on it too.

Every span passes the tests for the reason seen earlier, since a combination of combinations is a combination, so every span is a subspace. The converse also holds in the plane, where the only subspaces are the single point at the origin, the lines through the origin, and the whole plane, of dimensions 0, 1 and 2.

A set that stays inside itself

Every span we have met has one property worth noticing. Take the line through (1, 2) and pick two places on it, (2, 4) and (−1, −2). Their sum is (1, 2), which is on the line again. Every span is a subspace, because a sum of combinations of the same moves is another combination of them, and so is a scaled one. The line and the whole floor are both examples. The zero vector also gives a quick way to rule a set out: if a set misses it, the set cannot be a subspace.

The whole recipe

Here is the recipe from start to finish. Dot v with d, and dot d with itself. Divide the first number by the second to get c, the number of copies of d you need. Scaling d by c gives the projection. Subtracting that from v gives the residual.

Two checks catch most slips. Scaling d changes c but never the projection, because a longer d needs fewer copies. And when v and d already make a right angle, v · d = 0, so c = 0 and the projection is the zero vector: the whole of v is residual.

The opposite case also happens. When v already lies along d, the projection is v itself and the residual is zero.

For three evenly spaced points the slope depends on the ends alone

The two normal equations for measurements y₁, y₂ and y₃ at x = 0, 1 and 2 are 3c + 3s = y₁ + y₂ + y₃ and 3c + 5s = y₂ + 2y₃. Normalise, subtract, normalise: three moves build an orthonormal pair

The construction is called the Gram–Schmidt process, after Jørgen Pedersen Gram, who used it in 1883, and Erhard Schmidt, who set it out in 1907. Given independent vectors a and b, first normalise a to get u₁. Second, subtract from b its part along u₁, which for a unit vector is just (b · u₁)u₁, leaving w = b − (b · u₁)u₁. Third, normalise w to get u₂.

Take a = (4, 3) and b = (3, 5). The length of a is 5, so u₁ = (0.8, 0.6). Then b · u₁ = 2.4 + 3 = 5.4, and w = (3, 5) − 5.4(0.8, 0.6) = (−1.32, 1.76), whose length is √(1.7424 + 3.0976) = √4.84 = 2.2. So u₂ = (−0.6, 0.8), and the explorer shows the part of b along (4, 3) and along the across axis.

The eigenvalues are the values of λ that make det(A − λI) zero

To find eigenvectors without guessing, rewrite Av = λv as (A − λI)v = 0. A nonzero solution v exists only if A − λI is singular, which happens exactly when its determinant is zero. For the matrix with rows (2, 1) and (1, 2), this gives:

\det\begin{pmatrix}2-\lambda&1\\1&2-\lambda\end{pmatrix}=(2-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)=0

A check is available for a 2 × 2 matrix: the eigenvalues add up to the sum of the diagonal entries, 1 + 3 = 4, and multiply to the determinant, 1 × 3 = 3, which is 2 × 2 − 1 × 1. The equation for λ is a quadratic, so there are at most two eigenvalues for a 2 × 2 matrix.

Powers of a matrix only raise the eigenvalues to a power

Applying A twice to an eigenvector multiplies it by its eigenvalue twice: A(Av) = A(λv) = λAv = λ²v. In general Aᵏv = λᵏv. For the vector (1, 1) of the matrix above, whose eigenvalue is 3, A²(1, 1) = 9(1, 1) and A¹⁰(1, 1) = 3¹⁰(1, 1) = 59049(1, 1).

The whole matrix behaves in the same way, since Aᵏ = P Dᵏ P⁻¹: in the product the inner P⁻¹P pairs cancel, and Dᵏ just raises each diagonal entry to the kth power. For k = 2, D² has the diagonal 9 and 1, and P D² P⁻¹ has the rows (5, 4) and (4, 5), which agrees with multiplying A by itself, since the first row of A² is (2 × 2 + 1 × 1, 2 × 1 + 1 × 2) = (5, 4).

Finding A¹⁰⁰ directly would take ninety-nine matrix multiplications, whereas the diagonal form needs only 3¹⁰⁰ and 1¹⁰⁰. This is how the long-run behaviour of a repeated process is read from eigenvalues: the eigenvalue with the largest size comes to dominate, because its power grows fastest.

Applying a matrix again and again

Suppose the machine is used twice in a row. Apply A to an eigenvector v with eigenvalue λ and you get λv. The second application acts on λv. Since a matrix acts on a multiple by acting on the original and multiplying, A(λv) = λ(A v) = λ·λv = λ²v. Each application multiplies the arrow by λ once more, so n applications multiply it by λⁿ.

Every matrix is a turn, a stretch and a second turn: A = UΣVᵀ

In general a matrix is not one stretch followed by one turn, but a turn, then a stretch, then a second turn. The first turn is Vᵀ, which lines the input up with a set of perpendicular directions, Σ then stretches along them, and U turns the result. In symbols:

A = U\,\Sigma\,V^{T}

The matrices U and V are orthogonal: their columns are orthonormal, so each turns or reflects space without changing a length or an angle. The matrix Σ is diagonal, with the singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … on its diagonal, all at least zero. This is the singular value decomposition, or SVD, which Eugenio Beltrami found for real square matrices in 1873 and Camille Jordan found independently in 1874.

Turn, stretch, turn

The machine in the opening was a stretch followed by a turn. The general case allows a turn before the stretch as well, because the axes along which a matrix stretches need not be the axes the input came with. Every matrix A can be written as A = U Σ Vᵀ. Read it from the right. The input meets Vᵀ first, which turns it. The stretch Σ comes next, and U turns the result once more.

U and V are orthogonal matrices, which means they only turn or flip the plane and never change a length. Σ, the Greek capital sigma, is a diagonal matrix, and the numbers on its diagonal are the stretch sizes. These numbers are the singular values of A. For the machine in the opening they are 5 and 3, listed from largest to smallest as is usual.

Singular values are never negative. The matrix with rows (−5, 0) and (0, 1) flips the x axis as well as stretching it, but a flip is a kind of turn, so it goes into U or V and Σ keeps only the sizes. A shortcut helps for matrices like these: when the columns of A are at right angles to each other, the singular values are simply the lengths of the columns.

Zero singular values and rank

A singular value can be zero. Take the matrix with every entry 1, rows (1, 1) and (1, 1). Both columns equal (1, 1), so the machine sends every arrow (a, b) to (a + b, a + b), which lies on a single line. The plane has been flattened onto that line. The singular values are 2 and 0: the machine stretches the arrow (1, 1) by 2, and it crushes the perpendicular arrow (1, −1) to nothing.

This links back to rank. The rank of a matrix counts the independent directions it keeps, and each kept direction has a stretch above zero. So the number of positive singular values equals the rank. A rank-one matrix has one positive singular value and the rest zero. A square matrix with determinant 0 has at least one singular value equal to 0, which is the flattening seen earlier.

The decomposition works for matrices that are not square

Nothing in the construction needs the matrix to be square. If A has m rows and n columns, then Vᵀ is n × n and turns the inputs, Σ is m × n and stretches while changing the number of coordinates, and U is m × m and turns the outputs. Σ carries the singular values on its main diagonal, as many as the smaller of m and n, and zeros elsewhere. The matrix with rows (3, 0), (0, 1) and (0, 0) takes the plane into three-dimensional space by stretching by 3 and 1, and the explorer shows it beside a square example.

[Figure]

Because the singular values are listed from largest to smallest, keeping only the largest few gives the best approximation of the matrix by one of lower rank, a result of Carl Eckart and Gale Young from 1936. That is why the SVD is used to compress images and to find the strongest patterns in large tables of data.

Matrices that are not square

A matrix need not be square. A 3 × 2 matrix takes arrows with two entries and gives arrows with three entries, so it carries the plane into three-dimensional space. Take the matrix with rows (3, 0), (0, 1) and (0, 0). The arrow (1, 0) goes to (3, 0, 0), and (0, 1) goes to (0, 1, 0). The three factors change shape to fit. Vᵀ is 2 × 2, because it turns the plane of inputs, and U is 3 × 3, because it turns the space of outputs.
