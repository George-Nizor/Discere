About this course

Calculus is the mathematics of change, and it rests on two questions that algebra cannot answer on its own: how fast a quantity is changing at one exact moment, and how much has built up after a stretch of change. Engineers use it to design bridges and engines, scientists to model motion, growth and heat, and economists to follow how costs and profits shift, and anyone who goes on to physics, statistics or machine learning meets it early.

The course has twelve lessons in four parts, and each part builds on the one before. The first begins with the limit, which gives a meaning to a fraction that reads 0 ÷ 0 at a point, and ends with the derivative, the slope of a curve at a single point. The second gives rules for finding derivatives quickly, including the chain rule for a function placed inside another, and applies them to motion. The third uses derivatives to find where a curve turns, runs them backwards to recover a function from its rate, and builds an area from thin rectangles. The fourth gives areas a sign and shows that the derivative and the integral undo each other, which is the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Each lesson explains first and asks afterwards, so a question comes only after you have been shown what it needs.

  • You'll be able to find the slope of a curve at a point, from a limit and with the power and chain rules.
  • You'll be able to read speed and acceleration from a position, and find where a curve turns.

Approaching a value

Opener

The fraction (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is simple to evaluate at almost every input. At x = 3 it gives 5 ÷ 1, which is 5, and at x = 2.5 it gives 2.25 ÷ 0.5, which is 4.5. At x = 2 itself it cannot be evaluated at all, because the top and the bottom are both zero there and 0 ÷ 0 is not a number, so the graph of the fraction has a single missing point.

Whether such a gap can be given a meaning is the first question of calculus, and it is not an idle one. The slope of a curve at a single point, which this part of the course builds towards, turns out to be a fraction of exactly this kind, with zero on top and zero below at the very point where we want it. The limit is the value the outputs approach, whatever happens at the point itself

The number the outputs are closing in on is called the limit of the fraction as x approaches 2, and it is written with the abbreviation lim:

\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = 4

A limit describes where the outputs head. It makes no claim about an output at the point itself, which is why this limit can exist when the fraction has no value at 2. Not every point has a gap, however. The function h(x) = 10 − x is defined at 4, where it gives 6, and as x approaches 4 its outputs approach 6 as well. The idea was in use long before it had a careful definition. Newton and Leibniz built calculus in the later seventeenth century on quantities that were allowed to shrink towards nothing, and it was Augustin-Louis Cauchy who, in his Cours d'analyse of 1821, described a limit as the fixed value that a variable's successive values approach until they differ from it as little as one wishes.

Simplifying before you approach

Walking up to a gap shows roughly where a graph is heading, and it needs a calculator. Algebra gives the answer exactly. The plan is to rewrite the fraction in a simpler form that agrees with it everywhere except at the gap.

Take (x² − x) ÷ x, which gives 0 ÷ 0 at x = 0. The top factors as x(x − 1). For any x other than 0 we can divide top and bottom by x, and the fraction becomes x − 1. Near 0 the fraction and x − 1 give the same answers, so the fraction is heading for 0 − 1 = −1.

Dividing by x is allowed because a limit only looks at inputs close to 0, never at 0 itself, so x is never zero when we do it. A result of 0 ÷ 0 is called an indeterminate form. The substitution told you nothing about the limit, so the next job is to simplify and look again.

Many gaps come from a difference of squares, where x² − a² = (x − a)(x + a). For example, x² − 100 = (x − 10)(x + 10).

Recap

A limit is the value a function's outputs approach as the input approaches a point, and it does not depend on the function's value at that point.

The limit of a function as x approaches a point is the number its outputs head for when x gets close from either side. It ignores whatever the function does at the point itself, which may be undefined or may have been set to something else.

When direct substitution gives 0 ÷ 0, the fraction is indeterminate at that point and can usually be rewritten: factor the top, cancel the common factor, and let x approach the point in the expression that remains.

A graph can head for different values from its two sides, and the next lesson asks what that does to the function's continuity.

When the two sides agree

With a name for each side, we can ask whether they match. Take a graph with a step in it, where the output is 1 for every input below 0 and −2 for every input of 0 or above. Coming from the left the outputs stay at 1, so the left-hand limit at 0 is 1. Coming from the right they stay at −2, so the right-hand limit is −2.

Continuous at a point

A two-sided limit says where a graph is heading. A separate question is whether the graph actually arrives there. Take the line y = x + 1. At x = 3 it heads for 4, and its value there is 4, so the graph passes through the place it was heading for. A function that does this at a point is continuous there.

Continuity at a point needs the function to have a value there. It also needs the two-sided limit to exist, and the two numbers to be equal. Roughly, you can draw the graph through that point without lifting the pen.

A gap with no value fails the first condition and a jump fails the second. A dot that sits off the line, with both sides agreeing on a different height, fails the last.

Why the power comes down

A pattern found from a handful of values needs a reason, so look at where the 2 comes from. The slope of y = x² at x is the limit of the secant slope, and expanding the top and dividing by h leaves 2x + h. As h shrinks the h vanishes and 2x remains.

\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}=2x+h

The same sum can be drawn as areas. A square of side x has area x². Make the side x + h and the square gains two strips of area xh and a small corner of area h². The gain is 2xh + h², which is the top of the secant slope, and the corner is the part that vanishes once h shrinks.

Do the same for y = x³. Expanding (x + h)³ gives x³ + 3x²h + 3xh² + h³, so the top of the secant slope is 3x²h + 3xh² + h³. Dividing by h leaves 3x² + 3xh + h². The last two terms vanish as h shrinks, so the slope is 3x².

In general, the expansion of (x + h)ⁿ starts with xⁿ and continues with nxⁿ⁻¹h, and every later term has h² or a higher power in it. Those terms vanish and the term nxⁿ⁻¹ is left.

\frac{d}{dx}\,x^n = n\,x^{n-1}

This is the power rule. In words, bring the power down in front and take one off it. Finding a derivative is called differentiating, so x⁴ differentiates to 4x³ and x⁵ to 5x⁴.

Rates in a chain

Suppose you are paid £15 an hour, and each pound buys 1.2 euros. Your pay is a rate of 15 pounds per hour, and the exchange is a rate of 1.2 euros per pound. Together, 15 × 1.2 = 18 euros arrive for each hour you work.

The hours came first and the euros last, with pounds as the stage in between. To get the rate from hours to euros we multiplied the rates of the two stages. The units show why: euros per pound times pounds per hour leaves euros per hour, because the pounds cancel.

The derivative of a composition is the outer rate at the inner value, times the inner rate

The function f(x) = (4x + 5)² is a chain of two stages. The first stage turns x into 4x + 5 and is called the inner function; the second squares whatever it receives and is called the outer function. Writing u for the output of the inner stage, u = 4x + 5 and f = u². Each stage has a rate: u changes by 4 for every unit of x, and by the power ruleThe derivative of xⁿ is n xⁿ⁻¹. The rates multiply, with the outer rate taken at the value that the inner stage delivers, so the derivative is 2u × 4, which is 2(4x + 5) × 4, or 8(4x + 5). This is the chain rule: differentiate the outer function, keep the inner function inside it, and multiply by the derivative of the inner function. Two stages in one formula

The same structure appears in a formula. In f(x) = (4x + 5)² there are two stages. The inner function u = 4x + 5 turns x into a number, and the outer function u² squares that number. Move x up by 1 and u moves up by 4, so the inner stage has rate 4.

Reading motion from the curve means reading its slope, and then the slope of its slope, and the derivative gives both.

Where does the curve turn?

Opener

The velocity of a ball thrown upwards is positive on the way up and negative on the way down, so at the top, where it changes from one to the other, it must pass through zero. That observation turns the problem of finding the highest point into the problem of finding where a derivative is zero, and it works for any smooth curve and not only for the path of a ball.

Curves that peak, dip or level off are common in applications, from the best price to the least material for a container, and finding a highest or lowest value is among the main uses of the derivative. This lesson shows how to find the places where a curve turns, how to tell a peak from a dip and from a mere pause, and why the ends of an interval must be checked as well.

A local maximum is where the slope changes from positive to negative

Not every stationary point is a peak, but the peaks are among them, and the sign of the derivative on each side tells them apart. A local maximum is a point whose height is at least as great as that of every point near it. Approaching one the curve rises, so the derivative is positive; leaving it the curve falls, so the derivative is negative; and at the point itself the derivative passes through zero. The ball's height 4t − t² follows this pattern, since its derivative 4 − 2t is positive before t = 2, zero at t = 2 and negative after.

Finding a local maximum therefore has two parts: solve f′(x) = 0 for the stationary points, and then check the sign of f′ just before and just after each one.

Pierre de Fermat noticed in the 1630s that near the top of a smooth curve the height hardly changes when you move a little to either side, and he turned that observation into a method for finding maxima and minima, years before Newton and Leibniz developed the derivative.

Undoing the power rule

For a rate like 6x we could guess the antiderivative. For 12x² or 5x⁴ we want a method that always works, and it comes from the power rule. Differentiating xⁿ brings the power down in front and takes one off the power. To undo that, make the two moves in the opposite order: add one to the power, then divide by the new power.

So the antiderivative of x² is x³ ÷ 3, and the antiderivative of 5x⁴ is 5x⁵ ÷ 5 = x⁵. In symbols, the whole family of antiderivatives is written with an integral sign and is called the indefinite integral of the rate.

\int x^n\,dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C

The rule works for every power except −1, which this course does not need. A rate with several terms is undone one term at a time, and a plain number such as 2 undoes to 2x because 2x has slope 2. Differentiate the result to check it.

When areas cancel

Opener

An object whose velocity is +60 km/h for one hour and −60 km/h for the next ends where it began, although it has travelled 120 km. The area method of the last lesson gives 60 for the first hour and −60 for the second, and adding them gives 0, the change in position, while the 120 is the total distance. Both numbers are useful, and they come from the same graph by different arithmetic.

A graph that runs above the axis and then below it therefore needs a convention. The amount that has built up is not the area of everything between the graph and the axis, but a balance in which the parts above and below pull in opposite directions. Seeing how the balance is struck, and when the parts cancel, is the work of this lesson.

By the end you'll count area below the axis as negative, tell net change from total area, and know what happens to an integral when its bounds are swapped.

Total area adds the sizes, while signed area keeps the signs

Signed area answers the question of net change, but sometimes the quantity wanted is the total size of everything between the graph and the axis, such as the total distance travelled by an object that moves forwards and then backwards. Total area

Signed area answers the question of how much the amount changed. Sometimes the question is simply how much ground a region covers. Think of painting the region between a graph and the axis, where it takes the same paint above the axis as below. For that you want the total area, which counts every piece as positive.

When two pieces have the same size and opposite signs, the signed area is exactly zero even though a lot of area is there. A signed area of zero says that the gains and losses balance. It does not say that the graph is flat or that nothing happened.

Teaching

Swapping the bounds of an integral changes its sign

An integral from a to b runs through the interval in the direction from a to b. Running it the other way, from b to a, reverses the direction of every strip, so the widths of the strips become negative while the heights stay the same, and each product changes sign. The bounds of an integral are its two ends, a and b, and swapping them changes the sign of the result:

\int_b^a f(x)\,dx=-\int_a^b f(x)\,dx

For the constant rate 2, the integral from 0 to 4 is 2 × 4, or 8, and the integral from 4 back to 0 is −8. An integral between equal bounds is 0, since there are no strips at all.

In general, the area under a rate from a to b is the running total at b minus the running total at a, and the running total is an antiderivative F of the rate.

\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a),\quad F'=f

This is the fundamental theorem of calculus. To find an area under a rate you find an antiderivative, put in the two ends and subtract. No rectangles or triangles are needed.
