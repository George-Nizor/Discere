About this course

Mechanics is the part of physics that explains how things move and why, and it begins with motions that anyone can watch: a trolley rolling along a track, a ball thrown into the air, two carts that meet and lock together. This course follows a single chain of ideas through eighteen lessons, in which each new quantity is introduced because the one before it cannot answer a question that has just come up.

The first six lessons describe motion. Position, displacement and distance say where something is and how far it has gone, velocity and acceleration say how quickly those change, and the formulas for constant acceleration let you predict where a moving object will be, whether it is rolling along a track or falling under gravity. The next four lessons turn to causes, with balanced forces, Newton's second law, the difference between mass and weight, and the way two objects always push on each other in equal and opposite pairs.

The remaining lessons add friction and then replace forces by energy and momentum, which answer some questions much more directly. Work, kinetic energy and potential energy follow an object through a fall without following it second by second, power and efficiency measure how fast energy is delivered and how much of it is wasted, and momentum and impulse give the speed of two carts after they collide, however brief the impact. Each lesson explains an idea before it asks about it, so a question never comes before the explanation it needs, and the numbers are part of the work, so a calculator is welcome.

  • You'll be able to tell a change in position from the distance travelled, and find an average velocity from a trip or a graph.
  • You'll be able to predict the velocity and position of an object that speeds up or slows down at a steady rate.
  • You'll be able to use Newton's laws to find the acceleration that a set of forces produces.
  • You'll be able to find the work a force does and follow energy between motion, height and heat.
  • You'll be able to use momentum and impulse to predict the speeds after a collision.

Where did it go?

Opener

A standard outdoor running track is 400 m round, so a runner who completes one lap has run 400 m. When she stops on the finish line, which is also the start line, she is no farther from where she began than if she had never moved. Both statements are true, and they answer two different questions: how much ground did she cover, and where did she end up compared with where she began?

Physics keeps the two answers apart with separate quantities, because a motion problem usually turns on which of them is wanted, and the first step in telling them apart is a way of saying exactly where something is.

A runner stops exactly on the line where she started. How far is she from where she began, in metres?

After a right answer: 0 m. The 400 m she ran says how much ground she covered, but it says nothing about how far apart her start and finish are.

After a wrong answer: The length of the lap is how much ground she covered. This question asks how far her finishing place is from her starting place.

By the end you'll find both the displacement and the distance for a trip along a line, and say why the two can differ.

Teaching

A position is a signed number measured from an origin you choose

To say where something is, you have to say what you are measuring from and which way you are measuring. For an object that moves along a straight line, such as a trolley on a track, you begin by choosing one point on the line to call zero, which is the origin, and one direction along the line to call the positive direction. The object's position is then the number of metres between it and the origin, written with a plus sign if it lies on the positive side and a minus sign if it lies on the other.

[Figure]

The figure sends a trolley from the origin to each of these positions. The plus sign tells you that the first is five metres from the origin in the positive direction, and the minus sign tells you that the second is six metres from it the other way, so the sign carries information that the size alone does not. Nothing in the track decides which end is positive, and the choice is yours, but once it is made it has to be kept for the whole problem, because a position of −6 m means something only relative to the origin and direction you started with.

Saying where something is

Back in the corridor, we need a way to say where you are with a single number. The trick is to measure from a mark. Choose the door and call it zero, then measure in metres from there, as if a tape measure lay along the floor with its zero end at the door. That starting mark is called the origin, and you can put it wherever suits the job.

A number alone does not say which side of the origin you are on, so we also choose a positive direction. Say it points towards the far end of the corridor. A spot 5 metres along it has the position +5 m. A spot 4 metres behind the door, out in the street, has the position −4 m. The sign gives the side and the number gives how far from the origin.

Both choices are ours to make. Two people watching the same walk can pick different origins, or opposite positive directions, and both be right as long as each keeps to their own choice. Once the choices are made, a position is a single signed number. The same idea works up and down: with the ground floor as the origin and up as positive, the basement of a building is a negative position. With that in place we can describe a move from one place to another.

Teaching

Displacement is the final position minus the initial position

When the trolley moves, its position changes, and that change is the quantity that describes the motion. If the position at the start is the initial position and the position at the end is the final position, the change is called the displacement, and you find it by taking the initial position away from the final one.

\text{displacement} = \text{final position} - \text{initial position}

[Figure]

Because positions carry signs, a displacement carries one too. A trolley that moves from 1 m to 4 m has a displacement of 4 − 1 = +3 m, and one that moves back from 4 m to 1 m has a displacement of 1 − 4 = −3 m, so the size, 3 m, says how far each moved and the sign says whether it moved with the positive direction or against it. Physicists write a change in any quantity with the Greek capital delta, so the displacement is written \Delta x, where x stands for position.

The change in position

Now let the walk begin. You start at the door, which is position 0. You finish at the 5 m mark. That is a change in position. Subtract the starting position from the finishing one and you have its size and its direction, which physics calls the displacement.

\text{displacement} = \text{final position} - \text{initial position}

Try it on a walker who goes from 1 m to 4 m. Finishing minus starting is 4 − 1 = +3 m. The plus sign says the walker moved towards larger positions, which is our positive direction. The 3 says by how much. If the same walker goes back from 4 m to 1 m, the sum is 1 − 4 = −3 m. The size is unchanged and the sign flips, because the move now goes the other way.

Look at what the sum uses. It takes the place where the walker began and the place where it finished, and nothing in between. A walker who took a long route and came back gets the same displacement as one who walked straight there. That makes displacement a tidy answer to where something ended up compared with where it began. How much ground a trip covers needs more than its two ends, and we come to that after some practice.

Teaching

Distance is the length of the whole path, so every stretch is added

Displacement compares only the start and the finish, so it cannot tell you how much ground was covered on the way. A trolley that rolls from 0 m out to 6 m and then back to 2 m has a displacement of 2 − 0 = +2 m, which is exactly the displacement of a trolley that rolled straight to 2 m, even though the first one travelled much further.

The quantity that counts the ground covered is the distance, which is the total length of the path. You find it by splitting the trip wherever the object turns round and adding the length of each stretch: here the first stretch is 6 m long and the second is 6 − 2 = 4 m long, so the distance is 10 m.

[Figure]

The two trips end at the same place, so they have the same displacement, but their distances differ. A distance can only grow as the object moves, so it is never negative and has no direction, whereas a displacement can be positive, negative or zero. That is also why the runner in the opening, who covered 400 m and finished on the line she started from, has a displacement of zero.

The ground a trip covers

Go back to the walk that opened the lesson: 5 metres along the corridor, then 2 metres back. Its displacement is 3 − 0 = +3 m, since you finished at the 3 m mark and began at 0. A pedometer in your pocket would give a different total, because it counts every step in either direction. It would show 7 metres.

That second number is the distance: the total length of the path you followed. To find it, cut the path wherever you turn round, take each stretch as a plain length and add them up. The 5 m out and the 2 m back both count, and turning round gives no refund for the way back. Distance has no direction, so it is never negative.

So one trip has two honest descriptions. Displacement compares where you finished with where you began, and it can be positive, negative or zero. Distance adds up the ground covered, and it can only grow as the trip goes on. The two have the same size only when the trip never turns round. The picture sets a trip that doubles back beside one that goes straight, so you can compare them.

Teaching

Moving the origin changes every position but not the displacement

The origin is a matter of convenience, and two people who measure the same trip may well choose different ones. One student puts the zero of a tape at a point on the bench, and another puts hers 10 m further back, so they read different positions for the same trolley, because each reading is measured from a different origin.

The shift is the same for every reading, though, which is the point. Whatever amount is added to the starting position is added to the finishing position as well, and so it cancels when one is taken away from the other.

[Figure]

The displacement is 6 − 1 = +5 m on the first tape and 16 − 11 = +5 m on the second. A distance is built from displacements of single stretches, so it is unchanged as well, and the general rule is that positions depend on the origin you chose, while displacements and distances do not.

Moving the origin

One more check on our measuring set-up: does the choice of origin change the answers? Two friends watch the same walker. One lays a tape with its zero at the door. The other lays a second tape with its zero 10 metres behind the door, so every position she reads is 10 m larger than the first friend's.

This is why we can put the origin wherever it makes the sums easiest. Teaching

Average speed is the distance covered divided by the time it took

Two trolleys each travel 12 m along a track, the first in 3 s and the second in 6 s. The first is plainly the faster, because it gets over the same ground in less time, and a number that measures this is the ground covered in each second, which you find by dividing. The time that passes between the start of a trip and its end is called the elapsed time, and the total distance divided by the elapsed time is the average speed.

\text{average speed} = \dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{elapsed time}}

[Figure]

The first trolley covers 12 ÷ 3 = 4 m in every second and the second covers 12 ÷ 6 = 2 m in every second, so the first has twice the average speed. The unit follows from the division, since metres divided by seconds gives metres per second, written m/s. The word average matters, because a trolley need not move at a steady pace: the figure says only how much ground was covered over the whole elapsed time, and not how the ground was shared among the seconds. Average velocity is the displacement divided by the elapsed time

Speed is built from distance, which has no direction, so it cannot tell a trolley rolling forwards from one rolling back. When the direction matters, the displacement takes the place of the distance, and the average velocity is the displacement divided by the elapsed time.

\text{average velocity} = \dfrac{\text{displacement}}{\text{elapsed time}}

[Figure]

The unit is m/s again, but because the displacement carries a sign, so does the velocity. The first trolley is displaced +8 m in 2 s, so its average velocity is +8 ÷ 2 = +4 m/s, and the second is displaced −9 m in 3 s, so its average velocity is −9 ÷ 3 = −3 m/s. Their average speeds are 4 m/s and 3 m/s, but only the velocities show, through their signs, that the trolleys went opposite ways. The two averages have the same size whenever an object never reverses, and the next screen takes that condition away.

Adding a direction

Average speed forgets which way you went. That suits a pedometer, but the last lesson showed that direction matters, because displacement keeps it as a sign. Divide the displacement by the time instead of the distance and you get the average velocity.

\text{average velocity} = \frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time}}

A cyclist whose displacement is +8 m in 2 s has an average velocity of +4 m/s. A cyclist who ends up 9 metres behind the start after 3 seconds has an average velocity of −9 ÷ 3 = −3 m/s. The sign says which way along the line the trip went, and the number says how fast.

On a straight trip that never turns round, the displacement and the distance have the same size. The average speed is then the size of the average velocity, and the only difference is the sign. That is why speed is never negative while velocity can be. The two come apart once a trip doubles back, and a later screen looks at that.

When the two averages differ

Now let the trip turn round. Suppose you walk 30 m to a shop and straight back, and the whole round trip takes 20 s. The distance is 30 + 30 = 60 m, so the average speed is 60 ÷ 20 = 3 m/s. That matches how brisk the walk felt.

The displacement tells a different story. You finished where you started, so finishing minus starting is 0 m. The average velocity is 0 ÷ 20 = 0 m/s. The way out counts as positive and the way back as negative, so in displacement they cancel. In distance they cannot cancel, because every stretch adds.

This is the sense in which velocity and speed differ. Speed asks how much ground is covered each second. Velocity asks how fast the position is changing in a particular direction, so a trip that ends at its start has an average velocity of zero no matter how fast it went. From here on we keep both, and use whichever the question needs.

Working out the acceleration

Often you are given two velocities and the time between them, and the acceleration has to be found. Take the change in velocity, which is the finishing velocity minus the starting one, and divide it by the time the change took.

a = \frac{v_{\text{final}} - v_{\text{initial}}}{\text{time taken}}

Say a cyclist speeds up from 4 m/s to 16 m/s in 3 s. The change in velocity is 16 − 4 = 12 m/s, spread over 3 s, so the acceleration is 12 ÷ 3 = 4 m/s². This is an average, like the average velocity we found earlier. The velocity might not have changed at an even rate, and the sum gives only the overall rate for the stretch.

The sum works for any pair of velocities, so it also works when they are negative. That matters in a moment, because a negative velocity is just motion in the negative direction. First, try the sum on a case where everything is positive.

A shortcut and a starting point

Putting the pieces together gives a shortcut. The average of u and v = u + at is u + ½at, and multiplying by the time t gives the displacement, which we write s.

s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}

Take a cart that starts at 6 m/s with a = 2 m/s² for 3 s. The shortcut gives 6 × 3 + ½ × 2 × 3² = 18 + 9 = 27 m. The two-step method agrees: the cart ends at 12 m/s, averages 9 m/s and goes 9 × 3 = 27 m. The shortcut skips the finishing velocity, but it only holds while the acceleration is constant.

A displacement is a change of position, so to say where the cart ends up you add it to where it began. A cart that starts at the 10 m mark and has a displacement of 27 m finishes at the 37 m mark.

From rest, u is 0 and the formula shrinks to s = ½at². The time is squared, so doubling the time makes the cart go four times as far, and tripling it makes the cart go nine times as far. This is why an object that speeds up from rest seems to gather distance faster and faster.

The same law gives the force or the mass when the acceleration is known

The equation links three quantities, and any one can be found from the other two. Multiplying both sides of a = F_\text{net}/m by the mass gives the net force needed to produce a given acceleration, and dividing both sides of that by the acceleration gives the mass.

F_\text{net} = m\,a \qquad\qquad m = \dfrac{F_\text{net}}{a}

A net force to give a 7 kg object an acceleration of 3 m/s² is 7 × 3 = 21 N, and a net force of 21 N that gives an object an acceleration of 3 m/s² must be acting on a mass of 21 ÷ 3 = 7 kg. The quantity that combines force and distance is called work. When a constant force F acts in the same direction as the object's displacement d, the work it does is their product, and the unit is the joule, written J, which is the work done by a force of 1 N acting through a displacement of 1 m, so that 1 J = 1 N·m. W = F\,d

[Figure]

With friction, some mechanical energy becomes thermal energy, and the total stays the same

Friction and air resistance take energy out of the motion and turn it into thermal energy, which is the energy of the random motion of the atoms of the object and of the surface it rubs against. The object and the surface get slightly warmer, and the mechanical energy K + U falls by exactly the amount of thermal energy produced.

Nothing has been lost, because the total of every form of energy, thermal energy included, stays constant. This is the conservation of energy: energy is neither created nor destroyed, but moves from one object or form to another. Joule's measurements in the 1840s, in which falling weights turned a paddle in water and warmed it by a measured amount, were among the experiments that established it.

K_\text{final} = K_\text{initial} + (U_\text{initial} - U_\text{final}) - E_\text{thermal}

[Figure]

A 1 kg block that drops 3 m loses 1 × 10 × 3 = 30 J of potential energy. On a smooth ramp all 30 J becomes kinetic energy, but on a rough ramp that heats the surface by 10 J, only 30 − 10 = 20 J is left as motion, so the block arrives more slowly although it fell through the same height. The equation is a statement of accounts: what the object has at the end is what it had at the start, plus what was released from height, minus what was sent into heating.

Teaching

Power is the energy transferred each second, and its unit is the watt

The rate at which energy is transferred is called power, and it is found by dividing the energy by the time it took. The unit is the watt, written W, which is one joule each second. The unit is named after James Watt, the engineer who improved the steam engine and who also introduced the horsepower, about 746 W, as a way of comparing engines with the horses they replaced.

P = \dfrac{E}{t}

[Figure]

In the figure the same 600 J is transferred in two different times. In 12 s the power is 600 ÷ 12 = 50 W, and in 6 s it is 600 ÷ 6 = 100 W, so the faster job has twice the power although the energy is the same. Because power is energy divided by time, a machine's power says how quickly it can do a job and not how much the job needs. A 60 W lamp transfers 60 J every second, whether it is on for a moment or an hour, and the total energy depends on how long it is left on.

Impulse is the net force times the time it acts, and it equals the change in momentum

A constant net forceThe sum of all the forces on one object, with signs for direction. Met in 'When forces balance'. F gives an object an acceleration a = F/m, and in a time \Delta t that changes its velocity by \Delta v = a\,\Delta t. Multiplying through by the mass gives m\,\Delta v = F\,\Delta t. The right side is called the impulse of the force, measured in N·s, and the left side is the change in momentum, so the two are equal.

A push that lasts for a time

Momentum changes when something pushes. Push a stationary trolley with a steady force and it starts to move. Keep pushing and it moves faster. When only the two carts act on each other, their total momentum does not change

During the collision each cart pushes on the other, and by the third lawIf A exerts a force on B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force on A. m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2'
