About this course

How can you tell whether a conclusion really follows from the reasons given for it? Logic is the study of that question, and it gives you ways of answering it that don't depend on how convincing an argument sounds. It helps you read arguments more carefully and see where everyday reasoning goes wrong, and it is also the rule-following side of mathematics and computing.

The course has eight lessons in two parts. The first, Deduction, begins with the sentences logic works on, then takes up if-then claims, the ways of turning one round, a puzzle solved by trying each case, and the truth table, which lists every case at once. The second, Arguments, finds the conclusion and the reasons in an argument in everyday words, separates whether the conclusion follows from whether the reasons are true, and names the common ways reasoning fails.

Each lesson tells you the idea first and asks questions afterwards, so you meet a question only once you have been shown what it needs.

  • You'll be able to tell a claim from a question or a command, and say what its truth value is.
  • You'll be able to say exactly when an if-then rule is broken, and write its converse and its contrapositive.
  • You'll be able to solve a knights and knaves puzzle by trying each case and ruling out the ones that break a rule.
  • You'll be able to build a truth table for two claims and use it to test a piece of reasoning.
  • You'll be able to pick out the conclusion of an argument, tell valid from sound, and name the common fallacies.

What logic operates on

Opener

Here is a sentence: “The envelope on the table holds a £10 note.” The envelope is sealed and nobody has looked inside, so we can't say whether the sentence is right. We can say something else about it, though, that we couldn't say about “Does the envelope hold a £10 note?” or “Open the envelope.” The first sentence will be shown to be right or wrong the moment someone looks, and neither of the other two ever will be.

That difference is what this whole course rests on. Logic works on sentences that can be right or wrong, and asks what follows when they are, so before we can do anything else we need to pick those sentences out, and to see why not knowing the answer doesn't change what kind of sentence we have.

By the end you'll tell a claim from a question or command, say what its truth value is, and write its negation.

Teaching

A claim is a sentence that can turn out to be wrong

Sort the sentences you meet by what they are doing and three kinds come out. Some say how things are, such as “The lamp is on” or “The door is locked”. Some ask, as in “Is the door locked?”, and some tell, as in “Switch the lamp on”. We call a sentence of the first kind a claim, and the test for one is whether it can be wrong.

[Figure]

A question isn't wrong when you answer it, it is simply answered, and a command isn't wrong either, since it is obeyed or ignored. Only a claim puts forward something the world can contradict, and that is why logic, which is about what follows from what we put forward, works on claims and leaves the other two kinds alone.

Notice that the test has nothing to do with whether we agree or can check. “The lamp is on” is a claim whether or not the lamp is on, and whether or not you can see it from where you are.

Sentences that can be wrong

Go back to the envelope. Mia's sentence, “It holds a £10 note”, says how things are. That makes it something anyone could check. Open the envelope and either she was right or she was wrong. A sentence like that is called a claim.

Not every sentence works this way. “Is there a note in the envelope?” asks for information. It can be answered, but it can't be right or wrong in the way Mia's sentence can. “Open the envelope” is a command, and a command can be obeyed or ignored without ever being wrong. Only a sentence that says how things are can turn out to be wrong.

That gives the first skill in logic, which is sorting. Faced with a sentence, ask whether it says how things are. If it does, it is a claim and there is something to test. If it asks or tells, it is a different kind of sentence and there is nothing to test.

A claim is true or false, whether or not anyone knows which

Go back to the sealed envelope. The sentence “The envelope holds a £10 note” hasn't been checked, but that doesn't leave it in some third state of being neither right nor wrong. What the envelope decides

Back to Mia and Jon. Mia's claim is waiting for a verdict, and the verdict comes from checking the claim against how things are. If the envelope holds a £10 note, her claim passes. If it holds anything else, or nothing, her claim fails. That verdict is the claim's truth value, and the two values on offer are true and false.

Look at who sets the verdict. The envelope does. Nobody has opened it, but it still holds exactly what it holds, so Mia's claim already has a truth value. All that is missing is anyone knowing it. Calling a claim “unknown” describes what we have found out so far, and the claim itself already has one value. A third value never joins the list.

So in any real situation a claim has exactly one truth value, and that value is one of two. Jon's sentence, “It doesn't”, is the other side of the same envelope. Whichever way the envelope turns out, one of the two sentences passes and the other fails.

Teaching

The negation of a claim says the opposite, and takes the opposite truth value

The two sentences in the envelope figure are linked in a way that is worth naming. “The envelope holds no £10 note” says exactly what you would need to say to deny the first, and we call a claim that denies another claim its negation. We write the negation of a claim p as ¬p, read “not p”, so if p is “The lamp is on”, then ¬p is “The lamp is not on”.

Because ¬p is true exactly when p is false, negating a claim reverses its truth value: if p is true then ¬p is false, and if p is false then ¬p is true. The two can never share a value, and negating twice puts you back where you began.

Take care that a negation denies the claim and nothing more. The negation of “The soup is hot” is “The soup is not hot”, which leaves room for the soup being merely warm, and it is not “The soup is cold”.

Teaching

A claim that people dispute is still a claim

So far the claims have been ones you could settle by looking. Many sentences that people argue over are not like that, such as “Cats make better pets than dogs” or “The new tax is unfair”. It is tempting to say that these are only opinions, neither true nor false, and so none of logic's business.

But look at what the speaker is doing. Someone who says the tax is unfair is putting something forward as how things are, and someone who answers “No, it isn't” is contradicting it, which is exactly what claims allow and questions and commands do not. Being a claim doesn't depend on our agreeing, or even on our knowing how to settle it; it depends only on the sentence being put forward as true, so that it can be doubted.

Whether some claims, about beauty or fairness for example, have a truth value at all is a hard question in philosophy, and we can leave it open. Logic doesn't need the answer, because it asks what follows if the claim is true and what follows if it is false.

Claims that people argue about

A claim doesn't have to be easy to settle. The envelope can be opened in a moment, but some claims are hard to check and some are argued about for years. Two people can disagree sharply about whether a soup is too salty, and nobody calls the sentence “This soup is too salty” a question or a command.

What makes it a claim is that the speaker says how something is. Whether anyone can settle the matter, and whether anyone agrees, comes afterwards. The same holds for a claim about something hidden, such as a coin under a cup. A claim about something nobody has measured yet works the same way. Each is checked against how things are, and each has a truth value whether or not we can find it.

Logic does not decide matters of taste or fill in missing information. It starts from sentences that say how things are, and it asks what follows once their truth values are fixed. The last screen of the lesson tests the sorting on a sentence that people dispute.

If-then claims

Opener

A lock on a door comes with a rule: if the access code is correct, the door opens. Suppose someone types the right code and the door stays shut. Plainly the rule has failed. Now suppose someone types the wrong code and the door opens anyway, because a person inside has pressed the release button. Has the rule failed then?

Most people say that it has, since the door has done something the code didn't entitle it to do, and most people are wrong. The rule says what must happen when the code is right and says nothing about what happens when it is wrong. Working out exactly what an if-then claim does and doesn't say is what this lesson is for, and it is worth the effort, because a great many of the arguments we meet are built out of if-then claims.

By the end you'll name the parts of an if-then claim, say when it is false, and judge it when its condition fails.

When the condition isn't met

Now the days the promise doesn't cover. You fail the test, and Sam buys lunch anyway. He promised lunch if you passed, and you didn't pass, so the condition was never met. Whatever Sam does now, he hasn't gone against what he said. Buying you lunch is a generous extra, and it contradicts nothing.

The same goes for the day when you fail and Sam buys no lunch. He said nothing about that day either, and nobody can accuse him of breaking a promise that didn't apply. On both days the promise is untouched. In logic we call the conditional true in those cases, meaning that it has not been broken.

That is a convention, and it helps to state it plainly. A conditional gets the value false when its antecedent is true and its consequent is false. When the antecedent is false, the conditional is true whatever the consequent does.

Converse and contrapositive

Opener

Suppose a lock's manual says: if the access code is correct, the door opens. Two people reason from it. One says that the door didn't open, so the code can't have been correct. The other says that the door did open, so the code must have been correct. The two arguments look as alike as can be, yet the second is unsafe, because a person inside can open the door with the release button whatever code was typed, while the first seems to leave no room for doubt.

What separates them is the way each one turns the rule round. An if-then claim can be turned round in more than one way, and some of the ways give back the same claim in other words while others give a different claim altogether. Telling them apart is the work of this lesson.

By the end you'll write the converse and contrapositive of an if-then claim, and say which one means the same as the original.

Turning a rule round

Take the policy as it stands. “If a file is encrypted, then it needs a key” has the form p → q, with p for “the file is encrypted” and q for “the file needs a key”. The condition comes first and what follows from it comes second.

Two claims are the same only if they take the same truth value in every case

How do we decide whether two claims say the same thing? Not by how alike their wording is, but by whether they can ever disagree. If there is a case in which one is true and the other false, they are different claims, and if there is no such case, they are the same as far as logic is concerned. So we check them one case at a time. The figure begins the check for p → q and its converse q → p, with a row for each combination of p and q, and in the first row, where both are true, p → q is true and so is q → p.

[Figure]

One row of agreement settles nothing, because two quite different claims can agree in some rows. One row of disagreement settles everything, because it shows that the two can come apart. We should therefore go looking for such a row, and the natural place to start is where p and q have different values.

Swapping and negating

Swapping alone changed the meaning, so look for a way of turning the rule round that keeps it. The idea is to run the reasoning backwards. If every encrypted file needs a key, then a file with no key can't be encrypted, because had it been encrypted it would have needed one.

Written out, that is “If a file doesn't need a key, then it isn't encrypted”, which is ¬q → ¬p. Two things were done to the original. The halves were swapped, as for the converse, and each half was negated, using the ¬ from the first lesson. A rule built this way is called the contrapositive of the original.

It is worth checking that this one says the same thing, and the check is the one from before: go through the cases and compare. The next screen does the first two cases for you and leaves the other two to you.

Teaching

A rule can be used backwards through its contrapositive, never through its converse

The practical gain is that the contrapositive gives us a second way of using a rule we already hold. Because “If the code is correct, the door opens” and “If the door does not open, the code is not correct” say the same thing, we may take the rule together with a door that stayed shut and conclude that the code was wrong. The conclusion is exactly as safe as the rule itself.

The converse offers no such service. From an open door we would like to say that the code was right, but that needs the rule “If the door opens, the code is correct”, which the manual never gave us and which, as the release button showed, can be false while the manual's rule is true. So from the original rule and an open door nothing follows about the code. When you want to use a rule backwards, swap its parts and negate both, and be suspicious of any argument that swaps alone.

What the contrapositive lets you conclude

Because the contrapositive says the same thing, it gives a second way to use any rule. “If a file is encrypted, it needs a key” has the contrapositive “If a file doesn't need a key, it isn't encrypted”. A file that opens without a key must be unencrypted, and the engineer can say so with confidence.

The opposite finding gives no such confidence. A file that needs a key tells you nothing certain, because the converse, “If a file needs a key, it is encrypted”, is a different rule that nobody has said holds. The colleague who said the file must be encrypted was reading the converse into the policy. The other colleague had it right.

Of the two ways to turn a rule round, only one keeps its meaning. Swap the halves and negate both, and the meaning stays. Swap them and leave them alone, and the meaning changes.

Recap

Swapping a conditional's parts gives its converse, which can disagree with it; swapping and negating both gives its contrapositive, which always agrees.

The converse of p → q is q → p, and it is a different claim, since the two split in two of the four cases. The contrapositive, ¬q → ¬p, swaps the parts and negates both, and it takes the same value as the original in every case, so a rule can be used backwards through its contrapositive but never through its converse.

Knights and knaves

Opener

Imagine an island where everyone either always tells the truth or always lies, and you can't tell which by looking. A woman tells you, “I always tell the truth.” What is she? Someone who always tells the truth would say that, truthfully, and so would someone who always lies, falsely, so the sentence tells you nothing at all.

Change the sentence. Suppose she says of the man beside her, “He always lies.” Now something can be worked out, though perhaps not everything, and what a second person says in turn may settle the rest. The method for working it out is to suppose something and follow it until it either holds up or breaks, which is the move mathematicians use in proofs, and these islanders give us a small place to learn it.

A sound argument is valid and has only true premises

We call an argument sound when it is valid and every one of its premises is true. The whale argument is valid but not sound, since its first premise is false.

[Figure]

An argument can therefore go wrong in two separate ways. Running a rule backwards through its contrapositive is safe: that is modus tollens

There is a way of arguing backwards from a rule that cannot go wrong. If the street is dry, it did not rain, because rain would have made it wet. In letters this is “p → q; ¬q; therefore ¬p”: the rule, the denial of its consequent, and the denial of its antecedent. If p were true, the rule would force q, and q is false, so p cannot be true.

This is the contrapositive at work, and it is called modus tollens, from the Latin for “the mode that denies”. Its better-known sibling is “p → q; p; therefore q”, which reasons forwards from the condition and is called modus ponens. Both are valid, and the two fallacies are what you get by running them the wrong way round: affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are modus ponens and modus tollens turned back to front.

Not every fallacy is a pattern of if-then: some change the subject

Some fallacies can't be caught by a truth table, because what goes wrong is not the shape of the reasoning but the topic. Take “Their calculation must be wrong, because they are rude.” The conclusion is about a calculation, and the premise is about the person who made it. Rudeness has no bearing on arithmetic, since a rude person may calculate correctly and a polite one may not, so the premise gives no reason at all to accept the conclusion. We call this attacking the person.

The remedy follows from what an argument is. To answer one, examine its reasoning: look for a premise that is false, a step that doesn't follow, or a premise that has been left out. The character of the speaker matters only when the claim is about the speaker, as when we ask whether a witness was in the room, and that is not the case here.

Answering the argument, not the person

The slips so far are about the shape of the reasoning. There is another kind of slip that has nothing to do with shape, and it is answering the speaker instead of the argument. Suppose someone says, “Their sum must be wrong because they are rude.” Whether a person is rude and whether a sum is right are two separate matters, and the first gives no evidence about the second.

This is called attacking the person. It works in the flattering direction too: “Everyone admires her, so her sum must be right.” In both cases the speaker's manner, popularity or record is treated as though it were a reason. A reason that bears on the conclusion has to be about the sum itself.

To answer an argument, find the step you doubt and test it. You can recheck a calculation, look for a missing premise or search for a counterexample, as we did with the patterns above. If the argument holds up, it holds up whoever says it.

Skill check

In “p → q; q; therefore p”, which part is affirmed: the antecedent or the consequent? Answer with one word.

After a right answer: Consequent. The argument asserts q, the part after the arrow.

After a wrong answer: The antecedent is p, before the arrow. Which letter does the second premise assert?

In “p → q; ¬p; therefore ¬q”, which part is denied: the antecedent or the consequent? Answer with one word.

After a right answer: Antecedent. ¬p denies p, the part before the arrow.

After a wrong answer: The consequent is q, and the second premise denies p. Which part is that?

“If it's a bird, it has feathers. This animal has feathers, so it's a bird.” Is this reasoning valid or invalid? Answer with one word.

After a right answer: Invalid. It affirms the consequent: feathers could come from another source, so the rule doesn't give the conclusion.

After a wrong answer: The rule says what birds have. Does it say that only birds have feathers?

Recap

Reasoning from q back to p, or from not-p to not-q, is invalid, while reasoning from not-q to not-p is valid.

A fallacy is a pattern of reasoning that persuades without supporting. Of the four ways of using “p → q; …”, two are fallacies, affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent, and each has a counterexample in the row where p is false and q is true. The other two are valid, and modus tollens, which runs from ¬q to ¬p, is the contrapositive at work. Fallacies such as attacking the person change the subject, and the answer to them is to examine the reasoning.

The next time you meet an argument, find its conclusion first, then ask the two questions: does it follow, and are the premises true?
