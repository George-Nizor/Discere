About this course

What should you give up to get what you want? That is the question underneath economics, the study of choice when there is never enough to go round. Time, money and materials can each be used once, so every decision gives something up, and economists learn to count that cost and then to ask what happens when millions of people make such choices side by side.

That leads to questions most of us have wondered about. Why do prices move, what does a tax really do, and how do firms and governments behave when their choices affect each other? This course answers them with numbers instead of slogans: you will price a choice, find the price at which supply meets demand, weigh a tax, and work out how a rival is likely to respond.

There are twelve lessons in four parts. Choice and cost looks at what a decision gives up, who should make what, and how to decide one extra unit at a time. How markets set prices covers demand, supply, what moves them and how strongly buyers react. Welfare and policy asks who gains from a market, then what taxes, price controls and costs that fall on bystanders do to it. Firms and strategy covers costs and profit, monopoly and game theory. Each lesson explains first and asks afterwards, using short worked numbers.

  • You'll be able to price a choice by what it gives up, and say who should make what.
  • You'll be able to read demand and supply equations and solve them for the price where a market settles.
  • You'll be able to work out how a tax, a price control or a shift in buyers' wants changes a market.
  • You'll be able to find the output and price that maximise a firm's profit.
  • You'll be able to find the likely outcome of a simple strategic game between two players.

What it really costs

Opener

What does a £10 cinema ticket cost? The price tag says £10, and that is certainly what leaves your pocket, but it is only the money. An economist would ask a different question: what else could that £10 have done? Money and time can each be spent only once, so every purchase quietly rules something out, and the cost that matters is the thing ruled out.

That sounds like a quibble until you try to compare choices that have nothing in common, such as a film, a day's work and a new factory. Counting what each one gives up is the one measure that works for all of them, and the first step is to do it for something as ordinary as a ticket.

You have £30 for the day, and a sandwich costs £5. You spend £10 on a cinema ticket. How many sandwiches did the ticket cost you?

After a right answer: £10 would have bought 2 sandwiches, and that is the ticket's real cost in the things you can no longer have.

After a wrong answer: £10 is the price in money. The question asks for the cost in sandwiches, so divide the £10 by the £5 each sandwich costs.

After a wrong answer: Six sandwiches is the whole £30 spent on food. The ticket used only £10 of it.

After a wrong answer: £5 is the price of one sandwich. Count how many of those prices fit into the £10 the ticket took.

By the end you'll price a choice by what it gives up, and read the cost of each extra unit from a production possibilities frontier.

Teaching

The real cost of a choice is the best thing you give up

The sum you have just done priced a ticket in sandwiches rather than in pounds, and that small change of units is the idea this whole course is built on. Money is limited, so whenever you spend it on one thing you quietly decide not to spend it on another, and the price tag records only the first half of that decision. What you passed over is the other half, and we call it the opportunity cost of the choice: the value of the best alternative you gave up.

The figure shows a £20 afternoon as five £4 blocks. Spend it all on bubble tea and you have five teas. Put £8 into a paperback and the same £8 would have bought two teas, so the book's opportunity cost is two teas, and three are all that remain. We say the best alternative because you could only have done one of the other things. If £8 could have bought two teas or a snack you'd have enjoyed less, the cost is the teas, and not the teas and the snack added together.

Time works in exactly the same way, and it is often the scarcer of the two. An hour spent reading is an hour not spent on anything else, so its cost is the best use of that hour you gave up, whatever that use would have paid. Because the cost is measured in what you forgo, we can count it in any units we like, and that is what lets us price the choices of a workshop or a whole country over the next few screens.

What a choice really costs

Take an afternoon with £20 to spend, where a paperback costs £8 and a bubble tea costs £4. Buy the book and the shop takes £8, which is the price on the label. The same £8 would have bought two teas, though, and once it has gone into the book it can't buy them. So the book has a second price, which is two bubble teas.

Economists call what you give up the opportunity cost of a choice. It is the value of the best alternative you passed over, and the word best matters. Suppose the £8 could also have gone on a magazine or a bus fare. You could only have taken one of them, so the cost of the book is whichever you would really have picked instead, and the others drop out.

Money isn't the only thing that runs out. Time works the same way, since an hour spent on one job is an hour another job can't have. That brings us to a workshop, where the price of one job is counted in the output of another.

Teaching

Divide the two maximums to price one good in the other

Now put a workshop in place of a wallet. Suppose Workshop B can spend a whole week building chairs and finish 40 of them, or spend the same week building tables and finish 20. It can't do both with the same hours, so every table it builds costs it chairs, and the figure draws the two extreme weeks as the ends of a line. How many chairs is one table worth? Twenty tables take the whole week that 40 chairs would, so one table takes a twentieth of the week, and a twentieth of the week could have made 40 ÷ 20 = 2 chairs. That is the rule: the opportunity cost of one table is the most chairs divided by the most tables.

Workshop C, in the second view, can make at most 50 chairs or 10 tables. Its tables are slow to build, and the same division shows it: 50 ÷ 10 = 5 chairs for every table. Both workshops make tables, but a table displaces two chairs in one and five in the other.

The frontier shows every week the workshop could manage

So far we have looked only at the two extreme weeks, but a workshop can also split its time. Spend half the week on each and Workshop B makes 20 chairs and 10 tables, which is the marked point on its line; Workshop C, splitting its week the same way, makes 25 chairs and 5 tables. Every split lands somewhere on the straight line joining the two extremes, and we call that line the production possibilities frontier, because it marks the edge of what the workshop can make with the time it has.

[Figure]

Where a point sits relative to that edge tells you something. A point on the frontier uses the whole week. A point inside it, such as 20 chairs and 5 tables for Workshop B, leaves time unused, so the workshop could have made more of something without making less of anything else. And a point beyond the frontier would need more hours than the week contains, so no use of these resources can reach it.

On a real frontier, each step along it has its own price

Real economies don't swap one good for another at a steady rate, so their frontiers are rarely straight lines. The figure shows a country that can make food or machines, with five points marked on its frontier: 100 machines and no food at one end, and 80 units of food and no machines at the other. The price of moving from one point to the next is what you lose of one good divided by what you gain of the other, and you work it out one step at a time.

Take the first step, from no food to 20 units. Machines fall from 100 to 90, so 10 machines are given up for 20 units of food, which is 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5 machines for each unit. Hold that number in mind and switch to the straight frontier in the second view, where the price is the same everywhere: 100 ÷ 80 = 1.25 machines per unit of food.

Pricing a step along a curved frontier

So far the swap rate has been the same everywhere along the line. Many real frontiers curve. Take a country that makes food and machines. If it grows no food it can build 100 machines, and if it puts everything into farming it can grow 80 units of food and build none. Between those ends the frontier is a curve, drawn in the picture.

On a curve the price of food depends on where you stand, so we price it one short step at a time. Take the first step, from no food to 20 units. The machines drop from 100 to 90, so 10 machines are given up for 20 more units of food. Each unit of food costs 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5 machines.

The rule is the same as before, applied to a step: divide what you give up by what you gain. Next you will price a later step on the same frontier.

Teaching

On a bowed frontier each extra unit costs more than the last

Put the four step prices side by side: 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 machines for each unit of food. Every unit of food now costs more machines than the one before it, which is exactly why the frontier bows outward and doesn't run straight. We call this increasing opportunity cost, and it is the normal case rather than the exception.

Why should it happen? Because land, workers and machinery are not equally good at everything. When the country first turns towards food, it moves the resources best suited to growing it, such as fertile land, and gives up few machines. As it asks for more food it has to draw in resources that are far better at making machines than at farming, so each further unit costs more machines. If every resource were equally suited to both jobs, the frontier would be straight, as in the second view, and every unit would cost the same.

[Figure]

The rule holds from either end. The more machines the country already makes, the more food each additional machine costs it, so a country pays most for the last bit of whatever it is already making a lot of.

Teaching

A producer has a comparative advantage in the good it gives up least to make

Now compare two potters. Mo can make 3 vases or 9 bowls in a day, and Jo can make 2 vases or 4 bowls. Mo is simply better, since he makes more vases and more bowls, and we say he has an absolute advantage in both, meaning he can produce more of a good with the same time. If speed were all that mattered, Jo would have nothing to offer him.

Price the goods instead. A vase costs Mo 9 ÷ 3 = 3 bowls and costs Jo 4 ÷ 2 = 2 bowls, so Jo gives up fewer bowls for each vase he makes. Turn it over and a bowl costs Mo a third of a vase but costs Jo half of one, so Mo gives up less to make a bowl. We say a producer has a comparative advantage in a good when its opportunity cost of that good is lower than the other producer's. Jo has it in vases and Mo has it in bowls, however much better Mo is at both.

[Figure]

David Ricardo set the idea out in 1817, using English cloth and Portuguese wine. Whenever two producers' prices differ, as they do here, each has a comparative advantage in exactly one of the two goods, so nobody is left with nothing to offer. The second view, Ola and Pat, shows it again with different numbers.

Being quicker and being cheaper

Now put a second potter next to the first. Jo can make 4 bowls or 2 vases in a day, while the first potter, Mo, makes 9 bowls or 3 vases. Mo is quicker at both jobs. Being able to make more of a good with the same time is called an absolute advantage, and Mo has it in bowls and in vases.

It looks as if Mo should do everything, but look at the prices. A vase costs Mo 3 bowls and costs Jo 4 ÷ 2 = 2 bowls. Jo gives up fewer bowls for each vase, and giving up less is what makes a job cheap. Whoever has the lower opportunity cost of a good has the comparative advantage in it, so Jo has the comparative advantage in vases.

Bowls work the other way round. A bowl costs Mo a third of a vase and costs Jo half of one, so Mo has the comparative advantage in bowls. Between two people, each ends up with one of the two jobs, even though one of them is quicker at both.

The market settles where the two sets of plans match

Put the two sets of plans into one picture, with demand Qd = 60 − 5P and supply Qs = 5P. At a price of £4, buyers plan to buy 40 tubs and sellers plan to offer only 20, so 20 would-be buyers go without. We call that gap a shortage, and it pushes the price up, since disappointed buyers will pay more to be served. At £8 it is the other way round: buyers want 20, sellers offer 40, and the 20 tubs left on the shelf are a surplus, which pushes the price down as sellers cut it to clear their stock.

Somewhere between the two the pressures cancel. At £6, buyers plan to buy 60 − 5 × 6 = 30 tubs and sellers plan to offer 5 × 6 = 30, so the plans match, nobody is turned away and nobody is left with stock. There is then no reason for the price to move, and we call it the equilibrium price, with 30 tubs as the equilibrium quantity.

Teaching

Compare percentage changes, measured against the midpoint

A measure of responsiveness should not depend on units. Thirty more snacks is a lot for a stall and nothing for a supermarket, so we compare percentage changes. Percentages have a snag, though. A price that falls from £5 to £4 has fallen by 20% of its starting value, while one that rises from £4 to £5 has risen by 25%, so the same two prices give two different percentages depending on the direction.

The fix is to measure each change against the average of the start and the end, which is called the midpoint method. Take a concert ticket whose price falls from £11 to £9: the change is £2, the average of the two prices is £10, and the change is 2 ÷ 10 = 20% of the midpoint, whichever way you read it. Do the same for the number sold, which rises from 35 to 65: the change is 30, the average is 50, and 30 ÷ 50 = 60%.

What a ticket is worth to you

Every buyer has a highest price they would pay for a thing, and a price above it sends them home empty-handed. That limit is their willingness to pay. It belongs to the buyer and not to the shop. Sellers gain the gap between the price and their cost

Sellers have a mirror-image limit. So a sale is good news on both sides, and the price decides only how the gain is shared. The gain from one sale is what the buyer values the thing at, minus what it costs the seller, whatever the price. The market's total gain is largest at equilibrium

Add the two triangles and you have the total surplus, the whole gain from trade in a market: 90 + 90 = £180 for the ice cream. Why the market's own quantity is the best one

Consumer surplus and producer surplus are the two shares of the gain from trade. Added together they make total surplus, the whole gain a market produces. How the total is split depends on the price. How large it is depends on which trades take place.

A trade adds to the total whenever the buyer's willingness to pay is above the seller's cost. A lolly that a buyer values at £9 and that costs £4 to make creates £5 of value, and the price only decides how that £5 is shared. A trade where the cost is above the value would use up more than it creates.

Equilibrium is the quantity where the last trade is only just worth making. Up to it, every lolly has a buyer who values it above its cost, so every one of those trades happens. Beyond it, the 31st lolly would cost sellers about £6.20 to make and be worth only £5.80 to the next buyer. That trade would shrink the total, and the market does not make it.

A floor above the market price

A price floor works from the other side. A floor above the market price binds, because it holds the price up. Costs that move and costs that don't

Costs fall into two kinds. A fixed cost is one the firm pays whatever it makes, such as rent. A variable cost grows with output, such as paper, flour or wages paid by the hour. Adding the two gives total cost, the full bill for a given amount of output.

Take a print shop that pays £50 rent a day and spends £1 on paper and ink for every poster. If it prints nothing, its total cost is still the £50 of rent. If it prints 20 posters, the variable cost is 20 × £1 = £20 and the total cost is £50 + £20 = £70.

The difference matters when a firm makes decisions. The rent has to be paid whatever the firm chooses, but each extra poster brings a cost with it. A sensible choice about how much to print looks at the costs the choice itself changes, and the next two screens turn that idea into two measures.

Divide the total cost by the number of units and you have the average total cost, the cost of a typical unit: £4 a loaf for the bakery at 30 loaves. It does not stay there. At 60 loaves the bakery's total cost is £60 + 60 × £2 = £180, so the average falls to £3, because the rent is now shared among twice as many loaves.

It cannot fall for ever. Take a small workshop with rent of £36 whose variable cost grows faster than its output, because a crowded workshop slows down: its total cost is TC = 36 + q². At 2 units the average is 40 ÷ 2 = £20, at 4 units it is 52 ÷ 4 = £13, at 6 units 72 ÷ 6 = £12, at 9 units 117 ÷ 9 = £13, and at 12 units 180 ÷ 12 = £15.

The cost of one more

Average cost smooths everything into one number per unit. A firm deciding whether to make one more unit needs something sharper. Marginal cost is the extra cost of making that one more unit, which is the change in total cost when output goes up by one.

In the workshop, going from 5 to 6 units lifts total cost from 36 + 25 = £61 to £72, so the sixth unit has a marginal cost of £11. The seventh takes it from £72 to £85, which is £13. Marginal cost rises with output here, and the line MC = 2q runs through the middle of those two figures, at £12 for 6 units. This course uses MC = 2q for the workshop.

Marginal cost and average cost are linked. If the next unit costs less than the average so far, adding it pulls the average down. If it costs more, it pulls the average up. At 2 units the marginal cost is about £4 against an average of £20, so the average is being pulled down. At 12 units it is about £24 against £15, so the average is being pulled up.

The average stops falling at the output where marginal cost has risen to meet it. For the workshop that is 6 units, where marginal cost is £12 and so is the average.

A price-taker makes units until its marginal cost reaches the price

Now the firm's real choice: how much to make? Suppose the firm is small in a large market, so it must take whatever price the market sets and cannot change it by selling more. We call it a price-taker. For a price-taker, every extra unit sold brings in exactly the market price, so its marginal revenue, the extra revenue one more unit adds, is just the price.

That lets us use the rule from 'The next unit': make one more while its marginal revenue is greater than its marginal cost, and stop where they meet. For a price-taker this means producing the output at which price equals marginal cost, P = MC.

Now the choice itself. A firm in a very large market cannot move the price by changing its own output, so it takes the price as given. Such a firm is called a price-taker. A single seller chooses its price, and demand sets how many it sells

Suppose one company runs the only ferry across a river, with no bridge or rival boat to compete with it. What one more passenger adds

Suppose she charges £40, so 10 passengers cross and she takes in 10 × £40 = £400. To fill an eleventh seat she must drop the price to £39. An equilibrium is a pair of choices in which neither player gains by switching alone

A dominant strategy is a strong condition, and many games do not have one. A more general idea asks about a pair of choices: is each one a best response to the other? In the café game, open late for both is an equilibrium: if Ruby alone switched to closing early she would earn 3 instead of 6, and Sol would lose in the same way.

The trouble is not stupidity. Repetition can make cooperation pay

The dilemma assumes the firms meet just once, but real firms meet again next week. In a repeated game the same players play the same game several times, and each can now let its move depend on what happened before, which gives it a way to reward and a way to punish. The simplest such plan is tit for tat: cooperate in the first round, and after that copy whatever the rival did last round. In computer tournaments of the repeated dilemma that the political scientist Robert Axelrod ran around 1980, tit for tat, entered by Anatol Rapoport, scored best.

Take the cafés over three nights, with closing early as cooperating and Ruby playing tit for tat. If he cooperates throughout, both close early and he earns 8 × 3 = 24 as well. Exploiting Ruby gains him nothing over three nights, and over four it loses him ground, with 12 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 30 against 8 × 4 = 32.
