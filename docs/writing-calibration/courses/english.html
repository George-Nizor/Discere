Inside a clause

Opener

Here is a line from Shakespeare's Sonnet 18: “And every fair from fair sometime declines.” Read it twice and you may feel you understand it without being able to say how, because the words come in an order nobody would use in conversation. Which word says what happens, and which says to whom, or to what, it happens?

There is a reliable way to find out, and it does not depend on word order or on how a line sounds. Every sentence is built around a small core, a verb and the person or thing it is about, and once you can find that core you can read a tangled line of verse, or a tangled sentence of your own, by working outwards from it. This lesson teaches you how to find the core, how to tell what is attached to it, and how to tell whether a group of words can stand alone.

Teaching

A finite verb is the word that changes when the time changes

How do you find the verb in a sentence? The usual answer, that it is the word for an action, fails quickly: in “She is tired” nothing is happening, and yet “is” is the verb, while a noun like “collapse” names an action without being a verb at all. A better test is to move the whole sentence to another time and watch which word has to change. Take “She opens the window” and say it about yesterday, and you get “She opened the window”, where every word has stayed as it was except one.

The word that moved is the one that carries the time of the sentence, and we call it the finite verb, finite in the sense of limited, because the time (and, in a word like “opens”, the person acting) pins down its form. Some forms of a verb never change with the time, “to open” and “opening” for example, and the figure shows them standing still while the finite verb moves. Those forms cannot be the verb of a sentence on their own, which is why the finite verb is the one to look for.

The test asks only which word changes with the time, so it works on any sentence in prose or in verse, whatever order the words come in. It is the first thing to try on a line you cannot make sense of.

The word that changes with time

The exercise in the opening works because English marks time on a single word. Take “She opens the window” and move it into the past: “She opened the window.” The word “opens” changed and nothing else did. Go the other way, into the present, and the same word is the only one that has to move.

A verb that changes like this is called a finite verb. “Finite” means limited: the verb is tied to a particular time, and often to a particular person too, as in “I open” and “she opens”. Every complete statement has one at its centre. Without one you have only a label, such as “the window”. Nothing has been said about what happens.

That gives you a test that works on any sentence, no matter how oddly it is arranged. Put the sentence into a different time and watch which word has to change. Forms such as “to open” or “opening” never change with time, so they are not finite verbs, a difference we will need again before the end.

Who the verb belongs to

Finding the verb is half the job. A verb needs someone or something to carry out the action, and asking who or what does it gives you the second half. Take “She opened the window”. Ask who opened, and the answer is “she”. That word is the subject.

The subject and its finite verb are enough to say something complete: “She opened” tells you that someone did something at a particular time. A group of words built on a subject and its finite verb is a clause. Everything else in a sentence hangs on a clause, adding detail about when, where, how or why. Those added words and phrases are modifiers.

The figure sets Shakespeare's line beside a plain one. The words are shuffled, yet the same two anchors appear, “declines” and “every fair”, with the modifiers hanging off them. In ordinary prose the line would say that every beautiful thing at some time falls away from its beauty. Next comes what can follow the verb.

What follows the verb is either an object or a complement

Many clauses go on past the verb. Compare “The wind rattled the shutters” with “The wind was cold”. In the first, the shutters are what the wind acts on, and the word for whatever receives the action is the object. In the second, “was” describes no action at all; it links the wind to a description, and the word that completes the link is a complement. Verbs that do this job, such as “was”, “seems” and “became”, are called linking verbs.

So the question to ask is about the verb: does something receive its action, or does the verb only say what the subject is or is like? The figure lets you set the two clauses side by side.

[Figure]

Notice that the job a word does depends on the verb in front of it, and not on the kind of word it is. “Cold” is an adjective, but a noun can be a complement as well: in “She became a doctor”, “a doctor” describes her. And when an object or complement runs to several words, one noun in it is the head noun, the word the others describe, as the next example shows.

What follows the verb

Some clauses stop after the verb, as in “The dog barked”. Others carry on, and what comes next depends on the verb. Some verbs pass their action to something. In “The wind rattled the shutters”, the shutters are what get rattled. A word or phrase that receives the action of the verb is an object.

Other verbs, such as “was” and “seems”, pass nothing on. They link the subject to a description. In “The wind was cold”, nothing is done to the cold. The word tells you what the wind is like, and a word that does that after a linking verb is a complement.

To tell them apart, ask what the word after the verb does. If it receives the action, it is an object. If it says what the subject is or is like, it is a complement. Either can be longer than one word. The subject of “The tall oak by the gate fell” is built round one noun, “oak”. The other words describe it. The noun at the centre of a phrase like this is its head noun. An object can even be a whole clause: in “She remembered what he had said”, the clause “what he had said” is what she remembered.

A clause that can stand alone is independent; one that cannot is dependent

So far every clause we have looked at could be a sentence by itself, but that is not always so. In “We stayed inside because it was raining”, both “we stayed inside” and “it was raining” have a subject and a finite verb, and so both are clauses, yet only the first makes sense alone. The second begins with “because”, a word that makes the clause lean on another one for its meaning. A clause that can stand alone is an independent clause, a clause that cannot is a dependent clause, and the little word that makes it dependent is a subordinator. Others you will meet are “when”, “if”, “although” and “which”.

Switch the figure to its second view and you will see the opposite case, “The tide came in and the beach vanished”, where “and” joins two clauses that could each be a sentence. “And” joins them without making either depend on the other.

[Figure]

A dependent clause usually answers a question about its partner: why did we stay inside? because it was raining. That is the sense in which it depends on the other clause, and it is why a dependent clause on its own feels unfinished.

Clauses that cannot stand alone

So far every clause could stand as a sentence on its own. “The dog barked” could, and so could “She opened the window”. A clause like that is independent. Some clauses have a subject and a finite verb and still cannot stand alone.

Add one small word at the front and a clause starts to lean. “We stayed inside” is complete. “Because it was raining” has a subject, “it”, and a finite verb, “was raining”, yet it leaves you waiting to hear what followed from the rain. A word such as “because”, “when”, “if” or “which” that makes a clause lean on another is a subordinator, and a clause it opens is a dependent clause.

A dependent clause lets a writer attach a reason or a condition to the main statement, and a long sentence can stack several. What it never does is stand alone. Take away the clause it leans on and the sentence is left hanging.

Teaching

Counting finite verbs counts clauses

Every clause has exactly one finite verb, so counting finite verbs counts clauses, provided you count the right words. Compare “Anna decided to leave when she saw the rain” with “Walking home, Mia hummed a tune”. In the first, “decided” and “saw” are finite, since in the present they would become “decides” and “sees”, whereas “to leave” is an infinitive, a verb form that stays the same whatever the time. In the second, “walking” is a participle, another form that does not move with the time, so “hummed” is the only finite verb and the sentence holds one clause.

Two cautions help when you count in a real text. A verb phrase such as “do shake” or “was raining” counts once, because the helping word carries the time and the whole phrase acts as one verb. And when you doubt a candidate, run the time test on it, since a word that cannot change with the time cannot be a finite verb.

One finite verb for each clause

Every clause is built on one finite verb, so counting the finite verbs counts the clauses. It is a quick way to see how a long sentence is put together. In “Anna decided to leave when she saw the rain”, the finite verbs are “decided” and “saw”. That makes two clauses: “Anna decided to leave” and “when she saw the rain”.

Take care with verb forms that carry no tense. “To leave” is an infinitive, the plain form of a verb, usually with “to” in front. A participle is a form such as “walking” that adds detail without fixing a time. In “Walking home, Mia hummed a tune”, “walking” is a participle and “hummed” is the only finite verb. Neither infinitives nor participles change with time the way a finite verb does, so neither counts.

Counting this way is the fastest check on a long poetic sentence. Verse often runs one sentence across several lines, with clauses folded inside clauses, and the finite verbs show where each one is anchored.

Joining two statements

Two complete statements side by side raise a small problem, because the writer must decide what goes between them. Each is an independent clause, in the last lesson's words, so each could stand as a sentence. “The shop closed” is one and “We went home” is another.

The plainest choice is a full stop: “The shop closed. We went home.” The reader gets two sentences and works out the connection alone. To make one sentence, the writer needs a joiner. The usual one is a comma followed by a coordinating conjunction, a short word that links clauses of equal weight, such as “and”, “but”, “so”, “or” and “yet”. Each names a relation. “And” adds. “But” sets one against the other. “So” gives a result.

A comma on its own is too light for the job. “The shop closed, we went home” runs two complete statements together with nothing but a comma between them. Editors call this a comma splice, and in formal writing it counts as an error. Some writers splice on purpose. Frederick Douglass told a Fourth of July audience in Rochester in 1852, “You may rejoice, I must mourn”. The abruptness makes the two fates collide.

Opener

A passive can leave the actor out altogether

The passive has a second feature that matters even more than its length, which is that the “by” phrase is optional. “The office was closed by the manager” can lose its ending and become “The office was closed”, a complete and grammatical sentence in which nobody has closed anything. What an argument is made of

An argument is an attempt to get someone to accept a conclusion by giving them a reason. Qualifiers and rebuttals say how far a claim can be trusted

Real arguments rarely claim certainty, and Toulmin gave two parts to the ways they hedge. A qualifier is a word or phrase such as “probably” or “in most cases” that states how strongly the claim is made. Limiting a claim

A careful speaker admits the limits of a claim before an opponent can point them out, and there are two ways to do it. Take “The ferry will in most cases sail on time, unless fog closes the harbour.” The qualifier is “in most cases” and the rebuttal is “unless fog closes the harbour”. Neither weakens the argument. A speaker persuades through character, feeling and reasoning

More than two thousand years ago Aristotle, in his Rhetoric, asked what a speaker can do to persuade, and found that the speech itself offers three means. The first is the audience's sense of the speaker, which he called ethos, the Greek word for character and the root of our word ethics: an audience believes a speaker it trusts. The second is the feeling the speech stirs, which he called pathos, the root of sympathy: an audience is moved to act when it is moved to feel. The third is the reasoning, which he called logos, a word that means both word and reason: an audience is persuaded when the case makes sense.

Take a meeting about a bus route. A speaker who opens “I have ridden the number 9 to work for thirty years” is building ethos, because she has told you why you should listen to her. “Picture your own child waiting at the school gate in the dark” is pathos, and “Three of the last five buses ran late and the timetable has not changed, so it will run late again” is logos. Kairos is the fit between a speech and its moment

Aristotle's three appeals describe how a speech persuades, but they leave out when and where it is given. Antithesis sets opposed ideas side by side in matching form

Notice what else Dickens does. “It was the best of times, it was the worst of times” has a repeated frame, but the two endings are opposites, and the matching shape makes the opposition easy to see. Putting contrasting ideas in parallel form is called antithesis, from a Greek word meaning a setting against. Neil Armstrong's line from the Moon on 20 July 1969 does it too, as it is commonly quoted: “That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.”

In both examples the two halves have the same shape, and that shape tells the reader to compare them. The comparison is the point, since a small step and a giant leap are meant to be seen together. An antithesis, then, makes a choice or a contrast vivid, and it is a favourite device of anyone who has to say that two things differ.

Opposites in a matching frame

Chiasmus repeats its terms in reverse order

Some balanced sentences do more than set two things side by side: they turn the words round. John F. Kennedy said in his inaugural address on 20 January 1961, “ask not what your country can do for you—ask what you can do for your country.” The key terms come in the order country, you in the first half and you, country in the second. A figure in which the second half repeats the terms of the first in reverse order, A B then B A, is called chiasmus, after the Greek letter chi, which is shaped like an X, because the terms cross.

The reversal is more than decoration, because it changes the relation. In Kennedy's sentence the country is first the giver and then the receiver, and the reversal states the whole argument of the speech in a single turn. A proverb works the same way: “You can take the boy out of the country, but you can't take the country out of the boy”. A strict name for a chiasmus that repeats the very same words is antimetabole, which you will see in some books.

The figures overlap, so the way to read a passage is to ask what is repeated and what changes, and then what the shape does for the argument.

Teaching

An iamb is a weak syllable followed by a strong one

The commonest pattern in English poetry since the sixteenth century is a weak syllable followed by a strong one, as in “com-PARE”. A pair like that is called an iamb, and the unit in which verse is measured, here one weak syllable with its strong neighbour, is called a foot. Five iambs make a line of ten syllables with the beats on every second syllable, and a line of five feet is a pentameter, so the whole pattern is iambic pentameter: da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM.

Shakespeare wrote his sonnets and most of his plays in it, and so did Milton in his sonnets. In Sonnet 18, “Thou art more lovely and more temperate”, a beat falls on every second syllable, and Romeo's “But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?” does the same (Romeo and Juliet, 2.2). Many critics have noted that the pattern is close to the natural rhythm of English speech, which may be why it lasted.

Teaching

Scansion marks where the beats fall, and the pattern is a frame

To read the metre of a line, you mark which syllables carry the beats, and the marking is called scansion. The pattern is a frame laid over the words, and the words do not always fit it as speech would have them. In “Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?”, the beats fall on I, pare, to, sum and day, and an ordinary speaker would hardly stress “I” or “to” at all. The frame asks us to give those syllables a little more weight than speech would.

That is the reason scansion is a matter of judgement as well as counting. A stress is only ever stronger than its neighbours, and the same word can be strong in one line and weak in the next. If the first syllable count comes out wrong, the beats will not fall where the pattern needs them, and so the syllable count always comes first.

Reading against the pattern

With the pattern in hand you can mark up any line. The marking is called scansion. You write out the syllables and decide which are strong, then you compare them with the pattern. In a regular line of iambic pentameter the beats fall on the even-numbered syllables, the second, fourth, sixth, eighth and tenth. The odd-numbered ones are the weak syllables between them.

A line may end on an extra weak syllable

Not every line has exactly ten syllables. Macbeth says, in the fifth act, “To-morrow, and to-morrow, and to-morrow”, and when you tap it out you get to-MOR-row and to-MOR-row and to-MOR-row, which comes to eleven. The last syllable, “-row”, is weak, and it comes after the final beat. A weak syllable that follows the last beat in this way is called a feminine ending, in the traditional name for it, and a line that ends on its final beat has a masculine ending.

Notice what the extra syllable does. The line stops stressing and then trails off, and a speaker like Macbeth, who is running out of reasons to go on, is well served by a line that trails off. The regular pentameter is still there underneath, since the first ten syllables are an ordinary pattern of iambs, and the eleventh is simply added.

Alliteration repeats a consonant sound at the start of nearby words

Say “Betty Botter bought some butter, but she said the butter's bitter” and your lips will tell you what the line is doing: it repeats the same opening sound, b, in word after word. Repeating a consonant sound at the beginning of nearby words is called alliteration. Keats begins “To Autumn” with “Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness”, where the soft m sounds fall on the two words that matter most to the picture. Teaching

A rhyme scheme names the sounds that end the lines

Two words rhyme when their endings sound alike from the last stressed vowel onwards, and it is the sound that counts and not the spelling: “night” and “write” rhyme, though they look unlike. To describe how a poem rhymes, we give each ending sound a letter, starting with A for the first, and each time a new sound appears we use the next letter. Here is Jane Taylor's “Twinkle, twinkle, little star” (1806): star, are, high, sky gives A A B B, because the first two lines share a sound and so do the last two. The string of letters is the poem's rhyme scheme.

Lines are often grouped into small units, and a group of four lines is a quatrain, from the Latin for four. Sonnet 18 opens with a quatrain that ends day, temperate, May, date, and its scheme is therefore A B A B, since the first and third lines share a sound and so do the second and fourth.

Giving rhymes letters

Two words rhyme when they end on the same sound, as day and way do. In a poem the rhymes sit at the ends of lines, and the way they are arranged is the poem's rhyme scheme. To write a scheme down, give each new end-sound a letter in order of appearance and reuse the letter whenever the sound comes back. The first line ends on a sound we call A. If the second line ends on a different sound, that is B. If the third line rhymes with the first, it is A again.

Thomas Gray's “Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard” begins: “The curfew tolls the knell of parting day, / The lowing herd wind slowly o'er the lea, / The plowman homeward plods his weary way, / And leaves the world to darkness and to me.” The line endings are day, lea, way and me. Day and way rhyme, and lea and me rhyme, so the scheme reads ABAB. A group of four lines like this is called a quatrain, from the French word for four. Each of Gray's lines is also iambic pentameter, the ten-syllable line you met in the last lesson.

Once the endings have letters you can compare patterns at a glance. ABAB means that lines one and three rhyme with each other and lines two and four rhyme with each other. A line that rhymes with nothing takes a letter of its own.

The Italian form

Shakespeare's pattern is not the only one. The sonnet began in Italy in the 1200s, and the poet Petrarch, writing in the 1300s, made it famous. His pattern splits the fourteen lines into two unequal parts. The first eight lines are the octave and the last six are the sestet. The octave sets out a problem or a feeling, and the sestet answers it.

The octave's rhyme scheme is strict: ABBAABBA. The sound that ends line 1 comes back in lines 4, 5 and 8, and the sound that ends line 2 comes back in lines 3, 6 and 7. Italian has many words that end alike, so the pattern is manageable there. English has fewer, so an English poet who keeps to it is working hard. John Milton does in his sonnet on going blind, which begins “When I consider how my light is spent”. The sestet that follows is looser, and poets have several patterns for it.

The two forms divide the fourteen lines differently. Shakespeare's runs as three groups of four and a pair, and Petrarch's as a group of eight and a group of six. Who sees and who speaks

Opener

Here are two sentences from an invented story: “Ben opened the oven. The cake was a disaster!” The exclamation mark gives the second one away, because a person telling us about a cake from outside would hardly be so upset by it, so the feeling must be Ben's. Yet there are no quotation marks and no “Ben thought”, and the sentence is in the past tense, as the first one is.

Whose voice are we hearing, the one that tells the story or the one that lives in it? Novelists have a precise set of tools for letting the two voices mix, and this lesson teaches you to see them, starting with the question every reader should ask first, which is who is telling the story.

By the end you'll be able to say who is telling a story and from where, hear an omniscient narrator's irony, and recognise a character's thought inside the narration.

Previous text

Two friends are telling you about the same awkward entrance. Yesterday one of them walked into a meeting twenty minutes late, and the whole room turned to look. The first friend says: “I walked in and everyone went quiet.” The second, who watched from the corner, says: “She walked in and everyone went quiet.”

The event is identical and the two accounts feel different. The first puts you inside the person who walked in, feeling the heat in her face. The second puts you across the room, watching her. A story always comes from somewhere, and where the teller stands decides what you can see, what you can know and how far you can trust it.

This lesson is about that choice, which writers call point of view. It starts with the simplest question, who is telling, and works up to a trick Jane Austen used to let readers hear a character's thoughts without leaving the voice of the person telling the story.

By the end you'll tell first- from third-person narration and spot free indirect discourse in Austen.

Teaching

Every story has a narrator, and the narrator has a person

Every story is told by someone, and that someone is the narrator. The narrator is not the author. It is a voice that the author has chosen, and the first thing that voice tells you, often without saying so, is how it stands to the events. A narrator who is also a character in the story and says “I” writes in the first person. Edgar Allan Poe's “The Tell-Tale Heart” begins, “True!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad?”, and the narrator is both telling the tale and living in it.

A narrator who stays outside the story and refers to the characters as he, she and they writes in the third person. Jane Austen opens Persuasion in that way: “Sir Walter Elliot, of Kellynch Hall, in Somersetshire, was a man who, for his own amusement, never took up any book but the Baronetage”. The test for the person of a narrator is simply whether the narrator says “I” about their own part in the story.

Who is telling the story

The person who tells a story is its narrator, and the first thing to settle about any narrator is where they stand. Go back to the two friends. The one who says “I walked in” is inside the story and is one of its characters. Narration like this is in the first person, because the pronouns are I and we. Edgar Allan Poe's narrator in “The Tell-Tale Heart” works this way: “why will you say that I am mad?”

The friend who says “she walked in” stands outside, watching. That is the third person, because the people in the story are he, she and they. Jane Austen's Persuasion opens this way: “Sir Walter Elliot, of Kellynch Hall, in Somersetshire, was a man who, for his own amusement, never took up any book but the Baronetage.” Nobody says “I” there, and the narrator is not one of the Elliots. A rarer choice is the second person, which speaks to the reader as “you”, as in “You walk in and everyone goes quiet”.

The pronouns give the quickest test. Look at the words a narrator uses for themselves and for the characters. If the narrator says I or we, they are inside the story. If they never mention themselves and call the characters he, she or they, they stand outside it.

Teaching

A third-person narrator may know more than any character

A third-person narrator is not bound to see what a character sees. Take the sentence “Everyone in Little Wick knew that the new vicar would not last the winter, though he himself was sure he would.” The narrator knows what the whole village thinks, and also knows what the vicar thinks, and sets the two side by side, which no single person in the village could do. A narrator who knows the thoughts of all the characters, and the whole of the story, is called omniscient, from the Latin for all-knowing.

That wide knowledge lets an omniscient narrator do something a character cannot, which is to comment. The narrator can tell us what is true as against what the characters suppose, and can let us see the gap between them. A narrator who knows everything

A third-person narrator can stay close to one character or stand well back. The widest position belongs to the omniscient narrator, from the Latin for “all-knowing”. An omniscient narrator can see into every mind, say what happened before the story began and comment on what it all means. Take an invented line: “Everyone in Little Wick knew that the new vicar would not last the winter, though he himself was sure he would.” The narrator knows what a whole village thinks and what the vicar believes, and the two do not match.

That wide view lets a narrator speak as though for everyone while quietly standing apart. A narrator can state a belief that belongs to a community and let the reader notice that the narrator does not share it. The sentence reports the village's certainty in the village's confident voice, and its second half shows how little the vicar knows. The tone is dry. That dryness is a kind of irony, where the words say one thing and the situation suggests another, and a later lesson looks at it properly.

An omniscient narrator is not always the author speaking plainly. It can voice a community's assumption, state a general truth with a straight face and leave the reader to judge. When a novel opens with a confident statement about what everybody knows, ask whose belief it is.

Free indirect discourse lets a character's voice speak inside the narrator's

There are three ways to report what a character thinks. In direct speech the thought appears in quotation marks, in the character's own words: “This cake is a disaster!” In indirect speech the narrator reports it with a verb of thinking: Ben thought that the cake was a disaster. Slipping into a character's head

A narrator has a choice about how to report what a character thinks. Suppose Ben opens the oven and finds that his cake has sunk. The simplest way is to quote him in his own words and his own person: “This cake is a disaster!” That is direct speech, and the quotation marks show that the words belong to Ben. Another way is to report the thought from outside: Ben thought that the cake was a disaster. Now the narrator is in charge, and the thought arrives wrapped in “Ben thought that”.

There is a third way, which sits between the two. The narrator keeps the third person and the past tense but lets Ben's own exclamation into the sentence, and drops the reporting words: The cake was a disaster! No quotation marks and no “thought that” announce the change. The voice is the narrator's and the feeling is Ben's. Writers call this free indirect discourse. Words that only the character would use, such as an exclamation or a bit of slang, give it away.

Turning a direct thought into this form takes two changes. The person moves from I to he or she, and the tense moves back a step, so that “I have no time!” becomes “She had no time!” The exclamation and the choice of words stay the character's own. Because nothing signals the switch, the reader slides into the character's mind without noticing the join.

Teaching

A single paragraph can mix narration, speech and thought

Novels move between these modes without announcing it. In one paragraph a narrator may report what a character does, quote what she says, and drop into what she thinks, and a reader who knows the three marks can tell which is which. Plain narration reports actions from outside: “Mia hung up her coat.” Direct speech is in quotation marks: “Where have you been?” asked her father. A sentence in the character's idiom with the narrator's person and tense is free indirect: “Honestly, what a day it had been!”

The sorting rule has three questions. Are there quotation marks, and if so it is direct speech. Does the sentence report an action in the narrator's own voice, and if so it is narration. Does it carry a character's exclamation or opinion in the third person and past tense, with nothing to say who is thinking, and if so it is free indirect discourse.

Sorting the modes

Any line of a narrative can be sorted with two checks: who is speaking, and whose words they are. Quotation marks mark direct speech, where the words are the character's own. Actions and facts reported from outside, with no character's voice in them, are plain narration. The case in between, where the narrator's grammar carries a character's voice, is free indirect discourse. In the figure, Mia hangs up her coat, her father asks where she has been, and then comes a line in her voice but the narrator's grammar.

The effect matters as much as the label. In free indirect discourse the reader hears a character's mind with no narrator standing between. When the character is mistaken, the reader can take on the mistake along with them. Austen uses this in Emma, where we share Emma's confident reading of other people and discover only later that she has misjudged them.

A narrator who can be inside a mind and outside it at once can show how a character sees things and how things are. The gap between the two is where a writer can be funny or unsettling, and it leads into the next lesson.

A narrator's person and knowledge shape a story, and free indirect discourse lets a character's voice speak inside the narrator's third person.

A narrator writes in the first person when it is also a character and says “I”, and in the third person when it stays outside. A third-person narrator who knows everything is omniscient, and can comment on what the characters do not know.

Free indirect discourse lets a character's own exclamation or idiom enter the narration in the third person and past tense with no reporting verb, so that you hear the character without quotation marks.

Irony and unreliable narrators

Opener

When Mark Antony tells the Roman crowd that “Brutus is an honourable man”, he is speaking at Caesar's funeral, and he has promised Brutus he will say nothing against the men who killed Caesar. By the end of the scene the crowd is calling for those men's blood. Antony has not said a word against Brutus.

How can a speaker say one thing and make an audience hear its opposite? The device has a name, and in fact three varieties, depending on whether the gap lies in a speaker's words, in what an audience knows, or in how events turn out.

By the end you'll be able to tell verbal, dramatic and situational irony apart, and spot a narrator whose own account gives them away.

Teaching

Irony is a gap between what seems to be so and what is

Suppose a customer has just had a bowl of soup land in his lap and says, “Lovely, thank you.” His words say that the soup was lovely, and everyone in the room knows that he means nothing of the kind. That gap between what is said and what is meant is the heart of irony, and when it lies in a speaker's own words the figure is verbal irony: the speaker says one thing and means something close to its opposite, relying on the listener to see it.

How does the listener see it? From the context, since the soup in his lap is what shows that the words cannot be meant straight, and often from tone as well. On the page there is no tone, so a writer has to put the context in the scene, as the figure does for two invented speakers. In each case the line is true as a sentence and false as a meaning, and the distance between the two is where the irony lives.

A remark that means the opposite

Go back to the customer with soup in his lap. What he said was “Lovely, thank you.” What he meant was close to the reverse. When someone says one thing and means another, and the listener can hear the difference, we call the result irony. This first kind lives in the speaker’s words, so it is called verbal irony.

The difference only counts if it can be heard. A stranger who knew nothing about the soup would take the remark at face value, and the joke would vanish. What makes it audible is the scene, since everyone present can see the stain and so can measure how far the words fall from the facts. Tone of voice helps, though a page has no tone and there the scene is all a reader has.

So verbal irony needs two things, a statement that points one way and a situation that points the other. Take either away and what is left is a plain remark. That suggests a question about how far the idea can stretch. A single sentence can set up a gap, and a speaker could also build one slowly, over a whole speech. The next screen follows one who did.

Praise that turns on itself

Verbal irony does not have to stay inside one sentence. Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar has one of the best-known examples. Caesar has been murdered by a group of Roman senators led by Brutus, who tells the crowd that Caesar was ambitious and had to die for Rome’s sake. Mark Antony, Caesar’s friend, is allowed to speak at the funeral on one condition: he must not speak against the killers. So he does not. He praises them.

Antony keeps coming back to one phrase. Brutus says Caesar was ambitious, he tells the crowd, “and Brutus is an honourable man.” Then he offers some facts. Caesar brought captives home whose ransoms filled Rome’s treasury, and when a crown was held out to him he refused it three times. After each fact, the phrase returns.

The words never change, yet they come to mean something else. The first time, “honourable” sounds like respect. After the ransoms and the refused crown it sounds like an accusation, and the crowd supplies the meaning. Antony never calls Brutus a liar. The evidence builds the gap between what he says and what the crowd now believes, and the repetition makes sure nobody misses it. In the lines that follow, see how often he returns to the phrase.

Dramatic irony: the audience knows what a character does not

A different kind of irony does not depend on what anyone says, but on what the audience knows. When a character speaks in ignorance of something the audience has been shown, the character's words carry a meaning he cannot hear. This is dramatic irony, so called because it flourishes in plays, though it works in films and novels too. Take a retelling in which Little Red Riding Hood says, “What big teeth you have!” The line is an innocent remark about her grandmother, and for us who have watched the wolf swallow the grandmother, it is a line of terror.

When the audience knows more

Verbal irony needs a speaker who means the opposite. There is another kind of gap that needs no such speaker, and a story most people know shows it. Little Red Riding Hood reaches her grandmother’s bed and says, “What big teeth you have!” She is making a polite remark about her grandmother. The audience knows that the figure in the bed is the wolf, so every word she speaks carries a meaning she cannot hear.

That is dramatic irony. The audience, or the reader, knows something that a character does not, and the character’s words or actions take on a meaning the character cannot see. The name comes from drama, because the stage is where it works best. A theatre audience has watched the whole story and sits in the dark knowing more than anyone on stage, and a playwright can use that edge by letting a hero walk cheerfully towards a trap.

Nobody in Red Riding Hood’s scene says the opposite of what they mean. The gap lies between what the character knows and what we know, and it widens the longer the character stays in the dark. That makes it a favourite of tragedy, where the audience often knows how the story ends before the hero does. The next screen goes to one of the best-known cases, in a tomb.

A narrator who gives himself away

The last place a gap can open is in the narrator. In the last lesson you met narrators who tell a story in the first person, and so far we have taken their word for what happened. Often we should. Freytag’s five parts

In 1863 the German writer Gustav Freytag studied how the plays of ancient Greece and of Shakespeare were put together, and described the pattern he found in a five-act play. He drew it as a pyramid, which is the hill from the last screen with straight sides. The pyramid has five parts, and each has a name.

The first part sets out the world before anything happens, with its place, its time and its people. Freytag called it the introduction, and English critics usually call it the exposition. Next comes the rising action, a run of events in which the protagonist’s fortunes climb and the trouble tightens. At the top sits the climax. After the climax, the falling action carries events downhill until the play ends in the catastrophe, the final stroke that settles the protagonist’s fate.

“Catastrophe” sounds like a disaster, and in a tragedy it is, because the hero falls. In other kinds of story the same place in the pyramid is called the resolution or the denouement, and it may be a happy one. A comedy, for example, rises again at its end towards a wedding or a reunion. The five names hold for every plot, and they let you say where in a story any event belongs.

After a wrong answer: The climax is the high point at the middle of the pyramid. The part wanted is the last one, after the falling action.

After a wrong answer: The exposition opens the story. The part wanted closes a tragedy.

Tybalt's death is the climax of Romeo and Juliet. Romeo's banishment follows it, and Juliet takes the Friar's potion in Act 4. Where do the banishment and the potion sit on Freytag's pyramid?

After a right answer: Both come after the climax and carry the lovers down towards the catastrophe in the tomb, so they belong to the falling action.

After a wrong answer: Rising action comes before the climax. These events follow Tybalt's death, so they sit on the other side of the peak.

After a wrong answer: The exposition opens the story, before the conflict begins. These events come after the climax.

Recap

A story rises to a climax and falls to its end, and its scenes can be read for whether they turn the protagonist's fortunes.

Freytag's pyramid divides a drama into exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and catastrophe, and in Romeo and Juliet the climax falls in Act 3, when Tybalt's death turns the lovers' luck against them. The shape works as a map for fairy tales and films too, with a happier last part that is usually called the resolution.

Scoring each scene as + or − at its opening and its close lets you count the turning scenes, those in which the protagonist's fortunes reverse, and they tend to gather around the climax.
