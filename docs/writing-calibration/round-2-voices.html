Lesson voice, round 2

The same lesson again, rewritten after your notes on round 1: the rhythm of the book voice, no framing stories, no talking down, and examples taken from the subject itself. All four versions follow the principles below.

Principles behind this round

  1. Given before new. Each sentence starts from something the reader already has, often the end of the previous sentence, and ends on the new part (Pinker, The Sense of Style).
  2. Connect, don't chop. Ideas that depend on each other share a sentence and are joined by "because", "so", "which" and "since". A short sentence appears only when the point really is short, and never in runs.
  3. Examples come from the subject. No games, journeys or stories used as framing. The example is the subject's own material: an equation, a star, a query.
  4. One register for an adult reader. No baby-talk followed by a jump back to technical terms. Terms are named once, plainly, when the idea they name has been shown.
  5. Feedback states the fact and the reason in one or two connected sentences, with no verdict words.

The rhythm of round 1's book voice with the anecdote removed. It opens on the equation itself and keeps a slightly formal, written register.

Opener

Algebra rests on one convention: a letter may stand in a calculation for a number whose value has not yet been given. In the equation x + 5 = 12, the letter x is such a number, and the equation states a fact about it, namely that adding 5 to it produces 12.

Most of what follows in this course, from rearranging equations to drawing straight lines, depends on reading a line like this one correctly, as a statement about a particular number that you have not been told.

Teaching

The letter is chosen for convenience, and any letter would serve. It does the job that an empty box does in an arithmetic exercise such as □ + 5 = 12, with two advantages over the box: a letter can be read aloud, and when a problem involves more than one unknown number, each can have its own letter so that the two are never confused.

A letter used in this way is called a variable, because the same letter can stand for different numbers in different problems. A statement that joins two calculations with an equals sign is called an equation, and its content lies in that sign, which asserts that the calculation on the left and the calculation on the right produce the same number.

Testing a number

Because an equation makes an assertion, a proposed value can be checked against it directly. To test whether 7 satisfies x + 5 = 12, replace x with 7 and work out the left-hand side: 7 + 5 gives 12, which matches the right-hand side, so the assertion holds when x is 7, and 7 is called a solution of the equation. The same check rules out 6, since 6 + 5 gives 11. A value that makes the two sides nearly equal is not a solution, however small the difference, because the equation asserts that they are equal and not that they are close.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

With x = 3, the left-hand side is 5 × 3 − 2, which is 13, while the right-hand side is 12. The two sides differ by one, so the equation's assertion is false for this value and 3 is not a solution.

Recap

A letter in an equation stands for a number that has not been given, and the equation asserts that its two sides are equal. A number is a solution when substituting it for the letter, wherever the letter appears, makes that assertion true.

Opener

Here is an equation: 3x − 4 = 11. Before we learn any technique for solving it, it is worth being clear about what it says, because once that is clear, solving it turns out to be largely common sense.

The x is a number. We don't know which number yet, but it is an ordinary number all the same, and the equation tells us something definite about it: if you multiply it by 3 and take away 4, you get 11.

Teaching

Why use a letter at all? We could write the same thing in words, "a number, multiplied by 3, less 4, makes 11", and for centuries mathematicians did exactly that; even the equals sign only appeared in 1557, when Robert Recorde got tired of writing "is equal to". A letter is shorter, it can be carried from one line of working to the next, and when a problem has two unknown numbers we can call them x and y and keep them apart.

We call a letter that stands for a number a variable, and a line with an equals sign between two calculations an equation. The important thing about an equation is that it is a claim, and like any claim it is either true or false, depending on which number x actually is.

Testing a number

So we can test a candidate. Suppose we try x = 5 in 3x − 4 = 11. The left side becomes 3 × 5 − 4, which is 15 − 4, or 11, and that is exactly the right side, so the claim is true and 5 is a solution. Had we tried 4 instead, the left side would have come to 8, and since 8 is not 11 the claim would have been false. There is no partial credit here: a number either makes the two sides equal or it does not, and being close counts for nothing.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

Let's check. With x = 3 the left side is 5 × 3 − 2, which is 15 − 2, or 13, but the right side is 12. The sides are not equal, so the claim is false for x = 3 and 3 is not a solution.

Recap

An equation is a claim about an unknown number: that its two sides are equal. To test a candidate, we substitute it for the letter everywhere it appears and work out both sides, and the number is a solution only if they agree exactly.

Classic style as Pinker describes it: point the reader at something real and describe it. It has no box and no analogy, treats an expression as instructions waiting for a number, and chains each sentence from the one before.

Opener

The expression 3x − 4 describes a calculation that has not been carried out yet. It says: take a number, multiply it by 3, and subtract 4. The letter x marks where the number goes, and until someone supplies one the calculation waits.

Supply 5 and it produces 11; supply 10 and it produces 26. Reading algebra means reading every expression this way, as a set of instructions that will turn whatever number you give it into a result.

Teaching

When an expression is set equal to a number, as in 3x − 4 = 11, the instructions become a question about their input. The equation says that whatever number x is, running it through the instructions produces 11, and the task is to find which number does that.

A letter that holds the place of a number like this is called a variable, and a statement of the form "this calculation equals that one" is called an equation. The equals sign is what turns a calculation into a question, since it asserts that the left-hand side and the right-hand side have the same value.

Testing a number

You can answer the question for any particular number by running it through the instructions and comparing the result with the right-hand side. Running 5 through 3x − 4 gives 15 − 4, which is 11, the value the equation asks for, so 5 is a solution. Running 4 through it gives 8, which falls short of 11, so 4 is not. This check, called substitution, settles the matter for whichever number you try, although it cannot tell you which number to try; finding that directly is the subject of the next lesson.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

Running 3 through 5x − 2 gives 15 − 2, which is 13, one more than the 12 the equation asks for. Since the result has to equal 12 exactly, 3 is not a solution.

Recap

An expression such as 3x − 4 is a set of instructions waiting for a number, and an equation asks which number makes those instructions produce a given result. Substituting a number and comparing the two sides tells you whether it is a solution.

This version starts from a place where algebra is actually used, a fare formula, instead of a game or story. It is here so you can tell whether any real-world anchor helps or whether you want pure subject-first writing.

Opener

A taxi company charges £3 when you get in and £2 for every mile after that. To quote a fare the company needs a rule that works for any trip, and the rule is easy to say in words: double the number of miles and add 3.

Algebra writes that rule as 2m + 3, where the letter m stands for the number of miles. The letter lets one short expression cover every possible trip at once, which is why formulas in science, finance and computing are written this way.

Teaching

A letter that stands for a number like this is called a variable, because its value varies from one case to another: m is 4 for a four-mile trip and 10 for a ten-mile one. You can evaluate 2m + 3 for any trip by substituting its length and doing the arithmetic, so four miles costs 2 × 4 + 3, which is £11.

When the expression is set equal to a particular amount, as in 2m + 3 = 15, it becomes an equation, and the question changes direction. Instead of asking what a given trip costs, it asks which trip costs £15.

Testing a number

You can test a guess by substituting it. A six-mile trip gives 2 × 6 + 3, which is 15, so m = 6 makes the two sides of the equation equal, and 6 is called a solution. A five-mile trip gives 13, which does not match, so 5 is not a solution. A solution has to make the two sides exactly equal, because an equation that is nearly true is still false.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

With x = 3, the left-hand side is 5 × 3 − 2, which comes to 13 rather than 12. The two sides do not match, so 3 is not a solution.

Recap

A variable is a letter that stands for a number, which lets one expression describe every case at once. An equation sets an expression equal to a value, and a number is a solution when substituting it makes both sides equal.
