About this course

Algebra is arithmetic with a gap left in it, in which a letter is written where an unknown number belongs and is reasoned about like any other number. People study it because one short line with a letter in it can describe every case of something at once, such as the fare for a taxi trip of any length or the amount left in a savings pot after any number of months, and because it is the shorthand of science and computing, so that little in either can be read far without it.

The course has six lessons in two parts. The first three, called Rearranging, show how to read a letter as a number not yet given, how to find that number by doing the same thing to both sides of an equation, and how to undo a calculation one step at a time. The last three, called Straight lines, place numbers on a grid and measure how steep a line is, and the final lesson links a line on the page to the equation that draws it.

Each lesson explains before it asks, so that a question appears only once the lesson has shown what the question needs.

  • You'll be able to read an expression such as 3x − 4 as a calculation and work out its value for any number.
  • You'll be able to solve an equation such as 3x + 2 = 23 and say why each step keeps it true.
  • You'll be able to undo a calculation in the right order, including one with brackets.
  • You'll be able to find the gradient of a straight line from two points on it.
  • You'll be able to draw the line for y = mx + c and read its equation back off the line.

What a letter stands for

Opener

An equation such as x + 5 = 12 has a letter where a number should be, and it states something about that number: whatever it is, adding 5 to it gives 12. The number has not been given, but it is fixed all the same, and the letter is simply a way of referring to it before its value is known.

Most of algebra, from rearranging equations to drawing straight lines, depends on reading letters in this way, as a number with one definite value that is not yet known, and on being able to check whether a proposed value fits.

Once that reading is familiar, a line such as 3x − 4 stops looking like code and reads as a calculation waiting for a number, and testing a suggested value becomes a matter of substituting it and comparing the two sides.

By the end of the lesson you will read 3x − 4 as a calculation waiting for a number, and test whether a given number solves an equation.

Teaching

Working back from a result

Arithmetic often runs in a direction that hides the number being sought. A number has had something done to it, only the result is known, and the original has to be recovered by undoing what was done, which is possible because each operation has an opposite that restores the number it acted on.

If 8 has been added to a number and the result is 14, taking 8 away from 14 gives 6, and running the calculation forwards confirms it, since 6 plus 8 is 14.

In the same way, if a number has been doubled to give 18, halving 18 gives 9, because doubling 9 returns 18.

Each problem of this kind has a starting number that is not given, an operation applied to it, and a result that is, and the unknown is found by working from the result back through the operation. Algebra is a way of writing such problems down so that the writing holds whichever numbers are involved, and one more problem comes first, before any notation is introduced.

Giving the unknown number a name

Writing out “the number” each time is clumsy, and arithmetic has long marked the gap with a box where the answer belongs. The problem just solved, written in that way, is:

\square + 5 = 12

The box stands for “some number”, and the equation asks which number fits. A box cannot be spoken aloud, however, and a problem with two gaps gives no way of telling them apart, so a letter takes its place:

x + 5 = 12

The letter names the unknown number. It does not signify multiplication, and it need not be x, since any letter would serve equally well. Because the same letter can stand for a different number in a different problem, a letter used in this way is called a variable.

A line with an equals sign in the middle, like x + 5 = 12, is called an equation. The part before the sign is its left side and the part after it is its right side, and the equation claims that the two sides come to the same number. Read aloud as “some number, plus 5, makes 12”, it is the original problem again.

So far the letter has stood only for the answer, but it can also appear in a calculation whose result is not yet fixed, which is the subject of the next screen.

Keeping the balance

Opener

An equation says that its two sides are equal, and a pair of scales that sits exactly level says the same thing about the weights in its two pans, which makes the scales a useful way of seeing what can be done to an equation without changing its meaning.

In the last lesson an unknown number was found by trying values until one fitted, which is slow whenever the answer is not small. The scales suggest a method that needs no guessing, and it rests on a single question: what can be done to both pans while they stay level?

The answer, that the same change must be made to both sides, is the rule on which the solving of equations rests, and it is what allows an unknown number to be left alone on one side.

By the end of the lesson you will know why doing the same thing to both sides keeps an equation true, and will use that to solve for x.

What keeps the scales level

Take scales that are level, with a bag of marbles and 6 loose marbles in the left pan and 10 marbles in the right, every marble weighing the same. Lifting the 6 loose marbles out of the left pan makes that pan rise and the scales tip, but taking 6 marbles out of the right pan as well brings them level again.

To write the scales down, let x be the number of marbles in the bag. The left pan then holds x and 3 loose marbles and the right pan holds 11, so level scales give the equation:

The two pans are the two sides of the equation, and the equals sign says that they weigh the same. Taking 3 from both sides leaves x alone on the left and 11 − 3 on the right, which is the move made with the marbles, and it gives x = 8.

Steepness as a number

To compare the two paths, each climb is shared out evenly over its ground. On the steeper path, 30 metres of climb over 15 metres of ground gives 30 ÷ 15 = 2 metres of climb for each metre along, while on the gentler path the same 30 metres spread over 60 metres of ground gives 30 ÷ 60 = 0.5 metres for each metre along.

Drawing a line from its equation

An equation also tells you how to draw its line. Choose a value for x, replace x with it, and work out y, multiplying before you add. The pair you get is a point on the line. For y = 2x − 3, x = 0 gives y = 2 × 0 − 3 = −3, so one point is (0, −3). Choosing x = 3 gives y = 2 × 3 − 3 = 3, so another point is (3, 3).

Reading the equation off a line

The reverse direction runs from a line to its equation. For a line through (0, 5) and (2, 1), the point on the y-axis, (0, 5), gives the intercept at once, so c = 5, and the gradient comes from the rise and the run: from (0, 5) to (2, 1) the rise is 1 − 5 = −4 and the run is 2 − 0 = 2, so m = −4 ÷ 2 = −2.

[Figure]

The line is therefore y = −2x + 5, and putting the other point back in checks it, since x = 2 gives y = −2 × 2 + 5 = 1, which matches (2, 1). A line that falls always has a negative m, as the candle's line did.

Applying it
