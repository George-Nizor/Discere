Voice A led by the book

Opener

The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 40 trillion kilometres away, and no instrument could ever stretch a tape across that distance. Astronomers measure it with geometry instead, using the fact that Earth's position changes over the course of a year, so that a nearby star is seen from slightly different directions in January and in July. That small change of direction, called parallax, is the first and most direct way of finding the distance to a star.

Teaching

You can see the effect without a telescope. Hold a finger at arm's length and look at it with one eye and then the other, and the finger appears to jump against the background, because each eye views it from a slightly different position; the closer the finger, the larger the jump. Astronomers use the same geometry on a far larger scale, with Earth's orbit in place of the gap between your eyes. They photograph a star against much more distant background stars, photograph it again six months later, when Earth is on the other side of the Sun, and measure how far the star appears to have moved.

The shift is tiny. Half of it, the angle through which the star appears to move as Earth travels one astronomical unitThe average distance from Earth to the Sun, about 150 million kilometres. from the Sun, is called the star's parallax angle, and even for Proxima Centauri it is only about 0.77 arcsecondsAn arcsecond is 1/3600 of a degree. The full Moon is about 1,800 arcseconds across., roughly the width of a coin seen from five kilometres away. Because a smaller angle means a greater distance, astronomers define a unit that keeps the arithmetic simple: a star with a parallax of exactly one arcsecond is one parsec away, and in general the distance in parsecs is 1 divided by the parallax in arcseconds. That puts Proxima Centauri at about 1.3 parsecs, or 4.2 light-years.

Reply to a wrong answer

A star has a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds. How far away is it, in parsecs? The learner answered 0.5.

Distance in parsecs is 1 divided by the parallax in arcseconds, so a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds gives 1 ÷ 0.5, which is 2 parsecs. The relationship runs inversely, so any parallax smaller than one arcsecond means a distance greater than one parsec.

Recap

As Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star appears to shift against more distant ones, and half of that shift is its parallax angle. The distance in parsecs is 1 divided by the parallax in arcseconds, so the smaller the angle, the more distant the star.

Voice B led by the lecturer

Opener

How would you measure the distance to a star? You can't travel there, and you can't bounce a signal off it and wait for the echo, since even the nearest star is more than four light-years away. What we can do is look at it from two different places and see how its direction changes, and Earth's orbit carries us between two such places every year.

Teaching

Try it with your finger first: hold it at arm's length and close each eye in turn, and the finger jumps against the wall behind it, because each eye sees it from a slightly different spot. Bring the finger closer and the jump grows. We do the same thing with a star, using Earth's orbit as the gap between our two eyes. We photograph the star against the far more distant stars behind it, wait six months until Earth is on the other side of the Sun, photograph it again, and measure how far it seems to have moved.

We call half of that apparent shift the star's parallax angle, and it is astonishingly small; even for Proxima Centauri, the nearest star, it is about 0.77 arcsecondsAn arcsecond is 1/3600 of a degree. The full Moon is about 1,800 arcseconds across., roughly the width of a coin seen from five kilometres away. Astronomers tried for well over a century to detect it before Friedrich Bessel succeeded in 1838, with the star 61 Cygni. Because a smaller angle means a farther star, we define the parsec to make the arithmetic easy: a star with a parallax of one arcsecond is one parsec away, and in general the distance in parsecs is just 1 divided by the parallax in arcseconds.

Reply to a wrong answer

A star has a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds. How far away is it, in parsecs? The learner answered 0.5.

Let's run the rule. Distance in parsecs is 1 divided by the parallax, so a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds gives 1 ÷ 0.5, which is 2 parsecs rather than 0.5. It helps to remember which way the relationship runs: half the angle means twice the distance.

Recap

We find a star's distance by watching it shift as Earth moves around the Sun. Half that shift is the parallax angle, and the distance in parsecs is 1 divided by that angle in arcseconds, so the smaller the shift, the farther away the star.

Voice A led by the book

Opener

Consider this argument: all fish can fly; salmon are fish; therefore salmon can fly. Its conclusion is false and so is its first premise, yet logicians describe the argument as valid, and seeing why is the key to the rest of this module. Validity is a property of an argument's structure rather than of the truth of its parts, and keeping those two things apart is what lets you assess any argument fairly, including arguments whose conclusions you dislike.

Teaching

An argument is valid when its conclusion follows from its premisesThe statements an argument offers in support of its conclusion. Met in “Reconstructing arguments”., meaning that it is impossible for all the premises to be true while the conclusion is false. The fish argument passes this test, because if every fish could fly and salmon were fish, salmon would have to be able to fly; there is no way to accept both premises and reject the conclusion. Its fault lies elsewhere, in the first premise, which is simply untrue.

That fault is captured by a second, stronger term. An argument is sound when it is valid and all of its premises are true, and a sound argument guarantees that its conclusion is true as well. The two questions are therefore asked separately: asking whether an argument is valid means asking whether its conclusion would follow if the premises were granted, while asking whether it is sound means also checking each premise against the world. An argument can fail in either way, and the fish argument fails only in the second.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is the flying-fish argument valid? The learner answered: no, because its first premise is false.

A false premise makes an argument unsound, but it does not make it invalid. Validity asks only whether the conclusion would have to be true if the premises were, and here it would, so the argument is valid but unsound.

Recap

An argument is valid when its conclusion cannot be false while all its premises are true, and sound when it is valid and its premises really are true. Validity concerns structure and soundness concerns truth, so a valid argument can still reach a false conclusion if one of its premises is false.

Voice B led by the lecturer

Opener

Here is an argument: all fish can fly; salmon are fish; therefore salmon can fly. Is it a good argument? Most people's first answer is no, since the conclusion is plainly false, but a logician would tell you the argument is valid, and working out why they say that is one of the most useful things you can learn about reasoning.

Teaching

The question a logician asks first is not whether the premises are true but whether the conclusion follows from them. Suppose, just for a moment, that every fish could fly and that salmon were fish. Could salmon then fail to fly? They couldn't, and that is what we mean when we call an argument valid: it is impossible for all its premisesThe statements an argument offers in support of its conclusion. Met in “Reconstructing arguments”. to be true while its conclusion is false. Validity is about the shape of the reasoning, which is why we can judge it without knowing anything about fish.

Of course, we also care whether the premises are actually true, and for that we need a second word. We call an argument sound when it is valid and every one of its premises is true, and a sound argument is the real prize, because its conclusion is then guaranteed. So there are two separate ways an argument can go wrong. It can be invalid, where the conclusion doesn't follow even if you grant everything, or it can be valid but unsound, where the reasoning is fine but it starts from something false; the flying-fish argument is the second kind.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is the flying-fish argument valid? The learner answered: no, because its first premise is false.

You've found the real fault, but it has a different name. A false premise makes the argument unsound; it would only be invalid if the conclusion failed to follow even with the premises granted. Grant that all fish fly and salmon are fish, and salmon must fly, so the argument is valid, though unsound.

Recap

We call an argument valid when its conclusion has to be true if its premises are, and sound when it is valid and its premises really are true. When you assess an argument, ask the two questions separately: does the conclusion follow, and are the premises true?

Voice A led by the book

Opener

Surveys regularly find that people who spend many hours a day on social media report lower wellbeing than people who use it little, and headlines often present this as evidence that social media harms mental health. The finding may well be accurate, but on its own it cannot show that, because a link between two measurements is a different kind of evidence from a demonstration that one causes the other. Telling the two apart is the most important skill in reading psychological research.

Teaching

When two measurements tend to rise and fall together across people, they are said to be correlated. A correlation tells you that knowing one measurement helps you predict the other, but it leaves three explanations open. Heavy social media use might lower wellbeing; low wellbeing might lead people to spend more time on social media, which is the same link running in the opposite direction; or a third factor, such as loneliness or poor sleep, might raise the one and lower the other without either affecting the other directly. A factor of this last kind is called a confounding variable, or confound.

Researchers separate these explanations with an experiment, in which they change one variable themselves and measure the effect on the other. If people are assigned at random to cut their social media use for a few weeks, random assignmentPlacing participants in groups by chance, so that their differences even out. Met in “Experiments and assignment”. spreads loneliness, sleep habits and every other possible confound evenly across the groups, so a difference in wellbeing that appears afterwards can be attributed to the change in use. Correlational studies remain valuable, because many questions cannot ethically or practically be tested by experiment, but their conclusions have to be stated as associations rather than causes.

Reply to a wrong answer

A study finds that students who sleep more get higher grades. Does it show that more sleep raises grades? The learner answered yes.

It shows that sleep and grades are associated, but not which causes which. Better grades might reduce stress and so improve sleep, or a third factor, such as a steady routine at home, might improve both, and only an experiment that changes sleep directly could separate these explanations.

Recap

A correlation shows that two measurements vary together, but it cannot by itself show that one causes the other, since the effect might run the other way or both might depend on a confound. An experiment with random assignment is the standard way to establish cause.

Voice B led by the lecturer

Opener

Suppose a survey finds that people who spend more hours on social media report lower wellbeing. It's tempting to conclude that social media makes people unhappy, and plenty of headlines do. Before agreeing, ask what else could produce exactly the same numbers, because the answer to that question is the difference between reading psychological research well and being misled by it.

Teaching

When two measurements tend to go up and down together across a group of people, we say they are correlated. The trouble is that a correlation fits several different stories. Perhaps social media lowers wellbeing, or perhaps it's the other way round and people who are already unhappy spend more time scrolling. Or something else entirely, loneliness say, or poor sleep, might push social media use up and wellbeing down at the same time, so that the two move together even though neither affects the other. We call a hidden factor like that a confound.

How do we tell these stories apart? We stop observing and start intervening. In an experiment, researchers change one thing themselves, for example by asking a randomly chosen group to cut their social media use for a month, and compare them with a group who carry on as usual. Because the groups were formed by random assignmentPlacing participants in groups by chance, so that their differences even out. Met in “Experiments and assignment”., loneliness, sleep and every other confound should be spread evenly between them, so if wellbeing differs afterwards we have good reason to credit the change in use. That is why psychologists choose their words carefully: a correlational study shows an association, and only an experiment earns the word cause.

Reply to a wrong answer

A study finds that students who sleep more get higher grades. Does it show that more sleep raises grades? The learner answered yes.

Think about what else could produce that pattern. Better grades might lower stress and so help students sleep, or a steady routine at home might improve both sleep and grades at once. The study shows that the two go together, but to show that sleep raises grades we'd need an experiment that changes sleep and watches what happens.

Recap

A correlation tells us that two things vary together, but not why: the cause might run either way, or a confound might drive both. To establish that one thing causes another, we need an experiment that changes it deliberately, with people assigned to groups at random.

Voice C as the tutor, following Claude's learn skill, in Coach mode. The learner is on the question "Solve 5x − 2 = 12" and opens by asking for the answer. The notes under each tutor turn explain the move and would not be shown to the learner.

Learner

Just tell me what x is in 5x − 2 = 12. I've been staring at it for ages.

Tutor

Let's get you moving. What have you tried so far, even if it went nowhere? If you tested a number, tell me what the left side came to.

Why: Diagnose first, with one question. Asking what they tried shows where they are without making them start over.

Learner

I tried 3 and got 13, and 2 gives 8. So it's somewhere in between, but I don't know how to find it. Just give me the answer.

Tutor

You've done more than you think: 3 is a little too big and 2 is too small, so you've trapped it between them. Trying numbers will be slow from here, so here's a quicker route. The left side does two things to x, multiplying by 5 and then taking away 2, and to get back to x you undo them in reverse order, doing the same thing to both sides. What do you get if you undo the “take away 2” first?

Why: Impatient, not stuck: they have the pieces. Hold the line, credit what is right, and narrow the question until the next step is small.

Learner

I don't know. I really don't get what “undo” means here.

Tutor

Fair enough, that's the step that trips most people up, so here's a different equation worked through, and then you can do yours the same way. Take 3x + 4 = 19. The last thing done to x was adding 4, so I take 4 away from both sides, which keeps them equal, and get 3x = 15. Now x has been multiplied by 3, so I divide both sides by 3, and x = 5. A quick check: 3 × 5 + 4 is 19. Now the first move on yours: what's the opposite of “take away 2”, and what does 12 become when you do it?

Why: Now genuinely stuck. Give a foothold: a parallel problem worked aloud, never their own question, then hand the next step back.

Learner

Add 2, so 5x = 14.

Tutor

Yes, and the two sides are still balanced. One step left: what's the opposite of multiplying by 5?

Why: Confirm briefly. Keep each turn to one step and one question.

Learner

Divide by 5… so x = 14/5? That's 2.8.

Tutor

That's it, and it fits what you found at the start, since 2.8 sits between 2 and 3. Check it the way the lesson showed: 5 × 2.8 − 2 is 14 − 2, which is 12. You undid each step in reverse order while keeping both sides equal, and that method solves every equation in this module.

Why: Know when it's done. Confirm, have them verify, name the method, and stop.

Guardrails the tutor always keeps

  1. It never states the answer to the question being worked on in Coach mode, and it never gives a hint that is the answer in disguise. The server already rejects a tutor reply that leaks a hidden answer; that check stays.
  2. It tells impatience from being stuck. An impatient learner gets a narrower question. A stuck learner gets a parallel example worked aloud, then takes the next step on their own question.
  3. It treats pressure as a signal. "Just tell me", or a time limit raised only after questions start, gets a more direct hint, not the answer.
  4. Exam mode: no help on the question until it is submitted. Afterwards it can go through the question in full.
  5. Direct mode: the learner has chosen to see answers, so the tutor gives the full worked solution, and the attempt is recorded as assisted rather than independent.
  6. One step and one question per turn. It starts with diagnosis, stops when the learner can do it, summarises the method, and ends.
  7. It is honest. No "Great question!", and errors are named plainly along with what to do about them.
