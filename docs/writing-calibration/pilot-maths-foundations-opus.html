About this course

The expression 3 + 2m gives the fare, in pounds, for a taxi trip of m miles, whatever the value of m, and that is the point of algebra: a single line with a letter in it describes every case of a relationship at once. The letter stands for a number that has not been given, and you reason about that number through what the line says about it. Algebra is also the notation of science and computing, and it is difficult to read far in either without it.

The course has six lessons in two parts. The first three, Rearranging, teach you to read a letter as a number you have not yet been told, and then to find that number by doing the same thing to both sides of an equation and undoing its operations one at a time, in the right order. The last three, Straight lines, place pairs of numbers on a grid, measure how steep a line is, and finish by connecting a straight line to the equation that draws it.

Each lesson explains an idea before asking about it, so every question you meet draws only on what has already been shown.

  • You'll be able to read an expression such as 3x − 4 as a calculation and work out its value for any number.
  • You'll be able to solve an equation such as 3x + 2 = 23 and say why each step keeps it true.
  • You'll be able to undo a calculation in the right order, including one with brackets.
  • You'll be able to find the gradient of a straight line from two points on it.
  • You'll be able to draw the line for y = mx + c and read its equation back off the line.

What a letter stands for

Opener

Consider the line 2x + 3 = 17. It is short, but it makes a precise claim: there is a number which, doubled and then increased by 3, comes to 17. The letter x stands for that number, whose value you have not been told, and the rest of the line records what was done to it and what came out.

Read in this way, algebra is far less forbidding than its letters suggest, because each line is a sentence about a number written in a compact notation that takes only a few minutes to learn. The letter does not hide anything; it gives the missing number a name, so that the number can be carried from one line of working to the next before anyone knows its value.

The rest of this course depends on reading lines like this one. Solving an equation, which the next two lessons do step by step, means finding the one number that makes its claim true, and drawing a straight line from its equation means finding every pair of numbers that does. Both begin with the skill you will learn here, which is putting a number in place of the letter and seeing whether the claim holds.

By the end you will read 3x − 4 as a calculation waiting for a number, and you will be able to test whether a given number solves an equation.

Working back to the number

Suppose you are told that adding 8 to a certain number gives 14, and nothing else about it. Those two facts are enough to find the number, because every operation has an opposite that undoes it, and applying the opposite to the result runs the calculation backwards to the number it started from.

Subtraction undoes addition, so to reverse the adding of 8 you take 8 away from 14, which leaves 6. You can then check the answer by running the calculation forwards, since 6 + 8 is 14, the result you were given. Teaching

Giving the unknown number a name

The statement you have just worked with can be written in words as “my number + 5 = 12”, but writing the words out on every line soon becomes clumsy. School arithmetic books often replace them with an empty box, which marks the place where the unknown number belongs:

\square + 5 = 12

The box says that some number goes in that place, and the statement asks which number fits. A box has two drawbacks, however: you cannot read it aloud, and a statement about two unknown numbers would need two boxes that look exactly alike. Algebra therefore writes a letter where the box was:

x + 5 = 12

The letter is a name for the unknown number. It does not mean multiply, and it need not be x, since any letter would serve; the habit of choosing x goes back to René Descartes, who in 1637 used letters from the end of the alphabet for unknown quantities and letters from the beginning for known ones. A letter used in this way is called a variable, because in a different statement the same letter can stand for a different number.

A statement with an equals sign in the middle, such as x + 5 = 12, is called an equation. The part before the equals sign is its left side and the part after it is its right side, and the equation claims that the two sides come to the same number. Read aloud, it says “some number, with 5 added, makes 12”, which is the statement you began with.

So far the letter has stood only for a number you are trying to find, but it can also describe a calculation before any number has been chosen, and that is the subject of the next screen.

Teaching

Which operation comes first

The expression 3x − 4 asks for two things to be done to x: multiply it by 3, and take away 4. The order in which you do them changes the result, as x = 5 shows. Multiplying first gives 15, and taking away 4 then leaves 11, whereas taking away 4 first gives 1, and multiplying that by 3 gives 3.

Since a line of algebra has to mean the same thing to everyone who reads it, mathematicians follow an agreed rule: multiplication is done before addition or subtraction, unless brackets say otherwise. Under that rule 3x − 4 means multiply first and then subtract, and the same rule makes 2 + 3 × 4 equal to 14, because 3 × 4 is worked out before the 2 is added.

Keeping the balance

Opener

At the end of the last lesson, the equation 5x − 2 = 12 resisted every whole number you could try, because its solution is 2.8. Testing values can confirm a solution once you have one, but it cannot be relied on to find one, and an equation like this needs a method that leads to the answer directly.

The method rests on a single observation about equal quantities, which is easiest to see on a pair of scales. If two pans balance and you take the same weight from each, they still balance, whereas taking weight from one pan alone tips them. An equation behaves in the same way, since its two sides are equal amounts, and it stays true under exactly the changes that keep a pair of scales level.

That observation is all the justification the method needs. Each step in solving an equation does the same thing to both sides, chosen so that it undoes one of the operations applied to x, and when the last of them has been undone, x stands alone with its value on the other side.

By the end you will know why doing the same thing to both sides keeps an equation true, and you will use that fact to solve for x.

How steep is it

Opener

A path that climbs 30 metres over 15 metres of horizontal distance is far steeper than one that climbs the same 30 metres over 60 metres, although both end at the same height. Neither the climb nor the distance gives the steepness on its own, because steepness depends on the two together, on how much height is gained for each metre travelled across.

The same is true of any straight line on a grid, which climbs or falls by some amount for each unit it moves across. A single number that captures this lets you compare lines exactly, where words such as “steep” and “gentle” describe them only roughly.

That number is defined by one division, and once you can calculate it from the coordinates of two points, its sign also tells you whether a line rises, falls or stays level.

By the end you will find a line's gradient from two of its points, and say what the sign of the gradient tells you.

Teaching

Steepness as a number

To compare the two paths, divide each climb by the horizontal distance over which it happens. The shorter path climbs 30 metres over 15 metres, so it gains 30 ÷ 15 = 2 metres of height for each metre across, while the longer path spreads the same 30 metres over 60 metres and gains 30 ÷ 60 = 0.5 metres for each metre across.

Since 2 is four times 0.5, the shorter path is four times as steep. The number obtained by dividing the climb by the horizontal distance is called the gradient, so a gradient of 2 means that the path climbs 2 metres for every 1 metre it covers horizontally.

The same division measures the steepness of a hill, a ramp, a roof or a line on a grid, provided you can find the climb and the horizontal distance.

Writing the line as an equation

With these two numbers, the candle's line is y = −2x + 12, where −2 is the gradient and 12 is the intercept. Any straight line that is not vertical can be written in the same way with its own numbers, so the general pattern is written with letters as y = mx + c, in which m stands for the gradient and c for the intercept.

To see why c is the intercept, put x = 0 into the equation. The term mx becomes m × 0, which is 0, so y is simply c, and the point (0, c), which lies on the y-axis, is on the line. The line y = 3x + 4, for example, crosses the y-axis at height 4 and climbs 3 for every unit across.

Teaching

Drawing a line from its equation

Take the equation y = 2x − 3. Choosing x = 0 and substituting gives y = 2 × 0 − 3 = −3, so the point (0, −3) lies on the line, and choosing x = 3 gives y = 2 × 3 − 3 = 3, so (3, 3) lies on it too. Any value of x produces a point in the same way, by substituting it and working out y with the multiplication done first.

[Figure]

Two points are enough, because only one straight line passes through both, so you can join them with a ruler and extend the line in either direction. The gradient between the two points provides a check: (3 − (−3)) ÷ 3 = 2, which is the 2 in the equation.

Substitution also works in the other direction. If y = 2x and you know that y is 14, then 2x = 14, and dividing both sides by 2 gives x = 7.
