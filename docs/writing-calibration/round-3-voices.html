Opener

Here is an equation: 3x − 4 = 11. The letter x in it stands for a number whose value has not been given, and the equation states a fact about that number: multiply it by 3, take away 4, and the result is 11.

Before learning any method for finding it, it is worth being clear about what a line like this says, because most of what follows in this course, from rearranging equations to drawing straight lines, depends on reading it as a statement about one particular number that you have not yet been told.

Teaching

The same statement can be written in words, and for centuries mathematicians did write it that way, spelling out "a number, multiplied by 3, less 4, makes 11" in full. Even the equals sign only appeared in 1557, when Robert Recorde decided he would rather draw two parallel lines than keep writing "is equal to". A letter is shorter than the words, it can be carried from one line of working to the next, and when a problem involves two unknown numbers each can have its own letter, x and y, so that the two are never confused.

A letter used in this way is called a variable, because the same letter can stand for different numbers in different problems. A statement that joins two calculations with an equals sign is called an equation, and its content lies in that sign, which claims that the calculation on the left and the calculation on the right produce the same number. Like any claim, it is true for some values of x and false for others.

That gives us a way to check any proposed value. To test whether 5 satisfies 3x − 4 = 11, replace x with 5 and work out the left-hand side: 3 × 5 − 4 is 15 − 4, which is 11, the same as the right-hand side, so the claim holds and 5 is called a solution of the equation. Trying 4 instead gives a left-hand side of 8, which is not 11, so 4 is not a solution.

There is no partial credit in this test. The equation claims that its two sides are equal, not that they are close, so a value that misses by one has failed just as surely as a value that misses by a hundred.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

Let's start with an equation, 3x − 4 = 11, and ask what it actually says before we try to do anything with it. The x is a number. We haven't been told which one, but the equation tells us something definite about it: if we multiply it by 3 and take away 4, we get 11.

Teaching

Why write it with a letter? We could write the same thing in words, and mathematicians did for centuries; the equals sign itself only appeared in 1557, invented by Robert Recorde to spare himself from writing "is equal to" over and over. The letter does for the unknown number what Recorde's sign did for those words. It is short, it can be carried from one line of working to the next, and if a problem has two unknown numbers we can call them x and y and never mix them up.

We call a letter that stands for a number in this way a variable, since in another problem the same letter can stand for a different number, and we call a line with an equals sign between two calculations an equation. The equals sign is what gives an equation its content, because it makes a claim, namely that the left side and the right side come to the same number, and that claim is true or false depending on which number x is.

So we can test any value we like by substituting it and seeing whether the claim survives. Opener

People who find algebra hard often treat x as a symbol to be shuffled around by memorised rules. It is simpler than that: in a line such as 3x − 4 = 11, x is one specific number that the line hasn't told you yet, and every rule you meet later in this course follows from treating it that way.

This lesson stays with that idea long enough to make it solid: why unknown numbers are written as letters at all, what the equals sign is claiming, and how to check whether a number you've been handed is the one the equation describes.

Teaching

Read 3x − 4 = 11 aloud as a sentence and it says: some number, multiplied by 3, with 4 taken away, comes to 11. That is the whole of its meaning. Mathematicians once wrote equations out in exactly those words, and the notation we use now grew up to save the writing. Robert Recorde introduced the equals sign in 1557 so that he would not have to keep writing "is equal to", and letters for unknown numbers became standard over the following century. A letter is short, it can be copied from one line of working to the next, and two unknown numbers in the same problem can be given two different letters.

Two names are worth having from the start. A letter that stands for a number is called a variable, and a line that joins two calculations with an equals sign is called an equation.

The part that trips most people up is the equals sign. In arithmetic it tends to mean "and the answer is", as in 4 + 3 = 7, but in an equation it is a claim that the two sides are the same number. That claim might be true or false, and which it is depends entirely on what x is.

Testing a number

Because the equation makes a claim, you can test it for any number you like. Here is the test for x = 5, with the reasoning spoken aloud.

Start by putting 5 wherever x appears, so the left side becomes 3 × 5 − 4. Multiplication comes before subtraction, so work out 3 × 5 first to get 15, and then take away 4 to get 11. The right side is 11 already, so the two sides agree and the claim is true, which makes 5 a solution of the equation.

Now try x = 4 the same way. The left side is 3 × 4 − 4, which is 12 − 4, or 8, and 8 is not 11, so 4 is not a solution. The test gives no credit for coming close: the equation claims the two sides are equal, and a value that leaves them three apart has failed just as completely as one that leaves them three hundred apart.

Before the next screen, predict what x = 6 gives. Will the left side come out above 11 or below it, and by how much?

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

The left side doesn't come to 12. With x = 3 it is 5 × 3 − 2, which is 15 − 2, or 13, one more than the right side, so the claim fails and 3 is not a solution. It is an easy one to wave through because 13 is so close to 12, but the test is exact, so work out each side and compare the two numbers directly.

Recap

In algebra a letter is a specific number you haven't been told, and an equation claims that its two sides are equal. You test a value by putting it in place of the letter everywhere, working out each side in the usual order and comparing them; the value is a solution only when the two sides match exactly, and this is how you will check every answer in the lessons that follow.

Opener

If algebra put you off at school, the trouble probably started when the letters arrived, and the reason is worth naming because it is easy to fix. Up to that point every line of maths you had seen was a calculation with its answer at the end. Then lines such as 3x − 4 = 11 appeared, with a letter where a number ought to be, and nobody quite said what kind of thing that letter was.

This lesson says it plainly and spends its time on that one point, because the rest of the course is built on it.

Teaching

The letter is a number. It is one particular number that the line hasn't named, in the same way that "the smallest prime number above 20" picks out one particular number, 23, without writing it down. The equation 3x − 4 = 11 is a description of x: it is the number that, multiplied by 3 and with 4 taken away, gives 11.

Two pieces of notation make that description compact, and both are easy to misread. The first is 3x, which means 3 multiplied by x; the multiplication sign is left out because × looks too much like the letter x, so if x were 5, 3x would be 15 and not 35. The second is the equals sign. You have mostly met it at the end of a sum, meaning "and the answer is", but in an equation it is a claim that the calculation on the left and the number on the right are the same.

A letter used to stand for a number is called a variable, and a line that makes this kind of claim is called an equation.

Testing a number

Since an equation is a claim, checking a proposed value means seeing whether the claim is true for it. For x = 5 the left side is 3 × 5 − 4, which is 15 − 4, or 11, and that matches the right side, so 5 fits the description and is called a solution. For x = 4 the left side is 12 − 4, which is 8, so 4 does not fit.

When you do this yourself, the step most likely to go wrong is the order of the arithmetic: multiply before you subtract, so 3 × 5 − 4 is 15 − 4 and not 3 × 1. Keep the test strict as well, because a value that brings the two sides close has still failed to make them equal.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

Substituting 3 gives a left side of 5 × 3 − 2, which is 13, while the right side is 12, so the claim fails by one and 3 is not a solution. A near miss like this usually means the true value is close by, which is worth noticing once you start solving equations in the next lesson.

Recap

A letter in an equation is a particular number you haven't been told, and the equation describes it by claiming that two calculations are equal. To check a proposed value, substitute it everywhere the letter appears, work out each side in the right order and compare; only an exact match makes it a solution.
