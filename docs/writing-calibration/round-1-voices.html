Opener

You've probably played this game on a long car journey. Someone says they're thinking of a number, that they doubled it and added three and got seventeen, and then they wait for you to tell them what they started with. Most people get there without much trouble: you take the three back off to get fourteen, halve that, and land on seven.

What you've just done, without a single letter in sight, is algebra. The letters that make it look so forbidding on the page are only a way of writing that hidden number down, so that you can reason about it on paper in the same way you just did in your head.

Teaching

At school you might have seen sums with a little empty box in them, where the job was to work out what goes in the box:

□ + 5 = 12

A letter does exactly the same job as that box. Writing x + 5 = 12 says the same thing, "some number, plus five, makes twelve", and the reason mathematicians prefer a letter is a practical one: you can say it out loud, and if a puzzle has two different hidden numbers you can call one x and the other y instead of drawing two boxes and hoping nobody mixes them up.

A letter used like this is called a variable, and the whole line, with its equals sign in the middle, is an equation. The equals sign is making a promise that whatever is on the left comes to the same amount as whatever is on the right.

Testing a number

That promise gives you a way to check any guess. Say someone suggests that x is 7. You put 7 in place of the x, so the left side becomes 7 + 5, which is 12, and since the right side was 12 all along the promise holds, which makes 7 what we call a solution. Try 6 instead and the left side only comes to 11, so 6 isn't a solution, even though it's close. Close doesn't count here; the two sides either agree exactly or they don't.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

Put 3 in for x and the left side comes to 5 × 3 − 2, which is 13, one more than the 12 on the right. It's close, but a solution has to make both sides match exactly, so 3 doesn't work here.

Recap

A letter in algebra is just a number you haven't been told yet. Whenever you want to know whether a particular number fits, you put it in place of the letter everywhere it appears, work out each side, and see whether they agree. If they do, you've found a solution.

Opener

There is an old parlour game in which one person thinks of a number, does something to it, and announces only the result: "I doubled my number and added three, and I got seventeen." The other player works backwards, taking away the three and halving what is left, and announces seven.

Nobody playing this game thinks of it as mathematics, yet it contains the whole idea behind algebra, which is reasoning carefully about a number you cannot see. Algebra's letters, which put so many people off, are simply a way of writing that unseen number down so that the reasoning can be done on paper and checked.

Teaching

Arithmetic books for children often print puzzles in which a box marks a gap to be filled:

□ + 5 = 12

Algebra replaces the box with a letter and writes x + 5 = 12. The change is a practical one. A letter can be read aloud, and when a problem involves two unknown numbers they can be given two different letters, which two identical boxes could never distinguish.

A letter that stands for a number in this way is called a variable, and a statement with an equals sign between two calculations is called an equation. The equals sign is the important part, because it asserts that the left-hand side and the right-hand side have the same value.

Testing a number

That assertion can be tested. To find out whether 7 is the hidden number in x + 5 = 12, replace x with 7 and work out the left-hand side, which gives 12. Since the right-hand side is also 12, the two sides agree, and 7 is said to be a solution of the equation. Trying 6 gives a left-hand side of 11, which does not agree with 12, so 6 is not a solution. The test is strict, and a number that brings the two sides close together without making them level is not a solution at all.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

Substituting 3 gives a left-hand side of 5 × 3 − 2, which is 13. The right-hand side is 12, so the two sides differ by one, and 3 is therefore not a solution.

Recap

A letter in an equation stands for a number that has not yet been given. To test whether a particular number is a solution, substitute it for the letter wherever the letter appears and work out both sides; the number is a solution only if the two sides come out equal.

Opener

Algebra has a reputation it doesn't deserve. For a lot of people the trouble starts the moment the letters turn up, because a line like 3x − 4 = 11 looks less like a sum and more like a code that nobody gave you the key to.

Here's the key, then. Every letter in that line is a number. It's a perfectly ordinary number, the kind you've been adding and multiplying all your life, and the only odd thing about it is that nobody has told you yet which one it is. Hold on to that and almost everything else in this course follows from it.

Teaching

You've met the idea before, even if nobody called it algebra. Primary-school worksheets are full of sums like this one, and nobody panics about the box:

□ + 5 = 12

You just ask what goes in it and answer 7. Swap the box for a letter, x + 5 = 12, and you've got exactly the same puzzle in grown-up clothes. The letter is there for convenience, since you can't easily say "box" out loud and you certainly can't tell two boxes apart, whereas x and y are easy to keep straight.

Mathematicians call a letter like this a variable, and a line with an equals sign in the middle an equation. The equals sign is the part to take seriously, because it's making a claim: whatever is on the left comes to exactly the same as whatever is on the right.

Testing a number

Claims can be checked, and checking this one is about as easy as maths gets. Suppose you suspect x is 7. Rub out the x, write 7 in its place and work out the left side: 7 + 5 is 12. The right side was 12 all along, so the claim holds and 7 earns the name solution. Suspect 6 instead and the left side comes to 11, which falls one short, so 6 is out. One short might feel like nearly right, but an equation isn't marked generously, and the sides have to match exactly.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

Put 3 in for x and the left side comes to 5 × 3 − 2, which is 13. The right side is 12, so the equation's claim fails by one, and 3 isn't a solution.

Recap

Every letter is a number you haven't been told yet. To check whether a number fits an equation, write it in wherever the letter appears, work out the left side and see whether it matches the right. If it does, that number is a solution.

Opener

Before any letters, try this. I'm thinking of a number. I double it, add three, and get seventeen. What did I start with? Work it out before you read on.

If you said seven, you probably took the three away first to get fourteen and then halved it. Look at what you did there: you reasoned about a number you couldn't see by undoing, one step at a time, what had been done to it. That's the whole of this lesson in miniature, and the rest of it is learning to write that reasoning down.

Teaching

So how would you write that puzzle down? Children's worksheets use a box, and you can probably see straight away that 7 goes in this one:

□ + 5 = 12

Now picture a puzzle with two different hidden numbers. Two boxes would look identical, so you'd have no way to say which was which, and that's the reason algebra uses letters instead. x + 5 = 12 means exactly what the box version means, and if a second hidden number turns up it can be called y.

A letter used this way is called a variable, and a line like this, with an equals sign in the middle, is an equation. Its equals sign is claiming that the left side and the right side come to the same amount.

Testing a number

How could you check whether 7 really is the hidden number? You can test the claim directly. Replace x with 7 and the left side becomes 7 + 5, which is 12, the same as the right side, so the claim holds and 7 is called a solution. What happens with 6? The left side is 11 and the right is 12. They're close, but the equation says they're equal and they aren't, so 6 is not a solution.

Reply to a wrong answer

Is x = 3 a solution of 5x − 2 = 12? The learner answered yes.

Work out the left side with 3 in place of x: 5 × 3 − 2 is 13. The right side is 12, so the sides don't agree and 3 isn't a solution. Which number would bring the left side down to exactly 12?

Recap

You found a hidden number by undoing what had been done to it, and then gave it a name, x. Testing a number means putting it in place of x everywhere, working out the left side and checking it against the right. When they agree, the number is a solution, and when they don't, however close they are, it isn't.

Opener

Most people have played this game on a long journey. A friend says "I'm thinking of a number" and tells you only what they did to it, perhaps that they doubled it and then added something. You say what number they started with, and you are usually right, because you work backwards from what you've been told.

When you do that you are reasoning about a number you can't see, which is what algebra does in its smallest form. The letters that make algebra look so unfriendly are a way of writing the hidden number down. Once you can read them, a line of algebra reads like one of these puzzles.

Teaching

Writing out "my number" every time gets clumsy, so people shortened it. You may remember sums from school with a gap in them, a box where the answer goes. The round you just played looks like this:

□ + 5 = 12

The box means "some number", and the sum asks which number fits. A box is awkward, though. You can't say it aloud, and a puzzle with two boxes gives you no way to tell them apart. So the gap gets a letter instead.

The letter is a name for the hidden number. It doesn't mean multiply, and it doesn't have to be x; any letter would do as well. A letter used in this way is called a variable, because in another puzzle the same letter can stand for a different number.

Testing a number

A number that makes the two sides of an equation agree is called a solution, and finding it is called solving the equation. Testing a number is a short routine. Substitute it for the letter, then work out the left side. Last, compare the result with the right side.

How to reply

  • Pick the voice closest to what you want. Mixing is fine, for example "1's warmth with 2's precision".
  • Quote any sentence you love or hate. One quoted line teaches the writers more than a general description.
  • Say whether the length suits a lesson screen. These versions run about 10 to 30 percent longer than the app's text.
